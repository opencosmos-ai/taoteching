## The Overlay Audit — reading Laozi without the missionary lens

*A working checklist. Consult it whenever a rendering feels unaccountably churchy — and consult it deliberately at every chapter containing a flagged term.*

### What this is, and what it is not

This is not an argument against Christianity, and not a claim that the missionary translators were dishonest. Legge and his contemporaries were serious scholars who gave the English-speaking world its first real access to these texts, and we are in their debt. The problem is subtler and entirely ordinary: **they translated with the only religious vocabulary they had**, and that vocabulary carried a cosmology inside it. A century later their word-choices have hardened into "how the Tao Te Ching sounds in English" — so thoroughly that most readers experience the overlay as the text.

Our task is narrow and technical: **do not let one tradition's vocabulary silently rewrite another's.** Where a standard English rendering imports a creator, a soul, a sin, or a heaven that Laozi's Chinese does not contain, we strip it and go back to the character.

### How the overlay got in

The English Tao Te Ching was largely built by missionaries. **James Legge** (1815–1897), whose 1891 translation still underlies countless later versions and remains a public-domain staple, came to China with the London Missionary Society. He spent decades at the center of the **"Term Question"** — the long, bitter argument over which Chinese word should be conscripted to mean the Christian *God* (神? 上帝? 天主?). The dispute was never settled; the Chinese Union Bible was printed in **two editions**, one using 神 and one using 上帝, because the parties could not agree.

Traffic ran in both directions, and the clearest evidence sits in the most famous verse in the New Testament. The Chinese Union Bible renders **John 1:1** as 太初有道，道與神同在，道就是神 — *"In the beginning was the **Tao**; the Tao was with **神**; the Tao **was** 神."* Two of Laozi's central words — 道 and 神 — were drafted to mean *Logos* and *God*. Once that exchange had happened, English translations of Laozi were being made by people for whom 道 already meant the Word and 神 already meant God.

That is why the standard English Laozi reads like scripture. It was, in a real institutional sense, produced by the same hands.

|---|---|---|
| **天 / 天地** | "Heaven," "Heaven and Earth" — Genesis 1:1 cadence; the missionary word for God's realm | **sky and earth**; 天下 → *the world*; 天 alone → *nature*. See the 天地 entry and the Translation Notes standing principle. |

### Tier 2 — The live watchlist

Each is a place the overlay will try to enter; chapter locations are given so the sweep can be systematic. **Several have since been locked**, and each of those says so and links its entry, which is the ruling; the rest are still open.

**聖 / 聖人 — "the Holy Man," "the Saint," "the Perfect Man"** *(~33 occurrences; also 絕聖 at 19)*
The character is a picture of **hearing**: an oversized ear (耳) beside a mouth (口), on a standing figure. The *Shuowen* glosses it 通也 — *penetrating*. A 聖人 is one whose perception goes all the way through: **the acute listener**, not the sanctified one. The capture is easy to demonstrate — in modern Chinese, Bible translation has made 聖 the word for *holy*: 聖經 (holy classic = Bible), 聖靈 (Holy Spirit), 聖徒 (saint). **Our reading: "the sage" is sound; keep it, and never let "holy," "saintly," or "blessed" near it.** Note also that 聖 is not uniformly positive in this book — Chapter 19 says 絕聖棄智, *cut off sagehood*, which no translator of a "Holy Man" can easily explain.
→ **Locked:** *the sage*; *sagehood* where 聖 stands alone (ch 19). `glossary/sheng-聖.md`, 2026-10-17.

**神 — "God," "the divine," "spirit"** *(6, 29, 39, 60)*
The literal casualty of the Term Question. In Laozi it is **numinous potency** — a quality, not a being. Chapter 60's 其鬼不神 ("the ghosts lose their potency") already applies this correctly. Watch 谷神不死 (6, *the valley's undying power/spirit*), 天下神器 (29, the world as a *numinous vessel*), 神得一以靈 (39). **Never "God." Never "the divine."**

**鬼 — "demons," "devils"** *(60 only)*
Christian demonology imported wholesale. 鬼 are **the dead, ghosts, ancestral spirits** — not agents of evil. "Ghosts" ✓ (already our reading).

**罪 — "sin"** *(62; and the Mawangdui variant at 46)*
In Chinese Christianity 罪 became *the* word for sin (罪人 sinner, 認罪 confess, 贖罪 atone). In Laozi it is **legal**: crime, offense, culpability. Chapter 62's 有罪以免 is *"those with offenses are pardoned"* — a courtroom, not a confessional. Our Manuscript Note at 46 already reads 罪莫大於可欲 as **"no greater crime,"** which is right.

**義 — "righteousness"** *(18, 19, 38 ×3)*
Perhaps the most quietly biblical word in the English tradition — "righteousness" belongs to Paul, to justification, to the righteousness of God. 義 is **duty, rightness, what is fitting** — a Confucian social virtue Laozi is *criticizing* as a late, degraded substitute for the Tao (38's descent: 道 → 德 → 仁 → 義 → 禮). The verse reads *duty* in all five lines; the entry is still owed (`WORKLIST.md` R2). Likewise **仁** (5, 8, 18, 19, 38) → **locked as *humaneness*** (`glossary/ren-仁.md`), not Legge's "benevolence" and never "charity" (1 Corinthians 13); *kindness* is 慈's (*cí* — tenderness) ground, not 仁's.

**精 / 魄 — "spirit," "soul"** *(精: 21 ×2, 55 · 魄: 10)*
Both import the immortal, immaterial Christian soul, and with it a body/soul dualism Chinese thought does not share. 精 is **vital essence** — physical, seminal, energetic (already correct in 55). 魄 is the **corporeal, earthbound** member of the 魂魄 pair; rendering it as a single "soul" erases the fact that there were *two*, and that this one stays with the body. Chapter 10's 載營魄抱一 needs care. *(Resolved 2026-10-15: 魄 → **aliveness**, `glossary/po-魄.md`.)*
→ **Locked:** 精 → *vital essence / essence* (`glossary/jing-精.md`); 魄 → *aliveness* (`glossary/po-魄.md`).

**一 — "the One"** *(10, 22, 39, 42)*
Capitalized, this is Neoplatonism and monotheism — the One of Plotinus, the God who is one. Laozi's 一 is more modest: **unity, the whole, the single thing** — and in 42 it is something the Tao *gives birth to*, therefore not ultimate. Lowercase, and resist the definite-article grandeur.
→ **Locked:** *the one*, lowercase. `glossary/yi-一.md`.

**福 — "blessing"** *(58 ×2, 65)*
"Blessing" implies a **blesser** — a personal agent dispensing favor. 福 is **good fortune**, impersonal, and in 58 explicitly unstable (fortune is where disaster hides). "Fortune" ✓ (already our reading). *Honest nuance:* 福 does carry the 示 radical, associated with altars and offerings, so the word is not purely secular in origin — but it is luck, not grace.

**善 — "good"** *(8, 27 ×5, 49, 79, 81)*
The moral good/evil binary is a Christian inheritance; 善 frequently means **skilled, adept, good *at*** something. Chapter 27's run — 善行, 善言, 善數, 善閉, 善結 — is entirely about *skill*: the skilled traveler leaves no tracks. Read for competence first, morality only where the context demands it.
→ **Locked:** *masterful / masterful at*; *good* only where the text names the category. `glossary/shan-善.md`.

**自然 — "Nature"** *(17, 23, 25, 51, 64)*
Capital-N "Nature" is Romanticism, not Laozi — and in one place the mistranslation is **catastrophic**. Chapter 25's 道法自然 rendered "the Tao follows Nature" makes *Nature superior to the Tao*, inverting the entire chapter. 自然 is **"of itself," "so of itself"**: the Tao takes as its model *being what it is*. There is nothing above it to follow. (Never "self-so" — a calque, and a noun where both commentators refuse to name a thing; see `glossary/ziran-自然.md`.)
→ **Locked:** *of itself / so of itself*. `glossary/ziran-自然.md`.

### Tier 3 — Register, not vocabulary

The overlay also enters below the level of individual words, and this is the harder half to see:

- **King James cadence** — "thee," "thou," "verily," "he that," inverted syntax ("Great is the Tao"). Even with every word correct, KJV rhythm tells the reader they are in scripture. Our English should be *plain, modern, and unadorned* — 樸.
- **Devotional capitalization** — Heaven, Way, Virtue, Truth, the One, Nature, Being. English religious typography confers deity by capital letter. Capitalize only what is genuinely a proper name (the Tao), and interrogate every other capital.
- **The oracular frame** — "Thus saith the Master," "Blessed is the one who…" Beatitude and proverb cadences that turn observation into commandment.
- **Abstraction drift** — replacing concrete images with -ness and -ity nouns (*Emptiness, Oneness, Nothingness*) where Laozi wrote a valley, a hub, an unfired pot. When an abstraction appears in our English, check whether the Chinese held a *thing*.

### Other overlays, for evenhandedness

The missionary lens is the largest, but it is not the only one, and honesty requires naming our own:

- **Greek/philosophical** — "cosmos," "Being" and "Non-Being" for 有/無, "the Absolute," "the Ineffable." Imports metaphysics Laozi does not argue.
- **Victorian moralism** — "virtue," "benevolence," "righteousness," "propriety." A whole ethical register from a different century and continent.
- **Romantic** — "Nature," "the Sublime," the nature-worship frame.
- **New Age / modern spiritual** — "energy," "vibration," "the universe wants," "manifesting," "higher self." Contemporary, unmistakable, and no more native to the text than "Heaven."
- **Our own besetting temptation** *(guide §0)* — the **mechanistic**: "operating system," "source code," "generate," "process." As foreign to a 4th-century-BCE Chinese poem as any hymnal. The razor cuts toward us too.

### The two working tests

Before accepting any rendering, run both:

1. **The hymn test.** *Would this line sit comfortably in a hymn, a sermon, or a King James verse?* If yes, it is probably an import — check the character.
2. **The pointing test.** *Could Laozi have pointed at this?* His vocabulary is water, valley, uncarved wood, infant, dust, bellows, a hub with a hole in it. If our English names a category, a realm, or an abstraction where he named a thing, we have drifted.

### The honest limits

Two cautions, so this does not become its own dogma.

**Not everything churchy-sounding is an import.** The Tao Te Ching *is* a religious text in a real sense — awed, devotional, concerned with ultimate things. Stripping every solemn word would falsify it as badly as importing Heaven does. The target is **imported doctrine**, not reverence. 福 really does have an altar in its radical; 神 really is numinous. We remove what the Chinese does not contain — not everything that sounds sacred.

**And the danger runs both ways.** A translation that flinches from every word touched by Christianity would be shaped by that religion just as surely as one that borrows from it — merely in the negative. The goal is not to translate *against* the missionaries. It is to translate as though they had never come, and to read what the character actually shows.
