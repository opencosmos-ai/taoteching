## 雌 (Cí) — The Hen

*Say "tsuh," rising. Two appearances in two chapters, and both are addressed to you. The book's word for the female as a place a person can stand.*

**Locked: 雌 (*cí*) → "the hen," and its partner 雄 (*xióng*) → "the rooster."** *(Not "the female," which is 牝 (pìn)'s English. Not "the feminine" or "yielding energy," which turn a bird into an abstraction. Not "the receptive" or "the passive," which add a quality no line names. Not "the Hen," since a capital makes her a doctrine.)* *(The English was settled in the verse before this entry, at chapters 10 and 28. The entry argues for it and holds it. That 雌 and 牝 (pìn — the female) get two entries, not one, is Shalom's call, 2026-10-26.)*

### The graph: the mother among birds

> 雌，鳥母也。从隹此聲。
> *"雌: the mother among birds. From 隹 (zhuī — a short-tailed bird), with 此 (cǐ) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

**The class is 隹 (*zhuī*), the short-tailed bird, and 此 (*cǐ* — this) gives only the sound.** The Shuowen defines its partner in matching words: 雄，鳥父也 — *"雄: the father among birds."* It defines the other pair the same way among animals that walk: 牝 (*pìn*) is 畜母 (*chù mǔ* — "the mother among livestock") and 牡 (*mǔ*) is 畜父 (*chù fù* — "the father among livestock"). Two pairs of words for mother and father, one for beasts and one for birds, and the Tao Te Ching uses both (`glossary/pin-牝.md`).

**So the hen's English has to be a bird's.** The book's English gives 牝 and 牡 *the female* and *the male* at chapters 6, 55 and 61. 雌 and 雄 need words of their own, and the graph says which. English ornithology has the exact pair, *cock* and *hen*, for the male and female of any bird (a *cock pheasant*, a *hen harrier*). *Cock* is unusable in modern English, so 雄 is *the rooster*.

### Two lines, and both are asked of you

> *Know the rooster, hold to the hen,*
> *serve as the world's ravine.*
>
> 知其雄，守其雌，
> 為天下谿。
>
> — chapter 28

> *When the gate of the sky opens and closes, can you be the hen?*
>
> 天門開闔，能為雌乎？
>
> — chapter 10

**Neither line describes the hen.** The first tells you to hold to her, and the second asks whether you can be her. 守 (*shǒu* — to hold to) is a post you are answerable for (`glossary/shou-守.md`), and 為 (*wéi* — to be, to serve as) is a part you play.

**The female is the opposite.** 牝 (*pìn*) stands in five lines, and in every one she is spoken about: she has a gate, she mates, she lies beneath. No line asks the reader to be her. So the book keeps one word for the female as a body and another for the female as a stance, and that is why the two characters have separate entries. The pattern is a reason to look, not a proof (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`). Looking, it holds in every line.

### Chapter 28: know the one, hold to the other

**The hen is the first of three pairs, and each pair is built the same way.** 知其雄，守其雌 (*"know the rooster, hold to the hen"*), 知其白，守其黑 (*zhī qí bái, shǒu qí hēi* — "know the white, hold to the black"), 知其白，守其辱 (*zhī qí bái, shǒu qí rǔ* — "know the white, hold to the soiled"). You know one side and hold to the other. The rooster is not refused. It is known, and the hen is chosen with that knowledge.

**Both commentators read the pair as a position.** Wang Bi: 雄先之屬，雌後之屬也。知為天下之先也，必後也 — *"the rooster belongs to the class of going first, the hen to the class of coming after. To know how to be first in the world, one must be behind."* Heshang Gong: 雄以喻尊，雌以喻卑 — *"the rooster figures the honored, the hen the lowly,"* and 去之強梁，就雌之柔和，如是則天下歸之，如水流入深谿也 — *"set aside the swagger and come to the hen's soft harmony; then the world returns to you, as water flows into a deep ravine."*

**The line goes straight on to the ravine.** Holding to the hen, you serve as the world's 谿 (*xī* — ravine), the low place water runs to (`glossary/gu-谷.md`). The hen is where the chapter's lowness begins, and the ravine is what it makes of you.

### Chapter 10: the gate opens, and the hen answers

**Wang Bi reads the hen as the one who answers.** 雌應而不倡，因而不為 — *"the hen responds and does not lead the call; she goes along and does not act."* 倡 (*chàng*) is to lead the singing, the first voice that others follow. The rooster crows, and the hen answers. Of the gate of the sky he says 天門謂天下之所由從也，開闔，治亂之際也 — *"the gate of the sky is where the world passes through; its opening and closing are the turns between order and disorder."* At those turns, be the hen, 則物自賓而處自安矣 — *"and things come as guests of themselves and settle in their places."*

**Heshang Gong reads the gate as the breath.** 治身天門謂鼻孔，開謂喘息，闔謂呼吸也 — *"in caring for the body, the gate of the sky is the nostrils; opening is breathing out, closing is breathing in."* And on the hen: 治身當如雌牝安靜柔弱，治國應變和而不唱 — *"in caring for the body, be like the hen and the female: calm, still, soft and yielding; in governing the state, respond to change in harmony and do not lead the call."* He and Wang Bi agree on the hen. She answers, and she does not lead.

**The line's own graph is contested.** Our base text reads 能為雌乎 (*néng wéi cí hū* — "can you be the hen?"). The Siku edition of Wang Bi prints 能無雌乎 (*néng wú cí hū* — "can you be without the hen?") in its lemma, and its editors note that the commentary's sense reads 為 (*wéi* — to be). Heshang Gong's text also reads 無 (*wú* — without). But his own comment, quoted above, tells the reader to be like the hen, which is what 為 asks. The verse follows 為. `notes/manuscript.md`, `sources/variants.yaml`.

### What the commentators did with her

**Both merge her with the female.** Wang Bi, glossing chapter 61, writes 牝，雌也 — *"the female is the hen,"* and retells that chapter's 牝 and 牡 as hen and rooster. Heshang Gong, above, joins them in one phrase: 雌牝 (*cí pìn* — "the hen and the female"). The text keeps them apart, and so do the two entries. What the commentators hear in both is right: stillness, softness, answering rather than leading. But the hen is the stance and the female is the body, and the book never confuses them.

### 雄 (xióng — the rooster)

**The rooster stands once, and only to be known.** 知其雄 (*zhī qí xióng* — "know the rooster," 28). He has no other line. *Rooster* is narrower than 雄, which is the male of any bird; the pairing with *the hen* carries the sense. 雞 (*jī* — the fowl) at chapter 80 reads *chickens*, which keeps *rooster* free for 雄 (`chapters/080.md`).

### What is set aside

**"the female."** It is 牝 (*pìn*)'s English, in chapters 6, 55 and 61. A rendering already spoken for by another character is not available (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`). Giving the hen *the female* would erase the book's line between the body and the stance.

**"the feminine," "yielding energy."** They turn a bird into a quality. A hen can be held to, and a person can try to be one. *The feminine* is a category, and the pointing test fails. *Energy* is the mechanistic overlay this edition watches for.

**"the receptive," "the passive."** No line says she receives, and Wang Bi's hen is not passive: she answers. 應 (*yìng* — to respond) is an act. *Passive* hands her the verdict the rooster's admirers would give her, and the line passes none (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`).

**"the Hen."** A capital makes her a principle. Only the Tao keeps one.

### Working register

| Where 雌 (*cí*) stands | English | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| the object of 守 (*shǒu* — hold to) | *hold to the hen* | 28 (守其雌 *shǒu qí cí*) |
| the complement of 為 (*wéi* — to be) | *can you be the hen?* | 10 (能為雌乎 *néng wéi cí hū*) |

**Her pronoun is *she*.** 雌 says she is the hen, and the line is about her. Standing rule 1 forbids *he* for the sage: the Chinese never says whether the sage is a woman or a man, and *he* would make the male the default.

### What no rule can enforce

***Soft* and *yielding* cannot be ruled out here, though the hen is not their word.** Chapter 10 holds 柔 (*róu* — soft), which its verse renders *"Gathering the breath to softness,"* so forbidding *soft* for the hen would flag a correct line. *Yielding* is 弱 (*ruò*)'s English across the book. Both commentators call the hen soft, and that is their reading, not her name.

***Yin* is ruled out and cannot go on the list.** The checker matches strings, not words, and *yin* sits inside words the verse prints. 陰 (*yīn*) stands once in the book, in chapter 42, and never names the hen.

**The lock holds in this book only.** Forbidding *female* for the hen depends on this book's split, which gives *female* to 牝 (*pìn*). The I Ching vendors these locks as its own and draws its own lines between its animals, so this entry carries `binds: [taoteching]`, as `glossary/pin-牝.md` does.

### Left open

**What *rooster* costs.** 雄 is the male of any bird, and *rooster* is the farmyard's. *Hen* is wide in English, the female of any bird, and *rooster* is not. The pair stands, and the asymmetry is the price of losing *cock*.

**Whether the hen is a bird or a place in a line.** Both commentators read her as a position: after, low, answering. The text names a bird. The English keeps the bird, so a reader can see what the commentators saw in her without being told it.

**What chapter 10 asks, if the other reading is right.** The verse follows 為 (*wéi* — to be) on the strength of Wang Bi's own commentary. If 無 (*wú* — without) was the reading, chapter 10 asks whether you can do without the hen when the gate opens, which is a different question, and a stranger one. Neither commentator answers it.
