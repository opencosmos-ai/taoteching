## 王 (Wáng) — Sovereign

*Say "wahng," rising. Thirteen lines across eight chapters, and not one of them shows a 王 commanding anything.*

**Locked: 王 → "sovereign," in every line — and *supreme authority* at chapter 16, where 王 is a rung in a sequence rather than a person.** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-20, which gave* ruler *to 君 (jūn) and* sovereign *to 王 alone.)* **侯 (*hóu*) → "nobles," inside 侯王: *nobles and sovereigns*** *(Shalom's call, the same day).* *(Never "king," never "kings," never "monarch," never "emperor." Never "ruler," which is 君's.)*

Every English Tao Te Ching renders 王 as **king**. The word arrives so naturally that the question never gets asked, and three separate things ride in with it.

**It adds a gender the Chinese does not have.** Classical Chinese nouns carry no grammatical gender, and 王 contains no male element. English *king* is lexically male — it exists in a pair with *queen*, and no context can neutralise it. Rendering 王 as *king* is not translating a gender. It is **adding one**.

**It adds a throne.** Every English word for a monarch encodes elevation: the crown, the dais, the seat above. And in all eight chapters where 王 appears, Laozi is arguing that authority is what lies **underneath**. *Sovereign* keeps the height, because the height is what 王 means, and drops the sex and the crown. What the book does with the height is the subject of the sections below.

**The throne is not, however, a later insertion into the Chinese.** An earlier version of this entry said it was, on a bad reading of the Ch 25 witnesses. Corrected 2026-08-20: every excavated witness there reads 王, Guodian included, and the human reading is transmitted (傅奕, Tang; 范應元, Song). See `notes/manuscript.md` · Ch 25. **The case against *king* is unaffected**, because it never rested on the manuscripts — it rests on the graph and on the eight chapters below.

### The graph: an axe

The oracle-bone form of 王 is a **broad axe-head, blade downward** — the ceremonial 鉞 (*yuè*), the great bronze battle-axe carried before a war leader. In Shang practice it was the instrument of execution, so to hold it was to hold the power over life and death.

There is no person in the graph. No head, no body, no gender. **An implement.**

### The king who joins the three realms is Han cosmology, not etymology

The explanation everyone has met — three horizontal strokes for sky, earth and humanity, one vertical stroke for the king who unites them — comes from **董仲舒** (*Dǒng Zhòngshū*, c. 179–104 BCE), the architect of Han state Confucianism, in his 春秋繁露 (*Chūnqiū Fánlù*). It entered the *Shuowen Jiezi* from there, and from the *Shuowen* it entered every dictionary since.

It is roughly **fourteen centuries younger than the graph it explains**, and it is doing visible work: it makes the emperor a structural necessity of the cosmos rather than a man with an axe. Sincere cosmology and imperial legitimation at once, which is the usual condition of such things.

**A word of caution about how far this reaches.** It is tempting to pair Dong Zhongshu's reading of the graph with Chapter 25, where the received text lists the four great things as 道大，天大，地大，**王**亦大 and some editions read 人 (*rén* — human) instead. This entry once did exactly that. It does not hold: **every excavated witness at Ch 25 reads 王**, Guodian (~300 BCE) included, and 人 enters the record with 傅奕 in the Tang and 范應元 in the Song. Dong Zhongshu cosmologised the graph; nobody can be shown to have put the king into that line. *(See `notes/manuscript.md` · Ch 25 for the apparatus and the correction.)*

### The title the world goes to, and 君 (jūn — ruler) beside it

**The Shuowen's own definition, before Dong Zhongshu's strokes, is a direction:**

> 王，天下所歸往也。
> *"王 is that to which the world returns and goes."*

**The *Hanshi waizhuan* (2nd century BCE) says it by sound, and sets 君 (*jūn* — ruler) beside it:**

> 君者何也？曰：群也，為天下萬物而除其害者，謂之君。王者何也？曰：往也。天下往之謂之王。
> *"What is the 君? It is 群 (qún — the flock): the one who removes harm for the world's countless things is called 君. What is the 王? It is 往 (wǎng — to go to). What the world goes to is called 王."* — juan 5

It closes the passage with a second pun: 往之謂之王，去之謂之亡 — *"what the world goes to is called 王 (wáng); what it leaves is called 亡 (wáng — lost)."* These are Han sound-glosses, political theory more than etymology, and scholars caution that no pre-Qin text uses 君 to mean a flock. **But the book acts the gloss out.** Chapter 35: 執大象，天下往, *"grasp the great image, and the world comes to you,"* the gloss's own 往. Chapter 66: rivers and seas are 王 of the hundred valleys because every valley runs down to them, and Heshang Gong glosses it with the Shuowen's own verb, 江海以卑故眾流歸之 (*"rivers and seas, being low, so the many streams return to them"*). Chapter 32 draws the same water: *"the Tao in the world is like small valleys to the river and the sea."*

**And the book keeps the two words apart.** 王 always stands beside 天下 (*tiān xià* — the world), in the cosmic ladder, or in the rank-pairs 侯王 and 王公: the title at the top of the world's order. 君 is always the ruler *of* something, stillness of the restless (26), a ruler behind affairs (70), and it never rules the world (`glossary/jun-君.md`). So the English keeps them apart too: *ruler* for 君, which names a relation, and *sovereign* for 王, which names the top.

### How Laozi actually uses it — the position that receives

Read the eight chapters together and something becomes hard to miss. **Not one of them shows a 王 commanding anything.** Every appearance defines the word by lowness, absorption, or a condition unmet.

| Chapter | Chinese | What the 王 does |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | 公乃**王**，**王**乃天 | a **stage in a sequence** — impartiality leads to it, it leads to sky. No person at all. |
| 25 | 王亦大 *(傅奕/范應元: 人)* | one of four vast things — **beside** sky and earth, not above them |
| 32 | 侯**王**若能守之 | **if** they can hold to it. Conditional, and the outcome belongs to the countless things |
| 37 | 侯**王**若能守之 | same conditional |
| 39 | 是以侯**王**自謂孤、寡、不穀 | **calls itself orphaned, meagre, unworthy** |
| 42 | **王**公以為稱 | takes the same self-abasing titles |
| 66 | 江海所以能為百谷**王**者，以其善下之 | is sovereign of the hundred valleys **because it stays below them** |
| 78 | 受國之不祥，是謂天下**王** | **takes on the state's misfortune** and is therefore called it |

Chapter 78 is the plainest definition the book gives: 受國之垢，是謂社稷主；受國之不祥，是謂天下王 — *"the one who takes on the state's shame is called lord of the altars; the one who takes on the state's misfortune is called sovereign of the world."* The title is earned by absorbing what nobody else will touch.

**A word defined seven times over by what it goes under still means the thing on top, and that is the paradox.** The book keeps the word for the summit and changes what it takes to get there. If the English softened the summit, to *ruler* or to *leader*, the paradox would have nothing to turn. So the English keeps a word for the top, *sovereign*, and lets the lines do the redefining (`process/principles/perform-dont-label.md`).

### 侯 (hóu — nobles), a rank below, that comes to the sovereign's court

侯 (*hóu*) appears only inside 侯王, in chapters 32, 37 and 39. Its graph holds 矢 (*shǐ* — an arrow) beneath a shelter, and the Shuowen says it began as **an archery target**, 春饗所䠶矦也 (*"the target shot at the spring feast"*); the feudal rank came from the ritual archery through which it was conferred. Conventionally rendered *marquis*, which imports a European peerage.

**The Shuowen's own entry ranks the 侯 below the 王.** It quotes the archers' invocation: 毋若不寧侯，不朝于王所，故伉而䠶汝也 — *"do not be like the unruly 侯 who do not come to court at the 王's place, for which we raise the bow and shoot at you."* The 侯 is the one who goes to the 王, which is the 王 of the *Hanshi waizhuan*.

**So 侯王 names two ranks, and the English names both: *nobles and sovereigns*** (Shalom's call, 2026-11-20). *Lords and sovereigns* was the conventional choice and was declined: *lord* leans male, as *king* does, and it is already the word of 公 (*gōng*) at 42 and 主 (*zhǔ*) at 26. *Nobles* is a rank with no sex. Its costs are named under *Left open*. A single class word, *sovereigns*, was declined too: it promotes the nobles to the top rank, which the Shuowen's archers would not allow.

### What is set aside

**king, kings** — gendered in English where the Chinese is not, and it seats the word on the throne these chapters are arguing out from under. The lock's leading space is deliberate; see the note below.

**monarch, emperor** — the same throne, plus an anachronism: there was no emperor of China until 221 BCE, and *monarch* carries the constitutional apparatus of a much later world.

**ruler** — 君 (*jūn*)'s, since 2026-11-20 (Shalom's call). It names a relation, the ruler *of* something, and the book's 王 is a summit. **A ruling no list holds** (below).

**head of state** — a constitutional office of the modern state, and it would break the book's ladder: 王 is sovereign of 天下 (*tiān xià* — the world), which the book keeps above 國 (*guó* — the state) (`glossary/guo-國.md`). *Head of state of the hundred valleys* cannot stand at chapter 66.

**leader** — forbidden for 長 (*zhǎng* — chief) (`glossary/chang-長.md`), and it means going first, where chapter 66's 王 gets there by going below and behind.

**lord** — belongs to 公 (*gōng*), which is locked to *lord / minister* in `gong-公.md`, and stands beside 王 in Chapter 42's 王公. Also leans male, which is why 侯 is *nobles* (above).

**master** — 主 (*zhǔ*)'s territory, and the wrong relation besides. The sage in this book is nobody's master.

**sage-king, philosopher-king** — imports a Confucian and a Platonic ideal in one phrase, neither of which is here.

**sovereignty** — the abstract noun, not the person. It took two passes to see, because the word sounds abstract rather than regal. It is the central term of the **modern nation-state** — Bodin in 1576, Hobbes in 1651 — carrying territorial supremacy, legitimacy and non-interference. That is precisely the fault *monarch* is rejected for one entry above: **the constitutional apparatus of a much later world**, and a heavier import than the word thrown out for importing it. It held Chapter 16's rung until 2026-09-05.

*Chapter 16 takes **supreme authority** instead: no person, so the chain stays a widening of scope, and 王弼's 無所不周普 (nowhere not universally pervading) still reads through it. Two soft collisions were weighed and accepted — 威 (wēi — overawing force) carries* authority *once at ch 72, and 上 (shàng — upper) carries* supreme *once at ch 31 inside the rank-title 上將軍. Both are single occurrences in chapters with no 王, and an inconsistency is a smaller fault than an overlay.*

### What no rule can enforce

***Ruler* is ruled out and not forbidden.** No other character in 王's chapters prints it, so the gate could hold it here. But the I Ching vendors this lock as its own (`binds:` is absent), and it prints *ruler* for 王 in every hexagram that holds it, by the lock as it stood before 2026-11-20. Forbidding *ruler* would fail that book's gate on its next import, before it has decided its own 王. `concordance.py --english "ruler"` holds the ruling here: it reports every *ruler* as 君's, and lists any other as not backed.

### Working register

| Form | Chapters | English |
|---|---|---|
| 王, in a sequence | 16 | **supreme authority** |
| 為…王, a title become | 66 | *be sovereign of* the hundred valleys |
| 侯王, two ranks | 32, 37, 39 | *nobles and sovereigns* |
| 王公 | 42 | *sovereigns and lords* |
| 天下王 | 78 | *the sovereign of the world* |

**Chapter 66 is a noun, not a verb.** 能為百谷王 is 為 (*wéi* — to be, become) with 王 as its object: the rivers become the title. It read *rule the hundred valleys* until 2026-11-20, which turned a title into an act; *be sovereign of*, with no article, is the office, as English says *be king of*. 王 can be a verb in classical Chinese (*wàng* — to reign over), but the book never uses it so.

**A note on the lock string.** *king* is forbidden as **" king"**, with a leading space, because as a bare substring it fires inside *walking*, *cooking*, *taking*, *making*, *seeking* and *thinking*. None currently occur in these eight chapters; one rewrite would be enough. The space is the difference between a lock and a nuisance.

### Left open

**The office really was male, and the English no longer says so.** Where 侯王 and 王公 name the actual ruling stratum of the late Zhou — chapters 32, 37, 39, 42 — the people addressed were men. *Nobles and sovereigns* is true to the words and silent about the world. This edition renders toward the universal and notes the seam here rather than in the verse, which is standing rule 1 working as written; but the silence is real and a reader should know the room Laozi was speaking into.

**Two costs of *nobles*, accepted (2026-11-20).** *The noble* is 君子 (*jūn zǐ* — a ruler's son)'s English at chapters 26 and 31, so a reader will take the two for one word. The share is close to true: 君子 is the hereditary class the 侯 heads. And chapter 39's 貴 (*guì* — prized) read *"the noble is rooted in the humble"* until 2026-09-05, removed as a moralized price (`glossary/gui-jian-貴賤.md`); *nobles* now stands in that chapter three times, as a class, not a virtue.

**The I Ching has not decided.** It vendors this lock, prints *ruler* for 王, and gives *sovereign* to 后 (*hòu* — the sovereign of the Zhou) in the Great Images of hexagrams 11 and 44; at hexagram 24, 后 stands beside 王 in one line (先王…后不省方 (*xiān wáng … hòu bù xǐng fāng* — "the former 王… the 后 does not tour the regions")). Whether it follows *sovereign* for 王, keeps *ruler* with a `binds: [taoteching]` here, or finds 后 another word is its call (`WORKLIST.md` T5-29).

***Sovereign* and the hymn test.** *Sovereign Lord* is a hymnal phrase, and Calvinist theology speaks of God's sovereignty. The noun for a person is older and plainer than the theology, and the abstract noun stays forbidden. Whether readers hear the hymn is worth listening for.

**And whether the book is undermining the throne or advising it.** Chapters 32 and 37 speak *to* 侯王 with a conditional — *if nobles and sovereigns can hold to this* — which is counsel offered to power, on power's own terms. Chapters 66 and 78 do something else: they redefine the word so that it names whoever goes lowest and absorbs the most. Reform from inside, or a redefinition that empties the office out.

The text does not choose, and the same doubleness sits in 爭 — see `zheng-爭.md`, where non-contention is either an ethic or the most effective possible strategy in the contest being renounced. Both may be one question wearing two characters.
