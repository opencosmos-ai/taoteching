## 命 (Mìng) — What You Were Sent With

*Say "ming," falling. Three lines in two chapters, and one of them is the hinge of Chapter 16's chain toward clear-seeing.*

**Recommended, not yet locked: 命 → the allotted course — what is spoken over a thing and sent with it.** *(Never "mandate," never "destiny," never "fate," never "decree." Chapter 16's line and Chapter 51's verb are both still open; see the close.)*

說文解字 (c. 100 CE): **使也。从口从令** — *"命 is 使 (shǐ — to send; also, an envoy). From 口 (kǒu — a mouth) and 令 (lìng)."*

Each part, one level down:

- **口 (*kǒu*)** — 說文: **人所以言食也。象形** — *"what a person speaks and eats with. A pictograph."* A mouth.
- **令 (*lìng*)** — 說文: **發號也。从亼、卪** — *"to issue a call. From 亼 (jí) and 卪 (jié)."*
- **亼 (*jí*)** — the assembling sign, three strokes converging. Read either as an inverted 口, or as a **hand-bell rung to call people in**.
- **卪 (*jié*, also written 卩)** — **a kneeling figure.** *(說文 itself reads it as 瑞信也, "an auspicious tally"; the kneeling-person reading is modern palaeography. Neither component is among the Laozi's 711 characters, so the repository's 說文 does not carry them.)*

**And 命 and 令 were one graph.** In the oracle-bone script they are the same character — the gathering-sign above, the kneeling figure below. A chief sounds a bell to call people in; whoever receives the order kneels to take it. **口 was added during the Western Zhou bronze period**, and by the small seal script the two had separated.

**So the graph stacks up into a scene**: a summons, a gathering, someone on their knees, and a mouth. 命 is 令 with the utterance made explicit — and the dictionary defines the whole with 使, the word for *sending* and for *the person sent*.

**命 is what is spoken over you, and then you go.** Not a plan, not a fate written in advance: an instruction handed to someone at the moment they are dispatched. Everything below follows from that being a **speech act with a destination**, rather than a decree of the universe.

### 天命 is not in this book

天命 (*tiān mìng* — the Mandate of Heaven) is the compound this character is famous for: the Zhou dynasty's founding political theology, in which a ruler holds power by warrant from 天 (*tiān* — sky) and forfeits it by misrule. It is the single most consequential thing 命 does in classical Chinese.

**It has zero occurrences in the Tao Te Ching.**

So *mandate* imports a warrant-to-rule the book never invokes, and *destiny* and *fate* import a Greek and a Christian freight on top of it. All three make 命 a plot written in advance. The character describes a thing said out loud to someone who then leaves the room.

### 令 is in the book, and the two collapse in English

令 runs eight lines across four chapters, and the book puts the two characters in the **same frame** one chapter apart:

> ch 32 — 民莫之**令**而自均 — *"the people, with no one 令-ing them, even out of themselves"*
> ch 51 — 夫莫之**命**而常自然 — *"with no one 命-ing it, it is always so of itself"*

Identical construction 莫之X而, different character — **and the two were one graph before the Western Zhou split them**, which is why they are so easy to collapse and so worth keeping apart. The graphs separate them cleanly: **令 is the order as issued** — impersonal, and the book uses it that way (ch 12's 五色令人目盲, *"the five colours make people's eyes blind"*; ch 57's 法令, *"laws and decrees"*). **命 is the order as given to someone who is then sent.** One is the decree; the other is the commission.

### The commentators agree, in a word the text does not have

**王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, d. 249 CE):** 歸根則靜，故曰靜。靜則復命，故曰復命也。復命則得**性命**之常，故曰常也 — *"returning to the root is stillness, hence 'stillness.' Stillness is 復命, hence '復命.' 復命 is attaining the constancy of 性命 (xìng mìng — inborn nature and allotted span), hence 'the ever-present.'"*

**河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*, Han):** 言安靜者是為復還**性命**使不死也 — *"the still one is returning to their 性命, so as not to die."*

Both reach for **性命**. And **性 (*xìng* — inborn nature) has zero occurrences in the Tao Te Ching.** It is the vocabulary of a later settlement — 性 becomes a central term after this text, and both commentators are reading it back.

Their reading may still be right. But it is not the book's own word, and an English that says *nature* at Chapter 16 would be translating the commentary rather than the line. *(`CLAUDE.md`: a commentator's gloss is not the text.)*

### 復命 has its own entry

The compound at Chapter 16 — 是謂復命，復命曰常 — is treated in **`fuming-復命.md`**, including the two readings the book supports (the commentators' *return to what one was allotted*, and the idiom's *an envoy reporting back*), and the three candidate renderings with their costs.

### What is set aside

**mandate** — 天命's word, and 天命 is absent from this book. It imports the Zhou warrant-to-rule, and with it a 天 that grants and withdraws, which is precisely the overlay this edition strips from 天 (see `tian-天.md`).

**destiny, fate** — both make 命 a script settled before the actor arrives. 命 is spoken *to* someone, at a moment, and it can be reported back on. *Fate* additionally carries the Greek Moirai and the Christian providence in a text that has neither.

**decree, law** — 令's territory, and the book uses 法令 (*fǎ lìng*) for exactly that at ch 57.

**command** — 令 already carries it at ch 32, in the identical 莫之X而 frame. If 命 takes it too at ch 51, the pair the book distinguishes collapses into one English word. *(This is the open question below, not a settled prohibition — ch 51 currently reads* commands*.)*

**"the system's command"** — the Source table's literal gloss, and mechanistic in the way `CLAUDE.md` names as this edition's besetting temptation.

### The tension, left open

**Chapter 16's line is settled.** 是謂復命，復命曰常 → *"returning to what is given"* — present tense, giver present but unnamed. The four candidates it beat, and why, are in `fuming-復命.md`.

**Chapter 51's verb**, 夫莫之命而常自然, reads *"no one commands it"* — 令's English, in 令's own construction.

**And Chapter 51 has a variant.** Our vendored 王弼 carries 命並作爵 — *"命 is written 爵 (jué — to confer a noble rank) [in other editions]"* — under a collation note flagging the line as probably an editorial gloss mistaken for Wang Bi's own comment: 〔案此句疑係命字下原校注語誤作弼注〕. If 爵, the line reads *"no one ennobles it."* That is the conferral sense of 命 showing through in a second character, and it argues that whatever is happening at ch 51 is a **granting**, not an ordering.

**令 is owed an entry of its own.** Eight lines, four chapters, and at least three Englishes — *makes* (12), *command* (32), *laws* (57).
