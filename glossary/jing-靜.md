## 靜 (Jìng) — Stillness

*Say "jing," falling. Ten appearances in nine lines across seven chapters. Water that has stopped moving, so the silt goes down: the book's stillness is something that works.*

**Locked: 靜 → "still" and "stillness," and "still" as a verb (to make still).** *(Locked 2026-11-19, Shalom's call, after the chapter pass.)* *(Not "tranquil" or "serene," the spa and the hymnal. Not "meditation" or anything built on it, which turns a state of water into a practice. Not "passive" or "inert," which this edition refuses for the female the book makes still at chapter 61.)* **Covered:** 躁 (zào — restless), its opposite, → *restless* and *restlessness* (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19).

### The graph: the green of new growth, and a word for contending

> 靜，審也。从青，爭聲。
> *"靜: to settle and examine closely. From 青 (qīng — the green of new growth), with 爭 (zhēng — to contend) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

**The Shuowen defines stillness as 審, close and settled attention**, the look you can give a thing only when nothing is moving: water that has cleared enough to see into. Its meaning part is 青, the color of a fresh shoot and of deep clear water, the same 青 that gives 清 (*qīng* — clear) its sound (`glossary/qing-清.md`). The two words meet twice in the book, and both times the stillness comes first and the clearness follows: 濁以靜之徐清 (*"by stilling it, slowly come clear,"* 15) and 清靜 (*"clear stillness,"* 45).

*A calibration.* 爭 is the sound part, and the Shuowen says nothing more of it. A reader of this book, whose sage *does not contend* (不爭), may notice the word for contending sitting inside the word for stillness. We do not claim the scribes meant it.

**The silks spell it by its sound.** In the collated silk text, chapters 15 and 16 write 情 (*qíng* — feeling, 青 with 心 (*xīn* — the heart)), and chapter 61 writes 靚 (*jìng* — 青 with 見 (*jiàn* — to see)). Each keeps 青 and changes the rest, the way a scribe writes a word by its sound.

### Nine lines, and stillness always does something

> *Who can be muddy, and by stilling it slowly come clear?*
>
> 孰能濁以靜之徐清？
>
> — chapter 15

> *Hold steadily to stillness.*
>
> 守靜篤。
>
> — chapter 16

> *The female always overcomes the male by stillness.*
> *By stillness she is underneath.*
>
> 牝常以靜勝牡，
> 以靜為下。
>
> — chapter 61

**In every line, stillness is a cause.** It clears muddy water (15). It is what returning to the root is called (16). It rules the restless (26). It lets the world right itself (37, 57). It overcomes heat (45). It is how the female overcomes the male (61). Nowhere is it a mood to be enjoyed or a retreat from things. Heshang Gong on chapter 15 says how it works: 誰能知水之濁止而淨之，徐徐自清也, *"who can know the water's muddiness, stop it and still it, so that slowly it comes clear of itself?"* You do not clean the water. You stop stirring it.

**Chapter 16 makes stillness the root itself.** Heshang Gong: 靜謂根也。根安靜柔弱，謙卑處下，故不復死也, *"stillness means the root. The root is calm, still, soft and yielding, low and beneath, and so it does not die again."* Wang Bi: 歸根則靜，故曰靜。靜則復命, *"returning to the root is stillness, hence 'stillness.' Stillness is returning to the given"* (`glossary/fuming-復命.md`).

### Chapter 26: what does not move governs what moves

> *The heavy is the root of the light.*
> *Stillness is the ruler of the restless.*
>
> 重為輕根，
> 靜為躁君。
>
> — chapter 26

**Wang Bi reads the pair as mechanics.** 不行者使行，不動者制動，是以重必為輕根，靜必為躁君也, *"what does not go makes things go; what does not move governs what moves. So the heavy must be the root of the light, and the still the ruler of the restless."*

**Heshang Gong gives it two animals.** 龍靜故能變化，虎躁故夭虧也, *"the dragon is still, and so it can transform; the tiger is restless, and so it dies young."* And for the ruler: 王者行躁疾則失其君位，治身躁疾則失其精神也, *"a king who acts in restless haste loses his throne; one caring for the body in restless haste loses their vital spirit."* That is the chapter's last line, 躁則失君 (*zào zé shī jūn* — "restlessness loses the ruler").

### 躁 (zào — restlessness)

> 躁，疾也。从走，喿聲。
> *"躁: hasty. From 走 (zǒu — to run), with 喿 (zào) for the sound."* — Shuowen

**躁 is a body running, in a hurry.** Its sound part draws three mouths over 木 (*mù* — a tree), and the whole graph looks like the thing it names: legs going and a racket overhead. It stands three times, all against 靜, and it lives in this entry because it never appears without stillness set against it.

**Outside this book, 躁 is someone who cannot wait.** Three early texts use it, and all three describe a person moving of their own accord, too soon and too much:

> 侍於君子有三愆：言未及之而言，謂之躁。
> *"Attending on a noble person, there are three faults. To speak before your turn has come is called 躁."* — Analects 16.6

> 吉人之辭寡，躁人之辭多。
> *"The fortunate person's words are few; the 躁 person's words are many."* — the Commentary on the Appended Phrases (繫辭, *Xìcí*), part 2

> 君子齊戒，處必掩身，毋躁。
> *"The noble fasts and keeps vigil, keeps the body covered wherever they are, and does not rush about."* — Book of Rites, Monthly Ordinances (禮記·月令), at midsummer, when 陰陽爭 (*yīn yáng zhēng* — "yin and yang contend")

**Speaking out of turn, talking too much, rushing about.** The Xici's line is this book's own pair from the other side: chapter 23's 希言 (*xī yán* — sparse speech) and chapter 5's 多言 (*duō yán* — much speech). And the midsummer rite sets 躁 against the season's heat, as chapter 45 does.

**English *agitation* means two things, and 躁 is neither.**

- ***Agitate* in its first sense is to stir or shake something**, Latin *agitare*, to drive, to set going: a solution in a flask, the water in a washing machine. That sense is transitive. Something agitates the water, and the water is moved. **躁 is not moved by anything.** The person who speaks before their turn, the tiger, the ruler who rushes off ahead of the baggage wagons: each moves itself. *The agitated*, in chapter 26, quietly makes the restless one a victim of some outside stirring, and the chapter makes them the cause of their own loss.
- ***Agitated* in its common sense is upset**: anxious, irritated, worked up. That is a mood, and in modern Chinese 躁 can carry it, in 煩躁 (*fán zào* — fretful) and 暴躁 (*bào zào* — hot-tempered). **The early texts do not.** The Analects' 躁 is not angry; it is impatient. The Xici's 躁 person is not upset; they talk too much. Read *agitated* as *upset*, and chapter 26 says that a ruler who is irritable loses the throne, a narrower and different claim.

**What 躁 is, then, is restlessness**: the want of rest, in the body and in the temper at once, moving because one cannot keep still. *Restless* is the plain English antonym of *still*, built on the same idea, and it carries both halves of the early usage, the bodily rushing and the impatience. Wang Bi already reaches for it at chapter 61: 雄躁動貪欲, *"the rooster is restless and greedy."* You can point at a restless horse, a restless child, a restless sleeper.

> *Restlessness overcomes cold; stillness overcomes heat.*
> *Clear stillness brings order to the world.*
>
> 躁勝寒，靜勝熱。
> 清靜為天下正。
>
> — chapter 45

**Chapter 45 read 躁 as *movement* until 2026-11-19, and neither commentator does.** Wang Bi: 躁罷然後勝寒，靜無為以勝熱。以此推之，則清靜為天下正也。靜則全物之真，躁則犯物之性, *"only when restlessness is spent does it overcome cold; stillness, not doing, overcomes heat. Reason from this, and clear stillness is the world's standard. Stillness keeps things whole and genuine; restlessness violates their nature."* Heshang Gong reads the first clause as a season: 春夏陽氣躁疾於上，萬物盛大，極則寒，寒則零落死亡也。言人不當剛躁也, *"in spring and summer the yang breath rushes overhead and the countless things grow great; at its height it turns to cold, and with cold they fall and die. It says a person should not be hard and restless."* For both, 躁 is still chapter 26's, a thing that wins a little and costs much. *Movement* was neutral where they are not, and it cut the tie to chapter 26.

**Settled, in all three lines (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19):**

> *Stillness is the ruler of the restless.* (26)
> *Restlessness loses the ruler.* (26)
> *Restlessness overcomes cold; stillness overcomes heat.* (45)

The physical sense at chapter 45 survives, since a body that cannot keep still keeps warm, and the reader hears chapter 26 again.

### What is set aside

**"Tranquil," "tranquility," "serene," "serenity."** The register of the spa brochure and the evening prayer, and both are moods. The book's stillness is a cause, and it clears water.

**"Meditation," "meditative," "meditate."** A practice imported from later Buddhist and modern usage. Chapter 16's *hold steadily to stillness* is not a sitting instruction; Heshang Gong's stillness is the root's.

**"Quietude."** A Quaker and Romantic word, and a sound word: stillness heard, not stillness seen. 靜 is first of all water not moving.

**"Quiet."** Sound, which belongs to 希 (*xī* — barely heard). In modern Chinese 安靜 means *quiet*; in this book, 靜 stills water. It can stay plain English elsewhere.

**"Passive," "inert," "inactive."** For chapter 61's female, this edition's first standing rule refuses any English that makes her place an assignment, and *passive* by name (`CLAUDE.md`; `glossary/pin-牝.md`). Stillness in this book overcomes; *inert* cannot overcome anything.

**"Calm."** Not wrong, and kept for other characters: 安 (*ān* — at rest) and 恬 (*tián* — placid) need it. 靜 keeps one word.

**"Movement," "motion," for 躁.** Neutral where the character is hasty, and 動 (*dòng* — to move) is the book's word for moving (5, 8, 15, 40, 50). *Movement* also cannot be forbidden, since 動 shares the 靜 chapters.

**"Agitation," "agitated," for 躁.** Forbidden, and wrong in both its senses, as argued above. As stirring, it makes the restless one moved by something else; as upset, it makes 躁 a mood. The early texts' 躁 is neither: it is impatience, too many words, rushing about.

**"Haste," "hasty," "hurry."** The Shuowen's own sense, 疾也, and right at the Analects. But haste is speed, and the book's pair is stillness against its want; *haste* has *slowness* for its opposite, not *stillness*. *Hurry* is also spoken for: *unhurried* reads 繟 (*chǎn* — easy, slack) at chapter 73. Kept for notes.

**"Impatient," "rash," "impetuous."** Each catches one half, the temper without the body, and *rash* adds recklessness the texts do not.

### What no rule can enforce

***Calm* and *movement* are ruled out for 靜 and 躁, and cannot be forbidden.** 安 (*ān* — at rest) stands in chapter 15 beside 靜 and may print *calm*; 動 (*dòng* — to move) stands there too and prints *move*.

***Quiet* is held by argument.** It is plain English the verse may need for other things, and no line of the 靜 chapters prints it now.

### Left open

**Chapter 37's line was rebuilt with the 欲 entry** (2026-11-19): *"Not desiring, and so still, the world will right itself."* 靜's English did not change.

**Chapter 26's couplet mixes its forms.** *The heavy is the root of the light* keeps both halves adjectival; *stillness is the ruler of the restless* makes one a noun and one an adjective. The Chinese is four bare characters on each side. Noted, not worth a rewrite on its own.

**The silks' 情 at chapters 15 and 16.** 情 is *feeling*, and the silk reading *"holding to feeling"* would be a different chapter. Read as a sound spelling of 靜, which is how both commentators' texts print it, it changes nothing; recorded here because a reader of the silks will meet it.
