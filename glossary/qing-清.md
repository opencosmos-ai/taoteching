## 清 (Qīng) — Clear

*Say "ching," level. Four lines across three chapters.*

**Locked: 清 → "clear."** *(Not "pure" or "purity," and not "clarity," which is 明's noun. Not "settled," which is 寧's, or "still," which is 靜's, but those rulings no check can hold. See the last section but one.)*

*濁 (zhuó — muddy), its opposite, stands twice in ch 15 and is covered here.*

清 is the clearness of water after the silt has gone down, and of sky without cloud. The book never tells anyone to make something clear. It asks who can let muddy water be still, and wait: 孰能濁以靜之徐清, *"Who can be muddy, and by stilling it slowly come clear?"* (15). The clearing is slow, and it happens of itself. Elsewhere the sky receives the one, and so becomes clear (39), and clear stillness brings order to the world (45).

### The graph: water that has settled

> 清，**朖也。澂水之皃**。从水青聲。
> *"清 is bright. The look of water that has settled. From 水 (shuǐ — water), with 青 (qīng) for the sound."* — 說文解字

澂 (*chéng*) is 澄, to let water stand until the sediment sinks. The dictionary's picture is a process, and it is ch 15's process exactly: muddy, then still, then clear. **Nothing is taken out of the water.** The silt is still in the pool, on the bottom. Clear water is water that has stopped being stirred.

The first gloss, 朖 (*lǎng* — bright), is a light word, and the sky line of ch 39 can bear it. 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) reads 天得一以清 as 垂象清明, *"the sky hangs its images clear and bright."* English *clear* holds both senses: clear water, clear sky.

**青 gives the sound, and it stands in 靜 (*jìng* — stillness) too.** 說文 files 靜 under 青: 靜，審也。从青爭聲. The two words meet twice in the book, 濁以靜之徐清 at ch 15 and 清靜 at ch 45. In 清 the 青 is only sound, so this is a resemblance and not a pun the text was built on.

### 清 and 濁: the one opposite the book gives it

**說文 does not define 濁 as muddy.** It gives a river: 濁，水。出齊郡厲嬀山，東北入鉅定, *"濁 is a river. It rises at Mount Lìguī in Qí commandery and flows northeast into Jùdìng."* The muddy sense comes from use, and the book uses it twice, both in ch 15: 混兮其若濁, *"Mixed, like muddy water,"* and the line that turns it, 孰能濁以靜之徐清.

**Both commentators read the turn as a passage, not a fight.** 河上公: 誰能知水之濁，止而淨之，徐徐自清也, *"who can know the water's muddiness, stop it and let it be clean? Slowly, slowly it clears of itself."* 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) sets it inside a series: 夫晦以理物則得明，濁以静物則得清，安以動物則得生，此自然之道也, *"dim, to order things, and gain bright; muddy, to still things, and gain clear; at rest, to move things, and gain life. This is the way of things being so of themselves."* And he adds why the line asks *who can*: 孰能者言其難也，徐者詳慎也, *"'who can' speaks of how hard it is; 'slowly' means with care."*

**Clear is not where the chapter stops.** The next line runs the other way: 孰能安以動之徐生, *"Who can be at rest, and by moving it slowly come to life?"* The person ch 15 describes can let the stirred settle and stir the settled. The muddy state is no failure. It is one of the seven likenesses the chapter praises.

### 清 and 混

**混 (*hùn* — mixed) is not 清's opposite.** Ch 15 likens it to 濁, *"like muddy water,"* and 若 (*ruò* — like) keeps the two apart. 混 stands at the start of the chapter's turn and 清 at its end. 河上公 calls the Tao clear in ch 25, the chapter where it is 混成, *mixed and complete*: 道清淨不言, *"the Tao is clear and clean, and does not speak."* And across chapters, 混 is how three become one (14), and the one is what makes the sky clear (39). Clear is what mixed becomes when it is left still. See `glossary/hun-混.md`.

### Clear is not the goal: ch 39

天得一以清，地得一以寧, *"Sky received the one, and so became clear. Earth received the one, and so became settled."* And then the same lines turned round: 天無以清，將恐裂, *"Sky with no way to be clear is likely to split."*

**Both commentators refuse to let clear stand on its own.** 王弼: 用一以致清耳，非用清以清也。守一則清不失，用清則恐裂也, *"it is by using the one that it comes clear, not by using clearness to be clear. Hold to the one and the clearness is not lost; use the clearness, and it may split."* And further: 清不能為清…故清不足貴, *"clear cannot make itself clear… so clear is not worth prizing."* 河上公 goes further still: 天當有隂陽施張，晝夜更用，不可但欲清明無巳時，將恐分裂不為天, *"the sky must have yin and yang unfolding, day and night taking turns. It cannot want only to be clear and bright without end, or it may split apart and stop being sky."*

A sky that is only clear, with no night in it, breaks. The book prizes clear and will not let anyone grasp at it.

### Why "clear"

**It is the first English sense of the word.** Water you can see the bottom through, sky with nothing in it. It passes the pointing test: Laozi could point at a pool after the rain has stopped.

**One English word does all four lines.** *Come clear* (15), *became clear* (39), *to be clear* (39), *clear stillness* (45). The verb, the predicate and the modifier are all native English for this one word.

### What is set aside, and why

**"pure" and "purity"** are the overlay to refuse. 清淨 (*qīng jìng* — clear and clean) became, in later Daoist and Buddhist Chinese, the word for a monk's or a temple's purity, and English *purity* is moral and ritual. *"Purity and stillness are the standard of the world"* would sit comfortably in a sermon. And settled water has not been purified: nothing was removed from it.

**"clarity"** is 明's noun, in five lines (10, 27, 36, 52 twice). 明 (*míng*) is light, the moon through a window. 清 is water and air. 王弼's ch 15 comment keeps them on two axes, dim to bright and muddy to clear, and the English keeps them apart by keeping 清 off 明's noun. 明 and 清 share no chapter, so the build can hold this one.

**"settled"** is 說文's own picture, and it is 寧 (*níng*)'s, in the same chapter: 地得一以寧, *"Earth… became settled"* (39).

**"limpid"** is a poet's word, and **"lucid"** is a mind's. **"transparent"** is about glass.

**"still"** is 靜's.

### What no rule can enforce

**"settled" is ruled out for 清 and cannot be forbidden.** 寧 stands beside 清 in ch 39 and prints *settled* twice.

**"still" and "stillness" are ruled out and cannot be forbidden.** 靜 stands beside 清 in ch 15 and ch 45.

**Bare *clear* is also 明's, once.** 明's English is built on the same root, *clear-seeing*, *clarity*, *see clearly*, and at ch 41 it takes *clear* bare: 明道若昧, *"The clear Tao seems murky."* No rule can tell the two apart, since they never share a chapter. A reader holds it: bare *clear* is 清's in four lines and 明's in one.

### Left open

**Ch 41 reads like 清 and 濁, and the Chinese is 明 and 昧.** *"The clear Tao seems murky"* sets a clear thing against a murky one, and an English reader hears water. The Chinese pair is light: 明 against 昧 (*mèi* — dim). That is the distinction 王弼 draws at ch 15, 晦以理物則得明，濁以静物則得清, light on one axis and water on the other. The cost of keeping it is real, and so is the cost of removing it. *Bright*, the plain light word, is already worn by three characters: 皦 (*jiǎo*) at 14, 昭 (*zhāo*) at 20 and 光 (*guāng*) at 58. And 河上公 himself glosses 清 with 清明, *clear and bright*, so the two characters overlap in the commentary too. Ch 41 keeps *clear*.

**清淨 in the commentary.** 河上公 calls the Tao 清淨 at ch 25 and reaches for the word elsewhere. That is the compound that later became *purity*, and it is the commentator's, not the book's. The book's own pairing is 清靜, *clear stillness*, at ch 45.
