## 守 (Shǒu) — To Hold To

*Say "show," dipping. Eleven lines across eight chapters, and it is the verb the book reaches for when it tells you what to do.*

**Locked: 守 → "hold to" — and *guard* where what is kept is ground or property.** *(Never "embrace," never "attune to," never "stay with." Never *keep* either — but that one is a ruling no check can hold, and it is the likeliest wrong word here, so it has a section of its own below.)*

### The graph: a roof and a rule, and no hand anywhere

> 守，**守官**也。从宀从寸。寺府之事者。从寸。寸，**法度**也。
> *"守 is to keep an office. From 宀 (mián — a roof) and 寸 (cùn — an inch). It is the business of a court, hence 寸; 寸 means **a regulation**."* — 說文解字

This is the one member of the holding family with **no hand in it.** 持 (*chí* — to grip) has 手, the hand. 執 (*zhí* — to seize) has 丮, a hand, and a set of manacles. 保 (*bǎo* — to keep safe) has a person with a child. 守 has a building and a measure.

What you 守 is therefore not in your fist. **It is a post you have been given and are answerable for.** The English has to carry a charge, not a grip — which is why *hold to* takes a preposition where *hold* would not. You hold a cup. You hold to a post.

### Two registers, and the dictionary has both

說文 gives 守官 (*keeping a charge*) and 寺府之事 (*the business of a court*, which in a walled town includes the sentry at the gate). The book uses both, and the split runs by **what is being kept**:

| what is kept | reading | lines |
|---|---|---|
| a stance, a principle, a way of standing | **hold to** | 守中 (5) · 守靜 (16) · 守其雌 · 守其黑 · 守其辱 (28) · 守之 (32, 37) · 守其母 · 守柔 (52) |
| ground, property, a position under attack | **guard** | 金玉滿堂，莫之能守 (9) · 以守則固 (67) |

Nine of eleven are the first. The two that are not are the two where 守 has stopped being contemplative and gone back to the gate: a hall full of gold and jade that no one can guard, and 以戰則勝，以**守**則固 — *"fight with it and you win, guard with it and you stand firm"* — where 戰 (*zhàn* — to fight) and 守 are the plain military pair, attack and defence. 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) reads the second exactly that way, expanding the bare character into a compound: 以**守衛**則堅固, *"used to guard and defend, then solid and firm."*

### The instruction verb

守 is how the book says *do this*, and the shape recurs. Chapter 28 runs it three times as a template:

> 知其雄，**守**其雌 · 知其白，**守**其黑 · 知其榮，**守**其辱
> *"Know the rooster, hold to the hen · know the white, hold to the black · know the glory, hold to the disgrace."*

Knowing is the easy half. 守 is the half you have to keep doing, and the pairing with 知 (*zhī* — to know) is what makes the stanza a practice rather than an observation. Chapters 32 and 37 use one line verbatim — 侯王若能**守**之 — for the whole political programme, and chapter 52 uses it for the most consoling movement in the book: 既知其子，復**守**其母, *"know her children, return and hold fast to the mother."*

And at 52 the character carries the book's hardest paradox without breaking. 守柔曰強 — *"holding to the soft is called strong"* — draws 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) into a sentence that puts both valences of 強 (*qiáng* — strong) on one page:

> 守強不強，守柔乃強也。
> *"Holding to the strong is not strong; holding to the soft is truly strong."*

The verb is the same in both halves of his sentence. Only the object changes. That is the whole argument for one English.

### What is set aside

**"embrace" is 抱's** (*bào* — to embrace: chapters 10, 19, 22, 42, 54, 64), and the two characters are close enough that a Han commentator defines one by the other — 河上公 on ch 22's 抱一: **抱，守也**…聖人**守一**乃知萬事, *"抱 means to hold to… the sage holds to one and thereby knows the countless affairs."* That closeness is exactly why the English must keep them apart: 抱 takes the whole body and 守 takes a charge, and the two never share a chapter, so there is nothing to gain and a distinction to lose by letting them share a word.

**"attune to"** inverts both the register and the grammar. It makes 守 something that happens *to* you, a matter of resonance and sensitivity, when the character is a duty roster. You are not attuned to a post; you keep it.

**"stay with"** and **"abide in"** drop the effort. 守 costs something — that is why 王弼 can set 守強 against 守柔 and expect the reader to feel the difference — and a verb of dwelling has no cost in it.

**"preserve"** is a conservator's word, and it puts the emphasis on the object's condition rather than on the keeper's obligation. It also drifts toward 保 (*bǎo*), whose entry is `bao-保.md`.

### What no rule can enforce

***"keep"* is ruled out in the verse, and it is not on the `forbidden:` list, because it cannot be.** Those are two different facts and the difference matters: the ruling holds, and the enforcement does not exist. 保 stands with 守 in chapters 9 and 67, and **保's English is built on *keep* in both** — *keep safe* at 67, and **bare *keep* at 9**, where a declared flexion drops the qualifier (`bao-保.md`). A rule keyed to 守's presence would fire on 保's own line, and at chapter 9 it would fire on the exact word. So *embrace*, *attune to* and *stay with* are held by the build; *keep* is held only by this entry and by the reader. Likewise the two words a reader is most likely to confuse here sit two lines apart at chapter 9 — 守 at *guard*, 保 at *keep* — and again at chapter 67, where 保 takes *keep safe*. No check in this repository can tell them apart. See `bao-保.md`, `chi-持.md`, `zhi-執.md`.

### Left open

**Chapter 5's 守中.** 中 (*zhōng* — the middle) is rendered *the center*, and both commentators pull it toward emptiness rather than toward moderation — 王弼 reads 中 as the bellows' hollow two lines above. Whether the English should say *the center* or something that carries the hollow is unsettled, and 中 has no entry yet.
