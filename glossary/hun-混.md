## 混 (Hùn) — Mixed

*Say "hoon," falling. Three lines across three chapters, and every one of them describes the Tao or the people who kept it.*

**Locked: 混 → "mixed," and as a verb "mix."** *(Not "chaos" or "chaotic," not "undifferentiated," not "blend" or "merge." Not "muddy" either, which is 濁's, but that ruling no check can hold. See the last section but one.)*

The book never uses 混 for a mess. Three things that cannot be seen, heard or touched mix into one (14). The ancients who were masterful in service were mixed, like muddy water (15). Something mixed and complete was there before sky and earth (25). Each time, 混 is praised. It is how the Tao is, and how those who held to it were.

### The graph: a river in flood

> 混，**豐流也**。从水昆聲。
> *"混 is an abundant flow. From 水 (shuǐ — water), with 昆 (kūn) for the sound."* — 說文解字

A river in flood carries everything along with it: silt, leaves, branches, whatever it has picked up on the way. The mixing is what a full flow does, not a fault in it. 混 is a water word, and ch 15 makes that visible by setting it beside 濁 (*zhuó* — muddy water).

**Two other spellings of the same word stand in the witnesses.** Both Mawangdui silks write the bare sound element, 昆, at ch 25. 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*)'s text prints 渾 (*hún*) at ch 15, and 說文 defines 渾 by 混: 渾，**混流聲也**, *"渾 is the sound of a 混 flow."* Both are recorded in `sources/variants.yaml` as variants of spelling, not of meaning. 渾 also stands in our own base, once, at ch 49.

### What the book does with it

**Ch 14 — three become one.** 視之不見…聽之不聞…搏之不得…此三者不可致詰，故混而為一, *"Look, but you cannot see it… Listen, but you cannot hear it… Reach, but you cannot touch it… These three defy comprehension. Therefore, they mix into one."* 河上公 glosses the verb plainly: **混，合也**, *"混 means to join."* 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) gives the reason they cannot be pulled apart: 無狀無象，無聲無響, *"no shape, no image, no sound, no echo."* What has no edges cannot be kept separate.

**Ch 15 — the last of seven likenesses.** The ancients were careful, hesitant, dignified, loosening, solid, open, and 混兮其若濁, *"Mixed, like muddy water."* 王弼 on all seven: 凡此諸若，皆言其容象不可得而形名也, *"every one of these 'like's says that their bearing cannot be given a shape or a name."* The likeness is the point: 若 (*ruò* — like) holds 混 and 濁 apart even as it sets them side by side. 河上公 reads 濁 as a manner, not a state of the water: 濁者不照然也，與衆合同，不自尊, *"濁 is not shining; joining with the crowd, being the same as them, not holding oneself high."*

**Ch 25 — mixed, and already whole.** 有物混成，先天地生, *"Something mixed and complete, born before sky and earth."* 混成 is a paradox in two characters: undivided, and finished. 王弼: 混然不可得而知，而萬物由之以成，故曰混成也, *"mixed, it cannot be known, and yet the countless things are completed through it; so it says 混成."* 河上公: 謂道無形，混沌而成萬物, *"it means the Tao: formless, 混沌 (hùn dùn — mixed and unbroken), it completes the countless things."*

**What the three lines share is unknowability.** Mixed is why the three of ch 14 defy comprehension, why the ancients of ch 15 cannot be read, and why ch 25 goes on to say 吾不知其名, *"I do not know its name."* A thing whose parts cannot be told apart cannot be named. The book does not count that as a defect.

### Is 混 the opposite of 清?

**No. The book's opposite of 清 (*qīng* — clear) is 濁, and 混 stands beside 濁 rather than being it.** Ch 15 runs a single turn across two lines: 混兮其若濁。孰能濁以靜之徐清, *"Mixed, like muddy water. Who can be muddy, and by stilling it slowly come clear?"* 混 is likened to the muddy water, and the muddy water, left still, comes clear. 混 stands at the start of the turn and 清 at its end, but nothing in the book makes them rivals.

**The book praises both ends.** 混 is one of the seven likenesses for the ancients. And 河上公, glossing ch 25, the chapter of 混成, calls the same Tao clear: 道清淨不言, *"the Tao is clear and clean, and does not speak."* He holds it mixed and clear in one chapter.

**Two chapters put them in sequence.** 混 is how the three become one (14: 混而為一), and the one is what makes the sky clear (39: 天得一以清, *"Sky received the one, and so became clear"*). A pattern across chapters is a reason to look, not a ruling (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`), but it points the same way as ch 15: clear is what mixed becomes when it is left still, not what defeats it. See `glossary/qing-清.md`.

### What is set aside, and why

**"chaos" and "chaotic"** are the strongest pull, and 河上公's 混沌 (*hùn dùn*) invites them. They fail twice. Chaos is a cosmology, Hesiod's void before creation, and this book's beginning is not a void: it is full, a river in flood. And chaos is disorder, where 混 is abundance, and a full river is not disordered. `glossary/luan-亂.md` refuses *chaos* for 亂 (*luàn* — disorder) on its own grounds.

**"undifferentiated"** is a philosopher's word for a river's word, and it names an absence: *un-*, the differences missing. 豐流 names a presence, the flow full of everything. It also fails the pointing test. Laozi could point at a flooded river, not at the undifferentiated.

**"blend"** is something someone does on purpose, a cook's word or a tea merchant's. 混 happens of itself, the way a flood mixes. *Blend* also reads 沖 (*chōng*) at ch 42, which is under review on its own (`WORKLIST.md` T2-41).

**"merge"** fits ch 14, where three become one, and nothing else: at ch 15 and ch 25 there is nothing being merged into anything. It also loses the water.

**"muddy"** is 濁's. Ch 15 says 混 is *like* muddy water, not that it is muddy water.

### What no rule can enforce

**"muddy" is ruled out for 混 and cannot go on its `forbidden:` list.** 濁 stands beside 混 in ch 15 and prints *muddy* there twice.

### Left open

**渾 at ch 49.** 為天下渾其心 reads *"for the sake of the world, they let their heart go muddy."* 渾 is 混's own word, by 說文 and by 河上公's text of ch 15, but *muddy* is 濁's English. The chime with ch 15 is real in the Chinese, and it runs through 混, not through 濁. Whether ch 49 should carry *mix* rather than *muddy* is open (`WORKLIST.md` T2-63).

**The emperor without openings.** The 莊子 (*Zhuāngzǐ*) tells of 渾沌 (*Hùndùn*), emperor of the center, who had none of the seven openings everyone has for seeing, hearing, eating and breathing. The emperors of the two seas, repaying his kindness, bored one a day, and on the seventh day he died. It is the most famous story about this word, and it says exactly what this book's three lines say: what is whole and mixed is not improved by being opened up. It is also a later book's story, and this edition does not read it into the verse.
