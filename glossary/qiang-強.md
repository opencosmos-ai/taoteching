## 強 (Qiáng) — Strong

*Say "chyahng," rising. Twenty-one lines across twelve chapters, and the book makes it both the disease and the cure.*

**Locked: 強 → "strong" — *strengthen* where it takes an object, *forcing* where it strains against a thing's nature.** *(Not "mighty," not "forceful," not "brute strength," not "perseverance." 力 (lì) → "force.")*

Chapter 78 forces four words apart — 柔 (*róu* — soft) · 剛 (*gāng* — unbending) · 弱 (*ruò* — yielding) · 強 — and 強 is the one that carries opposite verdicts inside a single frame.

The book's sharpest joke depends on it, and any English that resolves the word makes the joke invisible.

Two chapters use the identical three-character frame **X 曰強** (*yuē qiáng* — "is called 強"):

> 守柔曰強
> *ch 52 — "holding to the soft is called 強"*

> 心使氣曰強
> *ch 55 — "the heart driving the energy is called 強"*

The first is praise. The second is a warning, and the line after it is 物壯則老 (*wù zhuàng zé lǎo* — "things at their prime grow old"). **One word, one frame, opposite verdicts.** A translation that renders the first *strength* and the second *forcing* has answered a question the text deliberately leaves standing — and has spent two English words to hide the fact that Laozi used one.

### The graph, and it is the same instrument as 弱

The character now written 強 is not the original word for strength. Two graphs are involved and the *Shuowen Jiezi* (c. 100 CE) separates them cleanly.

**彊** (*qiáng*) — 弓有力也，从弓畺聲: *"a bow having force"*; 弓 (*gōng* — a bow) with 畺 as phonetic. The image is a stiff bow, one that takes strength to draw.

**強** (*qiáng*) — 蚚也，从虫弘聲: *"the 蚚"*, a weevil in stored grain; 虫 (*chóng* — insect) with 弘 (*hóng*) as phonetic. From the Qin onward this borrowed graph displaced 彊 in the sense of strength, and it is the form our recension carries throughout: 彊 appears nowhere in the base text.

So two things follow, and the second is the one that matters.

**The word we read is a grain weevil borrowed for its sound.** The strength is a phonetic accident, exactly as 萬 (*wàn* — a scorpion) is an accident inside 萬物. Two of this book's load-bearing terms are insects conscripted by rhyme.

**And the word underneath it is a bow.** 弱 is two 弓 (bows) under 彡 (fine strokes) — a bow that gives. 彊 is 弓 at the limit of what it will take. **They are not opposites in kind. They are one instrument in two states**, and every image the book builds on the pair is a bow image:

> 木強則折
> *ch 76 — "a tree that is 強 snaps"*

That is a bow drawn past its give. Ch 76's whole botany is the same figure: 王弼 glosses 強大處下 as 木之本也 (*"this is the trunk"*) and 柔弱處上 as 枝條是也 (*"these are the branches"*). The trunk cannot bend and is nearer the ground; the branches bend and are nearer the light.

### The commentators name the quartet outright

河上公, glossing ch 42's 人之所教，我亦教之 (*"what others teach, I also teach"*), states both curricula in fourteen characters:

> 謂衆人所以教去弱為強，去柔為剛
> *"what the crowd teaches: leave 弱 for 強, leave 柔 for 剛"*

> 言我教衆人使去強為弱，去剛為柔
> *"I teach them: leave 強 for 弱, leave 剛 for 柔"*

A Han commentator, using all four characters, in two matched lines, treating them as a fixed set. This is the strongest evidence in the repository that 柔 · 剛 · 弱 · 強 must take four distinct and stable English words.

**And 王弼 states the paradox directly.** On ch 52's 守柔曰強:

> 守強不強，守柔乃強也
> *"holding to 強 is not 強; holding to the soft is truly 強"*

強 twice in seven characters, with opposite valence, from the fourth-century commentator the recension is named for. Whatever English 強 takes has to survive being said twice in one breath and meaning something different each time. **Strong survives it. Forcing does not, and neither does true strength.**

### Where it flexes, and the flexions are grammatical

The split is marked in the Chinese, so it can be checked rather than argued.

**Transitive, with an object — *strengthen*.** Two lines, and they are the same two lines where 弱 is licensed to read *weaken*:

- **ch 3** 弱其志，強其骨 — *weaken their ambition, strengthen their bones*
- **ch 36** 將欲弱之，必固強之 — *wanting to weaken it, first strengthen it*

The symmetry is not a coincidence: it is one grammar, so it is one exception, taken twice. A stance word cannot take an object.

**Straining against a thing's nature — *forcing*.** Where 強 modifies a verb of naming or describing, it is the strain of the act, not a quality of anything:

- **ch 15** 強為之容 — *forcing a description of them*
- **ch 25** 強字之曰道，強為之名曰大 — *forcing a style on it, I call it the Tao; forcing a name, I call it the vast*

The tradition heard this as a separate word. 河上公's text at ch 25 carries the phonetic note 強其文反, a *fanqie* spelling giving the third-tone reading *qiǎng* — "to force, to compel" — rather than *qiáng*. (These phonetic notes are later editorial additions to the Heshang Gong text, not the Han commentary itself; they record how the line was read, not what its first reader wrote.)

**Coercing something that is not yours — *force / dominance*.** Ch 30 uses 強 three times against the world and against people, where *strengthen* would be simply wrong: you do not strengthen a country by invading it.

- 不以兵強天下 — *does not use arms to force the world*
- 不敢以取強 — 河上公: 不以果敢取強大之名也, *"does not use boldness to seize the name of 強大"*
- 果而勿強 — 河上公: 果敢勿以為強兵堅甲以侵凌人也, *"succeed, but do not make strong troops and hard armor to trample people"*

**Everywhere else — *strong*.** Chapters 29, 33, 36 (柔弱勝剛強), 42, 52, 55, 76, 78.

### 力 is the plain word, and ch 33 has the pair inverted

強 cannot be settled without 力 (*lì* — a plough or an arm, the ordinary word for physical force), because ch 33 sets them against each other:

> 勝人者有力，自勝者強
> *"those who overcome others have 力; those who overcome themselves are 強"*

王弼: 勝人者有力而已矣，未若自勝者無物以損其力 — *"one who overcomes others merely has 力; unlike one who overcomes themselves, whose force nothing can diminish."* 河上公: 能勝人者不過以威力也 — *"one who can overcome others does so by no more than 威力"* (awe and force).

The couplet is **plain force against achieved strength**, and it rhymes with the line above it, where 智 (*zhì* — cleverness) is the lesser term against 明 (*míng* — clear-seeing). 力 is what you have. 強 is what you become.

**力 takes *force*** — which ch 68 already does (用人之力, *"using people's force"*) — and 強 takes *strong*. Neither needs an adjective; the couplet supplies the comparison.

### Never

**mighty · forceful · brute strength.** All three pre-judge. 強 is the word the book uses for the state it warns against *and* the state it recommends, and an English that carries a verdict cannot do both.

**perseverance** for 強行 (ch 33). 王弼 glosses it 勤能行之，其志必獲 — *"diligently able to practise it, their will surely attains"* — so persistence is the sense. But *perseverance* is a virtue-word that leaves no trace of 強, and it hides the fact that the chapter has just spent two lines distinguishing 力 from 強. Whatever the line takes, the character has to be audible in it.

**And do not let *strong* or *strength* drift onto its neighbours.** Two characters have borrowed 強's English in chapters that contain 強 — 固 (*gù* — firm) at ch 55's 握固, and 壯 (*zhuàng* — in its prime) at 物壯則老 in ch 30 and ch 55. A lock is a claim in both directions, and no checker rule can catch these: 固 and 壯 sit in the same chapters as 強, so the evidence gate that makes the tool trustworthy is exactly what blinds it here. This is a reader's job.

### What stays open

**強 is both the disease and the cure, and nothing in the text resolves it.** It is rigor mortis at ch 76, an army that cannot win at ch 76 and ch 30, a roof-beam at ch 42, the heart bullying the breath at ch 55 — and it is also what you are when you overcome yourself at ch 33 and when you hold to the soft at ch 52.

The temptation is to read the good instances as a different word, or as irony. 王弼 refuses that escape: 守強不強，守柔乃強也 puts both on the page at once and lets them stand. The English should be able to say the same sentence.

What is genuinely unsettled is **ch 55's 心使氣曰強**, the one line where the reader most needs help and gets least. Rendered *"the heart driving the energy is called strong,"* it can be misheard as approval for exactly one beat — until 物壯則老 lands in the next line. Whether that beat is a flaw or the point is not a question the Chinese answers.
