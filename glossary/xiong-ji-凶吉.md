## 凶 & 吉 (Xiōng, Jí) — Ominous & Auspicious

*Say "shyoong," level, and "jee," rising. 凶 stands three times in three chapters, 吉 once, beside it. The diviner's two words: what the sign says about the way ahead.*

**Locked: 凶 → "ominous" · 吉 → "auspicious."** *(Locked 2026-11-19, Shalom's call, after the chapter pass.)* *Lean* for 凶年 (*xiōng nián* — a year of failed harvest) at chapter 30, and *inauspicious* for 凶事 (*xiōng shì* — the rites of mourning) at chapter 31, where both compounds have hardened. *(Not "disaster," which is 禍 (huò)'s. Not "evil," a moral verdict on an outcome. Not "unlucky," which makes chance of what follows from conduct.)* The I Ching renders the pair the same way (Shalom's calls, 2026-10-20), and this entry binds only the Tao Te Ching, because its list must forbid a word the I Ching needs.

### The graph: a pit, and a mouth that speaks well

> 凶，惡也。象地穿交陷其中也。
> *"凶: bad. A picture of the ground broken open, with something crossed fallen into it."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

> 吉，善也。从士、口。
> *"吉: good. From 士 (shì — a man of service) and 口 (kǒu — a mouth)."* — Shuowen

凶 is a picture: a pit in the ground, and something caught in it. 吉 is a compound the dictionary only names. **Their home is the altar**, which the Shuowen defines through them: 示，天垂象，見吉凶，所以示人也 (*"示: the sky hangs down images and shows the auspicious and the ominous; that is how it shows people"*). 吉 and 凶 are what the sign says. What then befalls is 福 and 禍 (*fú / huò* — fortune / disaster), and this glossary keeps the two pairs apart, as the Shuowen does (`glossary/huo-fu-禍福.md`).

**Where the picture stops.** The book never divines. It uses 凶 as a verdict on conduct (16), as a hardened word for a hungry year (30), and as a rite (31), and 吉 only in the rite. No line consults an oracle.

### Three lines, and no oracle in any of them

> *Knowing the ever-present is called clear-seeing.*
> *Not knowing the ever-present, reckless action is ominous.*
>
> 知常曰明。
> 不知常，妄作凶。
>
> — chapter 16

> *Where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow.*
> *After a big military campaign, a lean year follows.*
>
> 師之所處，荊棘生焉。
> 大軍之後，必有凶年。
>
> — chapter 30

> *Auspicious occasions honor the left; inauspicious occasions honor the right.*
>
> 吉事尚左，凶事尚右。
>
> — chapter 31

**In each line 凶 follows from what people do**: acting without knowing the ever-present, marching an army over the fields, killing. Wang Bi reads chapter 16 as a fall out of the whole, 失此以往則邪入乎分 (*"lose this and go on, and the warped enters, divided"*); Heshang Gong as the loss of the spirit's clarity, 妄作巧詐則失神明，故凶也 (*"reckless craft and cunning lose the spirit's clarity, and so it is ominous"*). Neither reads chance, and neither reads a god's displeasure.

### 凶年 is a famine, and 凶事 is a funeral

**Heshang Gong reads chapter 30's 凶年 as crops failing**: 天應之以惡氣，即害五穀，五穀盡傷人也 (*"the sky answers with foul air, which harms the five grains; when the grains are gone, people are harmed"*). The compound names the year itself, a year with nothing to eat, not a sign of one. *Ominous year* would make it a portent. *A lean year* is the English for the thing.

**Chapter 31's 吉事 / 凶事 are the Zhou ritual categories**, 吉禮 (*jí lǐ* — the auspicious rites) and 凶禮 (*xiōng lǐ* — the rites of mourning), and the line seats generals by them; the chapter says so itself, 言以喪禮處之 (*"which is to say they are placed by the rites of mourning"*). Heshang Gong: 左生位也 … 陰道殺人 (*"the left is the place of life … the way of yin is killing"*). Here the pair is named as a pair, *auspicious* and *inauspicious*, and the English keeps the ritual's own matched names. *Ominous occasions* would also sit two lines below 不祥之器 (*bù xiáng zhī qì* — "tools of ill omen") and hand 凶 the root of 祥 (*xiáng* — omen)'s English, in a chapter that holds both.

### Why 凶 is not *disaster*

***Disaster* is 禍's**, at chapters 46, 58 and 69, and `glossary/huo-fu-禍福.md` rules it 禍's alone. The two pairs work on different axes: 禍 is the harm that befalls, 凶 the verdict on the way ahead. The I Ching keeps *disaster* for 災 (*zāi*), which stands in the same lines as its 凶 (24.6, 62.6), and rules *disaster* out for 凶 for that reason (`iching/glossary/凶-xiong.md`). Since 2026-11-19, when chapters 16 and 30 stopped printing it, *disaster* is on this entry's `forbidden:` list: none of 凶's chapters holds 禍, so the gate holds the ruling.

### What is set aside

**"Disaster," "calamity," "misfortune."** Outcome nouns, and each belongs to another character: *disaster* to 禍, *calamity* to 殃 (*yāng*). 凶 is the verdict, not the harm.

**"Evil."** Legge's word for the I Ching's 凶, and a moral verdict where the Chinese has an outcome. It fails the hymn test.

**"Unlucky," "lucky."** Chance, where the book and the I Ching's *Xici* derive 吉 and 凶 from conduct.

**"Ill omen" for 凶.** It is 不祥 (*bù xiáng*)'s English at chapters 31, 55 and 78.

### What no rule can enforce

***Ominous* beside *ill omen*** at chapter 31 is the reason for the flexion there; no rule can see a shared English root.

### Left open

**The I Ching does not inherit this entry.** It vendors this glossary's locks as its own and has its own 凶 and 吉 entries, with the same renderings. This entry's `forbidden:` list carries *disaster*, which the I Ching needs for 災, so it binds the Tao Te Ching only. The two books agree on the words and differ on the list.

**Whether 凶 is a sign or an outcome in this book.** The I Ching's 凶 is a verdict read off a figure. The Laozi's 凶 at chapter 16 is a verdict too, but nobody reads it off anything: it is simply what reckless action comes to. *Ominous* keeps the diviner's register in a book that never divines, and that is a cost this entry accepts for one word across two books.
