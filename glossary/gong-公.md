## 公 (Gōng) — Impartiality

*Say "gong," first tone, level and held. A word English splits into two that have nothing to do with each other: a virtue and a peerage.*

**Locked: 公 → "impartiality" where it names the quality; "lord" or "minister" where it names the office.** *(Never "duke." Reasoning below.)*

Open almost any English Tao Te Ching at Chapter 42 and you will find *dukes*. Open it at Chapter 16 and you will find *fairness*, or *equanimity*, or *the impartial*. Nothing tells the reader these are one word. So the fact the Chinese states plainly — **the office is named after the virtue** — disappears, and with it the quiet judgment the text passes every time it uses the title.

### The graph: a negation, and that is the whole of it

公 is two pieces:

- **八** (*bā*) — two strokes turning away from each other. It is the numeral eight by sound-loan, but the picture is *dividing*, and by extension **turning one's back on**.
- **厶** (*sī*) — a small self-enclosing curl. It is the original form of **私** (*sī* — private, one's own).

So the graph is *turning your back on the self-enclosing*. The *Shuowen Jiezi* (~100 CE) glosses it 公，平分也 — **"公 is fair division"** — and then, unusually, names a source: 韓非曰：背厶爲公, *"Han Feizi says: turning your back on 厶 is 公."*

Han Feizi's own sentence, from 五蠹 (*Wǔ dù* — "The Five Vermin"), gives the full form:

> 古者蒼頡之作書也，自環者謂之**私**，背私謂之**公**。
> *"When Cangjie made writing in ancient times, what encircles itself he called **私**, the private; what turns its back on 私 he called **公**."*

**That 公 is a negation and not a positive quality is the finding.** English *impartiality*, *fairness*, *justice* all sound like attainments — things you build up. 公 is what is left when the curl uncurls. You do not acquire it; you stop doing the other thing.

### Why that matters at Chapter 16

Ch 16 puts 公 on a chain where each line begins with the word the last one ended on:

> 知常**容**，**容**乃**公**，**公**乃**王**，**王**乃**天**，**天**乃**道**，**道**乃久
> *knowing the ever-present is capaciousness; capaciousness is impartiality; impartiality is sovereignty; sovereignty is the open sky; the open sky is the Tao; the Tao endures.*

Every rung widens the circle beyond the self, and **公 is where the widening leaves the person.** 容 (*róng* — capaciousness) is still an inner quality, something you have. 公 is the first rung that is not about you at all, because it is defined as facing away from you. Read as *equanimity*, rung two collapses back inside the person and the ascent stalls — which is exactly why that rendering was set aside.

### The counter-term is in the book, twice

Laozi does not use 公 and 私 in one line, but he uses 私 where it counts, and it is the same negation running the other way.

> Ch 7 · 天地所以能長且久者，以其不自生 … 非以其**無私**邪？故能成其**私**。
> *"Sky and earth endure because they do not live for themselves … Is it not because they have no private self? And so they can fulfil their own."*

That is 公 stated as a paradox: the self is completed by facing away from it. And Ch 19 gives the instruction in four characters — 少**私**寡欲, *"reduce the private, diminish desire"* — where 私 sits beside 欲 (*yù* — desire) as one of the two things to thin out.

### The office, and the judgment hidden in it

Chinese titles are often meaning-transparent in a way English peerage is not. English has one good parallel: *justice* is both the virtue and the person who holds the office — Justice Roberts. Nobody hears *fair division* in **duke**.

That transparency does work in Chapter 42:

> 人之所惡，唯孤、寡、不穀，而**王公**以為稱。
> *"What people most hate is to be called orphaned, meagre, unworthy — yet kings and lords take these very words as their titles."*

The chapter is about rulers naming themselves with words of **lack**. A title that means *turned away from the private* belongs in that sentence, and the joke is sharper for it: the rank itself already says *not for yourself*, and the chapter is telling rulers to live up to their own letterhead.

And in Chapter 62 the manuscripts hand us an argument. Where the received text reads 三**公**, the Mawangdui silks write 三**鄉**, which editors read as a loan for 三**卿** (*sān qīng* — the three high ministers). **The oldest witness names the office with the plain word for a minister of state** — not with a rank. *Minister* is therefore not a modernization; it is the older reading's own word.

### What is set aside

**"Duke."** Rejected outright. It imports the European feudal ladder — duke, marquess, earl — an entire social order the Zhou did not have and did not resemble, and it severs the office from its meaning. A reader who meets *duke* in 42 and *impartiality* in 16 has no way to know they read the same character.

**"Equanimity."** Inner calm. It puts rung two of Ch 16's ladder back inside the person, halting a climb whose whole motion is outward. 公 is a direction of facing, not a state of nerves.

**"Justice."** A legal and moral frame, concerned with **desert** — what a person is owed. 公 is concerned with **whose interest** — it asks who you are facing, not who deserves what. The distinction is the difference between a courtroom and a horizon.

**"The public"** as a noun. A modern civic abstraction with a nineteenth-century shape, and it makes 公 a body of people rather than a way of standing.

*"Fairness"* is not forbidden, and it is not wrong — only thinner. Fairness is a **procedure**; 公 is a **direction**. You can execute a fair procedure while facing entirely inward.

### Working register

| The grammar | Where | Render |
|---|---|---|
| the quality, as a noun in the ladder | 16 (容乃公) | **impartiality** |
| the quality, as an adjective | — | **impartial** |
| the office, compounded with 王 | 42 (王公) | **lords** |
| the office, the three highest of state | 62 (三公) | **the three ministers** |

### Left open

**The two senses cannot take one English word, and this entry does not force them to.** Every available rendering for the office severs it from the root, so a reader of Chapter 62 will not hear what a reader of Chapter 16 hears. That is a real loss, and the glossary can only partly repair it: the entry can say *the office is named after the virtue*, but the verse cannot.

Whether that is worth a translator's note in the finished edition, or whether the loss should simply be borne, is not settled here.

**And 私 has its own drift.** It reads *"selfish"* at Ch 7 and *"the self"* at Ch 19 — and Chapter 7's paradox, 故能成其私, *"and so they can fulfil their own,"* has vanished from the English altogether, which takes the sting out of a line built to sting. 私 may want an entry of its own rather than shelter under this one.
