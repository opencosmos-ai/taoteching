## 精 (Jīng) — Vital Essence

*Say "jing," level and light. Only three appearances, in two chapters — and one of the smallest footprints in this glossary hides one of its clearest inversions.*

**Locked: 精 → "vital essence" / "essence."** *(Not "primordial mass," not "soul," not "spirit," not "energy.")*

Chapter 21 rendered 其中有精 as *"within its dark depths lies the primordial mass of creation."* That is not a loose translation; it is the **precise opposite** of the character. 精 is what remains when the mass is taken **away**.

### The character, read slowly — hulled rice

精 is 米 (*mǐ* — rice) beside 青 (*qīng*), which supplies the sound and lends a sense of *fresh, pure*. The *Shuowen Jiezi* (~100 CE) glosses it in a single word: **擇也** — *to select, to sift.*

The picture is **grain that has been hulled and winnowed**: the husk beaten off, the chaff blown away, the broken and the empty discarded, and what is left is the small heavy edible heart of the thing. 精 is the *result of subtraction*. Everything bulky has been removed.

That is why "primordial mass" fails so completely. **Mass is exactly what 精 is not** — mass is the husk and the chaff, the undifferentiated bulk that sifting removes. The rendering also imports Big Bang cosmology, a different overlay from the mechanistic one but the same family of anachronism, and it fails the pointing test flatly: Laozi could point at a bowl of polished rice. He could not point at a primordial mass.

Every good English sense of the word follows from the winnowing: *refined*, *concentrated*, *choice*, *precise*, *potent*. In modern Chinese 精 still carries all of them, and 精米 is still polished rice.

### The evidence — both chapters are about undiminished potency

**Chapter 55, the newborn.** 未知牝牡之合而朘作，精之至也 — *not yet knowing the union of female and male, yet quickening with life; this is 精 at its utmost.* The infant has the most 精 of anyone in the book, and has spent none of it. Nothing has been dissipated, nothing performed, nothing sought. The concentration *is* the vitality.

The same chapter then draws the line that keeps 精 from meaning merely "energy": 心使氣曰強 — *when the heart drives the 氣, that is forcing.* **氣** (*qì*) is breath-energy in motion, and it can be driven, pushed, mismanaged. 精 cannot be driven; it can only be kept or squandered. One is a current, the other a reserve. A translation that renders both as "energy" loses the whole distinction Chapter 55 is built on — and it is the distinction that makes 益生曰祥 (*to force life to increase is an ill omen*) legible.

**Chapter 21, the source.** 窈兮冥兮，其中有精；其精甚真，其中有信 — *deep and dim, and within it there is 精; its 精 is utterly real, and within it there is trust.* Here the sifting image does its cosmological work: at the bottom of the indistinct there is not a mass but a **concentrate** — the small dense real thing left when everything vague has been winnowed off. The chapter's whole descent is a sifting: through 象 (*xiàng* — image), 物 (*wù* — thing), 精, 信 (*xìn* — trust), each finer than the last.

### The lock: what is set aside, and why

**"Primordial mass"** inverts the character, as above, and dates the text to the twentieth century.

**"Soul"** is on the overlay watchlist for exactly this word. Chinese has a vocabulary for souls — 魂 (*hún*, the ethereal soul) and 魄 (*pò*, the corporeal one) — and Laozi never uses 魂 and uses 魄 once, at chapter 10, for the body's own **aliveness** (`po-魄.md`). Rendering 精 as "soul" imports a separable immortal self into a book with no such thing, and makes Chapter 55's newborn a vessel for something rather than simply a creature brimming.

**"Spirit"** carries the same freight and collides with 神 (*shén* — numinous potency), a different character that the text does use (6, 29, 39, 60).

**"Energy"** collapses 精 into 氣, as Chapter 55 forbids. It is also the vaguest word available in English for the most precise word in the family.

> **Why "energy" is not on the `forbidden` list.** It cannot be. Chapter 55 contains **both** 精 and 氣, and 氣 legitimately takes *vital energy* in 心使氣曰強 (*when the heart drives the vital energy, that is forcing*) — so forbidding the word for 精 fires a false error on a correct line. `tools/check_locks.py` gates on whether a character is present in the chapter, which cannot express *right for that character, wrong for this one, same chapter.* The rule this implies is general: **a forbidden rendering must be a word no other character in those same chapters can legitimately claim.** The same limit applies to 德 and 信 (see `glossary/xin-信.md`), and this distinction is held by the reader of the entry rather than by the tool.

**"Semen"** deserves naming, because it is a real reading and not a foolish one: in later Daoist and medical literature 精 unambiguously means seminal essence, and Chapter 55's line about the infant's arousal invites it. We decline it for two reasons. The specialized sense is **later** than this text, and taking it here would make the passage a claim about one organ in one sex, where the point is a whole small body full of unspent life. The male-default is the text's, not the argument's. *(See the Chapter 55 manuscript note on 朘 / 全, where the same question arises in the source text itself.)*

### The working register

精 barely flexes — three occurrences, two grammatical positions:

- as the thing present in something → **vital essence** (其中有精 → "within it there is vital essence")
- when already named and referred back to → **essence** (其精甚真 → "its essence is utterly real")
- at its extreme → **vital essence at its utmost** (精之至也)

**Never** "primordial mass," "soul," "spirit," "energy," "life force."

### The tension, left open

**Is it one word here, or two senses?** Chapter 55's 精 is unmistakably **bodily** — an infant's undissipated vitality, sitting in a line about genitals and grip strength. Chapter 21's 精 is **cosmological** — what is found at the bottom of the indistinct, before anything has a name. English "vital essence" covers both, which is convenient and may be a cheat.

The winnowing image is what argues they are one word: in both places 精 is *the concentrate left after everything loose is removed*, whether the sifting is done to grain, to a body, or to the visible world. But it is worth admitting that Laozi may simply be using a household word for *the good part* in two unrelated ways, and that our unified reading is tidier than his usage.

We leave it single, and note the seam here rather than smoothing it in the verse.
