## 名 (Míng) — Name

*Say "ming," rising. Twenty-four times in twenty-two lines, across ten chapters. The word the book opens its second line with, and the one it keeps setting against the Tao.*

**Locked: 名 → "name," in every line: a noun, and a verb where it takes an object.** 無名 and 有名 are *nameless* and *named*, and 可名 and 不可名 are *can be named* and *cannot be named*. *(Not "reputation" or "fame" in chapter 44, which spend a second English on one word and hide the chapter's link to chapter 32. Not "description" or "designation," which turn a spoken name into an account of a thing. Not "be called," which is 謂's (wèi — "is called"); see* What no rule can enforce.*)*

### The graph: a mouth calling out at dusk

> 名，自命也。从口从夕。夕者，冥也。冥不相見，故以口自名。
> *"Name: to name oneself. From 口 (kǒu — mouth) and 夕 (xī — dusk). 夕 means 冥 (míng — the dark). In the dark people cannot see one another, so they name themselves with the mouth."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

**The picture is someone calling out who they are, after dark.** The oracle bones already write 名 with the same two parts: 夕, a crescent moon, beside 口, a mouth. Where the two sit varies in the oracle-bone graphs and settles in the bronzes, but the parts never change. Xu Shen reads them as a scene: two people who cannot see each other at dusk, and a voice saying *it's me*.

**So in the oldest account of the word, a name is what you reach for when sight fails.** That is not a gloss laid over the book. It is what the book does with the word, twice, in so many characters (below).

**Where the picture stops.** The Shuowen's 自命 (*zì mìng* — "to name oneself") is a person naming themselves. In this book nobody does: things are named, and the Tao is named by force. The reflexive scene is the graph's, not the book's.

### Twenty-two lines: a name is given where sight fails, and forced on the Tao

> *The name that can be named is not the ever-present name.*
> *The nameless is the beginning of sky and earth.*
> *The named is the mother of the countless things.*
>
> 名可名，非常名。
> 無名，天地之始；
> 有名，萬物之母。
>
> — chapter 1

> *Look, but you cannot see it — its name is the invisible.*
>
> 視之不見，名曰夷；
>
> — chapter 14

> *I do not know its name.*
> *Forcing a label on it, I call it the Tao.*
> *Forcing a name on it, I call it great.*
>
> 吾不知其名，
> 強字之曰道，
> 強為之名曰大。
>
> — chapter 25

> *When the carving begins, there are names.*
> *Since names already exist, one must know when to stop.*
>
> 始制有名，
> 名亦既有，夫亦將知止，
>
> — chapter 32

> *Therefore, the sage knows without venturing,*
> *names without seeing,*
>
> 是以聖人不行而知，
> 不見而名，
>
> — chapter 47

**In these lines a name stands where seeing cannot, or where the Tao will not be held.** Chapter 14 is the Shuowen's scene, word for word: you look and cannot see, so a name is given (視之不見，名曰夷, three times over, for sight, hearing and touch). Chapter 47 prints the definition as an instruction, 不見而名, *"names without seeing."* Chapter 25 says what it costs: the name has to be forced (強, *qiǎng* — "to force"). 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) gives the reason in four characters, 名以定形, *"a name fixes a form,"* and the mixed-and-complete has no form to fix.

**The Tao is named three ways in the book, and each is an admission.** It is 無名 (*wú míng* — "nameless," 1, 32, 37, 41), it 不可名 (*bù kě míng* — "cannot be named," 14), and when it is named at all the name is forced (25). Chapter 21 then says the opposite of all three in one line, 自古及今，其名不去, *"From ancient times until today, the Tao's name has never been erased,"* and reads the beginning of things *in* that name. The book never reconciles the two, and the English does not either.

### Names and knowing when to stop: chapters 32 and 44

> *Since names already exist, one must know when to stop.*
> *Knowing when to stop, you come to no danger.*
>
> 名亦既有，夫亦將知止，
> 知止可以不殆。
>
> — chapter 32

> *Your name or your body — which is dearer?*
>
> 名與身孰親？
>
> — chapter 44

**Two chapters put names beside knowing when to stop, and they close on the same words.** Chapter 32 reaches 知止可以不殆 from names; chapter 44 opens with a name weighed against the body and ends 知止不殆, *"Know when to stop, and you come to no danger."* In chapter 32 the names are the ones carving makes, the uncarved cut into vessels with functions. In chapter 44 the name is the one a person makes. **They are the same word, and the book treats them as the same danger**: a name is where you stop, or fail to.

**This is why chapter 44 reads *name*.** English *name* carries the person's sense natively, *a good name*, *to make a name for yourself*, and so it can hold chapter 44 without leaving chapter 1. The commentators keep the one word as well. 王弼 on chapter 44: 尚名好高其身必疏, *"whoever exalts a name and loves height, their body must grow distant."* 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*): 名遂則身退也, *"when the name is achieved, the body withdraws."*

### What the commentators did with chapter 1: the same word, two readings

At the book's second line the two commentators read 名 differently, and the difference is the one between chapters 32 and 44.

- **王弼:** 可道之道，可名之名，指事造形，非其常也, *"the Tao that can be made a way and the name that can be named point at affairs and make forms; they are not the ever-present."* A name is a cut, as it is in chapter 32.
- **河上公:** 名可名：謂富貴尊榮高世之名也, *"the name that can be named means the name of wealth, rank, honor and glory that stands high in the world."* A name is a standing, as it is in chapter 44.

**The English keeps both open by keeping one word.** Had chapter 44 read *reputation*, chapter 1 would have had to choose between them, or a reader would never learn there was a choice.

### 名 as a verb: chapters 34 and 47

Twice 名 takes an object and is a verb, and it is *name* both times.

> *The work is done, and it does not name it its own.*
>
> 功成而不名有。
>
> — chapter 34

**河上公 reads the line with the word itself:** 有道不名其有功也, *"having the Tao, it does not name the merit as its own."* The opening, *the work is done*, is the one 功成 (*gōng chéng*) takes in chapters 2 and 77, so the three lines open alike, as they do in the Chinese.

Chapter 47's 不見而名 is the received reading, against silk A's 明 (*míng* — "clear," the same sound), and both commentators print 名: 王弼, 雖不見而是非之理可得而名也, *"though it does not see, the principle of right and wrong can be got and named"* (`sources/variants.yaml`).

### What is set aside

- **"Reputation," "fame," "renown."** The conventional English for chapter 44 (Legge: *"Or fame or life, which do you hold more dear?"*). Each is a true sense of 名, and each is a second English for one word, which hides what the chapter shares with chapter 32 and what chapter 1's first line may already mean (河上公). *Fame* adds a size the line does not have: chapter 44 weighs any name against the body, not a famous one.
- **"Description."** A description is an account of what a thing is like. A 名 is a word spoken for it, and in chapter 25 the whole argument is that no account is possible, which is why the name has to be forced. *Description* there makes the line say the Tao was described.
- **"Designation."** Exact for 王弼's 稱 (*chēng* — "designation"), which he uses of 字 (*zì*) in chapter 25, and bureaucratic. It fails the pointing test: you can point at someone calling out in the dark.
- **"The Name," capitalized.** The divine-name overlay, which turns chapter 1 into a sermon on an ineffable Name. Lowercase, as everything but the Tao.
- **"True name."** Chapter 25's 吾不知其名 is *I do not know its name*. A *true* name invents one existing somewhere out of reach, and turns a claim about formlessness into one about the speaker.

### Working register

| Form | Lines | English | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| a noun | 1, 14, 21, 25, 32, 44 | **name** | 吾不知其名 → *"I do not know its name"* (25) |
| 名曰 X | 14 ×3 | **its name is** | 名曰夷 → *"its name is the invisible"* |
| 異名 | 1 | **differ in name** | 同出而異名 → *"emerge together, but differ in name"* |
| 無名 / 有名 | 1, 32, 37, 41 | **nameless / named** (*names* where 有 is "there are") | 道常無名 → *"the Tao is ever nameless"* (32); 始制有名 → *"there are names"* (32) |
| 可名 / 不可名 | 1, 14, 34 | **can be named / cannot be named** | 可名為大 → *"it can be named great"* (34) |
| a verb, with an object | 34, 47 | **name** | 不見而名 → *"names without seeing"* (47) |

The split is grammatical, not by chapter, so it stays in prose and nothing goes in `flexions:`. **可 + 名 is always passive** (`ke-可.md`): the Tao is what gets named, never what names.

### What no rule can enforce

- **"Be called" belongs to 謂 and 曰 (*yuē* — "to call"), not to 名.** 可名 must read *can be named*, never *can be called*, but *called* cannot go on the forbidden list: 謂 stands in chapters 1 and 14 (同謂之玄, 是謂), and 曰 in chapter 25 (*"I call it great"*), all three of them 名 chapters. A reader, and `concordance.py --english "called"`, are the only check.
- **"Label" belongs to 字 (*zì* — "a courtesy name"), once, in chapter 25.** 王弼 keeps the two apart, 名以定形，字以稱可言, *"a 名 fixes a form; a 字 designates what can be spoken,"* and so does the English: *"Forcing a label on it, I call it the Tao. / Forcing a name on it, I call it great."* *Label* cannot be forbidden here, because chapter 25 holds both characters.

### Left open

- **Chapter 21 against the rest.** Everywhere else the Tao is nameless, cannot be named, or is named by force, and in chapter 21 its name has never been erased and the beginning of things is read in it. 王弼 resolves it by reading the name as namelessness itself, 以無名說萬物始也, *"it is by the nameless that the beginning of the countless things is told"* (`fu-父.md`). The English leaves the contradiction standing, as the Chinese does.
- **Chapter 34's fork.** The Yongle encyclopedia's text of 王弼 reads 而不居 for 不名有, which would turn the line back into chapter 2's 功成而弗居 and take 名 out of the chapter's first half. Our base keeps 不名有. The fork is noted in `chapters/034.md` and not yet recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`.
- **Chapter 9's longer reading.** Every edition the Siku editors knew reads 功成名遂身退 (*gōng chéng míng suì shēn tuì* — "the work done, the name achieved, the body withdraws"), and our base prints the shorter 功遂身退. Adopting it would add a 名 line, and the name and the body would stand together in chapter 9 as they do in chapter 44 (`sources/variants.yaml`, our call: base).
- **The mouth in the graph.** 說文 makes a name something said aloud, and the book distrusts speech: 不言之教 (*bù yán zhī jiào* — "the unspoken teaching," 2, 43). Whether the book's suspicion of names is its suspicion of speech is a question for the 言 (*yán* — speech) entry, still owed (`glossary/TRIAGE.md`).
