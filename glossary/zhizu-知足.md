## 知足 (Zhī zú) — Knowing You Have Enough

*Say "jr dzoo," the first level, the second rising. Four lines in three chapters: 33, 44 and 46. Two plain words, to know and enough, set where other books set wealth, honor and safety.*

**Locked: 知足 → "knowing you have enough"; where a line wants the state as a noun, "contentment."** *(Not "sufficiency," which is stiff where the Chinese is two plain syllables. Not "fulfilled," which fills a vessel to the brim. Not "satisfaction," which names an appetite met, and a met appetite comes back.)*

### The graph: a knee above a foot, and an arrow spoken

> 足，人之足也。在下。从止、口。
> *"Zú is a person's foot. It is below. Made from 止 (zhǐ — a foot) and 口 (kǒu — an enclosure)."* — the Shuowen

**足 (zú) is the leg down to the ground.** The oracle-bone graph draws a knee above 止 (*zhǐ* — a foot, to stop): the whole leg, ending where it meets the earth. It was one graph with 疋 (*shū* — the foot) until the seal script split them. The Shuowen's second sentence is the one to keep: 在下 (*zài xià* — "it is below"). The foot is the lowest part of a standing person, and the part that touches the ground.

**The book still uses the first sense.** Chapter 64's proverb puts the foot where a journey starts:

> *The thousand-mile journey begins beneath your feet.*
>
> 千里之行，始於足下。
>
> — chapter 64

**How the foot came to mean *enough* is not settled, and this entry does not settle it.** The book holds both senses and lets them sit near each other: the foot is what a person stands on, and enough is what a life stands on. The resonance is real; the etymology is not proven.

> 知，詞也。从口从矢。
> *"Zhī is speech. Made from a mouth and an arrow."* — the Shuowen

**知 (zhī — to know) is a recognition clear enough to say.** The Shuowen pictures knowing as words that fly straight, like an arrow from the mouth.

### Four lines, and in each one the knowing is the wealth

> *One who knows others is clever.*
> *One who knows themselves is clear-seeing.*
> *One who overcomes others has force.*
> *One who overcomes themselves is strong.*
> *One who knows they have enough is wealthy.*
> *One who walks with strength has purpose.*
>
> 知人者智，
> 自知者明。
> 勝人者有力，
> 自勝者強。
> 知足者富。
> 強行者有志。
>
> — chapter 33

> *Know when you have enough, and you are never disgraced.*
> *Know when to stop, and you come to no danger.*
> *And so you can last long.*
>
> 知足不辱，
> 知止不殆，
> 可以長久。
>
> — chapter 44

> *There is no greater disaster than discontent,*
> *No greater fault than craving to acquire.*
> *And so the contentment of knowing you have enough is ever-present contentment.*
>
> 禍莫大於不知足；
> 咎莫大於欲得。
> 故知足之足，常足矣。
>
> — chapter 46

**In every line, enough is already there, and what is missing is the knowing.** No line tells a person to get more, or to want less. Chapter 33 makes the knowing itself the wealth. Chapter 44 makes it the safety. Chapter 46 makes its absence the greatest disaster, and its presence a contentment that does not run out.

### Chapter 33 is a ladder of knowing

**Three of the chapter's lines begin with 知 (zhī — to know), and they climb.** 知人 (*zhī rén* — "knows others") is intelligence. 自知 (*zì zhī* — "knows themselves") is clear-seeing, the sight the 明 (*míng* — clear-seeing) entry finds left over when nothing obstructs it. 知足 (*zhī zú* — "knows enough") is wealth. The object of the knowing moves from other people, to the self, to what the self already has. Every line keeps one frame, 者 (*zhě* — "the one who"), and the English keeps it too: *One who …*

### The oldest witness says it outright: knowing that enough is enough

**Guodian ends chapter 46 with a fuller sentence than the received text.** The bamboo slips, from about 300 BCE, read 知足之為足，此恒足矣 (*zhī zú zhī wéi zú, cǐ héng zú yǐ* — "knowing that enough is enough: this is ever enough"). The received text trims it to 故知足之足，常足矣 (*gù zhī zú zhī zú, cháng zú yǐ* — "so the enough of knowing enough is ever enough"). The slips' 之為 (*zhī wéi* — "as being") makes the phrase a recognition: to know enough is to know *that it is* enough. Their 恒 (*héng* — ever) stands where the received text writes 常 (*cháng* — the ever-present), as the 常 entry records of the silks.

**Heshang Gong reads chapter 33 in the same words.** On 知足者富 (*zhī zú zhě fù* — "one who knows enough is wealthy"): 人能知足之為足，則長保福祿，故為富也 — *"if a person can know that enough is enough, they keep their good fortune long, and so they are rich."* The slips and the commentator, centuries apart, spell 知足 out the same way. The verse keeps the received text, and `sources/variants.yaml` records the slips' reading.

### Knowing enough, and knowing when to stop

**知足 travels with 知止 (zhī zhǐ — "knowing when to stop").** Chapter 44 sets them as a couplet, and chapter 32 has 知止 alone:

> *Knowing when to stop, you come to no danger.*
>
> 知止可以不殆。
>
> — chapter 32

**They are two knowings, not one.** 知足 is about having: what is held is already enough. 知止 is about doing: the action halts before it overreaches. Wang Bi joins them in chapter 46, describing a world that has the Tao: 知足知止，無求於外，各修其內而已 — *"they know enough and know when to stop, seek nothing outside, and each simply tends what is within."* *Enough* and *stop* keep separate English, as the Chinese keeps separate words.

### Where the two commentators place the wealth

**Wang Bi keeps the wealth inside the knowing.** On chapter 33: 知足自不失，故富也 — *"one who knows enough does not lose themselves, and so is rich."* In chapter 20 he says why in one line: 自然已足，益之則憂 — *"what is so of itself is already enough; add to it and there is worry."*

**Heshang Gong pays the wealth out as fortune.** His chapter 33 knower *"keeps their good fortune long,"* and 福祿 (*fú lù* — good fortune and an official's salary) is worldly benefit. In chapter 44 he makes 知足 a discipline: 知足之人絶利去欲，不辱於身 — *"a person who knows enough cuts off profit and puts away desire, and is not disgraced in body."* In chapter 46 he names it twice: on 知足之足, 守真根也 — *"it is holding to the true root"*; on 常足, 无欲心也 — *"it is a heart without desire."*

**The verse follows the line, not either reading.** 富 (*fù* — wealth) is simply *wealthy*: the line says the knower is rich, and leaves the reader to find out in what.

### What is set aside

**"sufficiency."** A word for economists and for sermons on all-sufficient grace. It fails both tests: the hymn test, because it is at home in a sermon, and the pointing test, because no one can point at it. The Chinese is two syllables a child could say.

**"fulfilled," "fulfillment."** A vessel filled to the brim, which is the fullness chapter 9 tells you to stop short of: *"Rather than keep filling what you hold, stop."* It also carries the register of self-help, where a life is *fulfilled* by getting what it wanted.

**"satisfaction," "satisfied."** An appetite met. A met appetite comes back, and the book's enough does not run out.

**"content with little."** It adds *little*, a quantity the text never names, and turns knowing enough into frugality. Frugality is 儉 (*jiǎn* — frugality), chapter 67's second treasure, and a different word.

**"contentment," for every line.** It drops the knowing. *Contentment* is kept for the one place the line wants the state as a noun, chapter 46's close.

### Working register

| Where 知足 (*zhī zú*) stands | English | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| "the one who," the subject of a description | *one who knows they have enough* | 33 (知足者 *zhī zú zhě*) |
| a condition, opening a couplet | *know when you have enough* | 44 (知足不辱 *zhī zú bù rǔ*) |
| negated, as the subject of a comparison | *discontent* (in `flexions:`) | 46 (不知足 *bù zhī zú*) |
| as a noun, with its 足 (*zú* — enough) again | *the contentment of knowing you have enough* | 46 (知足之足 *zhī zú zhī zú*) |
| 足 alone, the state that lasts | *ever-present contentment* | 46 (常足 *cháng zú*) |

**Chapter 46's close keeps the Chinese's three 足 (zú — enough).** 知足之足，常足 (*zhī zú zhī zú, cháng zú*) hammers the one word three times, and the English answers with *contentment*, *enough*, *contentment*: the state-noun, the plain word, and the state-noun again.

### What no rule can enforce

**"Enough" belongs to 足 (zú — enough) wherever it stands, and most of its lines are not this entry's.** 足 alone, or in 不足 (*bù zú* — "not enough"), stands in chapters 17, 19, 23, 28, 35, 41, 48 and 77, and the foot stands in 64. Those lines print *enough*, *not enough*, *too little* and *runs short*, and no entry governs them. So *enough* cannot be held to 知足 in either direction: `concordance.py --english "enough"` reports those lines as unbacked by 知足, and 足 alone licenses each of them.

**"Sufficiency," "fulfilled" and "satisfaction" are forbidden only where 知足 stands,** in chapters 33, 44 and 46. The checker gates on the character's presence, so in any other chapter the ruling rests on the reader.

### Left open

**Chapter 33's third rung is longer than the first two.** The Chinese climbs in three steps of two characters: 知人 (*zhī rén* — "knows others"), 自知 (*zì zhī* — "knows themselves"), 知足 (*zhī zú* — "knows enough"). The English spends five words on the third, *knows they have enough*, because *knows enough* is heard as *has enough knowledge*, and *knows sufficiency* would make the recognition a concept and is ruled out here *(Shalom's call, 2026-10-30)*. The rungs are even in Chinese and uneven in English, and the English keeps the meaning over the shape.

**Whether 足 (zú — enough, foot) wants an entry of its own.** This entry governs four lines. 足 stands in seventeen, with several Englishes for 不足 (*bù zú* — "not enough") and a foot in chapter 64 that no entry argues for. An entry for 足 would hold both senses in one place, and could take this one in as its compound: `WORKLIST.md` T2-75.

**Guodian's fuller close.** The verse keeps the received 知足之足 (*zhī zú zhī zú* — "the enough of knowing enough"). The slips' 知足之為足 (*zhī zú zhī wéi zú* — "knowing that enough is enough") says the recognition outright, and Heshang Gong says it at chapter 33. Whether chapter 46's English should carry *that enough is enough* has not been asked.
