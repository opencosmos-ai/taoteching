## 士 (Shì) — In Service

*Say "shr," falling hard — the same sound as 事 (*shì* — affairs) and 恃 (*shì* — to rely on), which each have their own entry. Five appearances across three chapters, two of them on one identical phrase.*

**Locked: 士 → "in service."** *(Lowercase. Never "student," never "scholar," never "warrior.")*

A small word with a long reach: it is the first thing Chapter 15 says, the whole architecture of Chapter 41's opening, and the first of Chapter 68's four masteries. It had three Englishes until 2026-09-07 — *masters* (15), *student* (41), *warrior* (68) — **two of them on the identical phrase 善為士者**.

### The graph, and the reading that was put on it later

說文解字 (c. 100 CE) opens with the definition that matters and then explains it away:

> 士，**事也**。數始於一，終於十。从一从十。孔子曰：「推十合一爲士。」
> *"A 士 is **事** (affairs, service). Numbers begin at one and end at ten; the graph is from 一 (one) and 十 (ten). Confucius said: to push the ten together into one is a 士."*

**The first two characters are the definition. Everything after them is philosophy.** 一 + 十 with a Confucius quotation attached is a moral reading of a shape — a person who gathers the many into one — and it is the same move Han scholarship made on 王 (*wáng* — ruler), where 董仲舒 read the three strokes as sky, earth and human joined by the king. `glossary/wang-王.md` dates that one to roughly fourteen centuries after the graph it explains. **This book's rank-words attract that treatment**, and the pattern is worth knowing before trusting any traditional etymology of one.

**The honest limit:** what the graph originally depicted is contested and this entry does not settle it. What is not contested is 說文's own gloss — **事** — and that is the whole of what the English needs.

### 士 is defined by a character this glossary already holds

**事 (*shì* — affairs / to attend to)** has its own entry, and its graph is **a hand gripping the badge of office**. So 說文 is saying: **a 士 is the person whose hand that is.** Not someone who studies affairs, not someone who owns them — someone who *handles* them on another's behalf.

Historically that is exact. A 士 was the **lowest rank of the Zhou nobility**, between the 大夫 (*dà fū* — grandee) and the commoner: a person with a sword, a post, and a lord. By the Warring States the word had widened to cover the courtier, the retainer, the man-at-arms and the man of talent looking for a patron — but the centre held. **A 士 serves.**

### How Laozi uses it — five lines, two shapes

**善為士者 — "masterful in service" (15, 68).** The identical four characters open both chapters' arguments, and they must read identically:

> 古之**善為士者**，微妙玄通 (15) — *the ancients who were masterful in service were faint, subtle, dark and unobstructed*
> **善為士者**，不武 (68) — *those masterful in service are not martial*

**上士 · 中士 · 下士 — ranked (41).** 上 (*shàng* — upper), 中 (*zhōng* — middle), 下 (*xià* — lower) grade the same people into three grades of hearer. 上 is a rank marker throughout this book — ch 38's 上德 is *the highest integrity*, ch 8's 上善 *the most masterful* — so the English takes the height and keeps the noun: *those highest in service*, *those middling in service*, *those lowest in service*.

### The commentators disagree about what a 士 is, and that decides the English

This is the entry's real finding, and it is why a narrower word cannot be used.

> 王弼 on 68: **士卒之帥也**。武尚先陵人也 — *"a 士 is **the commander of the troops**. 武 (martial) means prizing being first and overbearing people."*
> 河上公 on 68: **言貴道德，不好武力** — *"it says: prize the Tao and integrity, and do not favour martial force."*

**王弼 makes 士 an office. 河上公 makes it a disposition.** One is reading a rank in an army, the other a person of a certain character. 河上公 is equally concrete at 41, where the middle 士 治身以長存，**治國以太平** — *"governs the body for long life, governs the state for great peace"* — and the lowest are 貪狼多欲, *"greedy as wolves and full of desire."*

**"In service" is the English that leaves both standing.** It does not say soldier and it does not say sage; it says *someone whose position is to serve*, which is what a commander and a moral agent both are here. **Convergence is strong evidence; divergence is a finding, and it must not be averaged away by picking one commentator's noun.**

### What is set aside

**"Student"** — the rendering this replaced at 41, and the one furthest from the character. 說文 says 事, affairs. Nobody in Chapter 41 is at school; 河上公's middle 士 is *governing a state*, and his lowest are greedy, not slow. It also makes the chapter a lesson about pupils when it is a claim about how the Tao is received by people with power.

**"Officer"** — the strongest rival, and it lost on register rather than on meaning. It is concrete, takes 上/中/下 naturally, and matches 王弼's 士卒之帥 exactly. But in English it reads **bureaucratic**, and carries a negative valence for many readers that the Chinese does not have; it also picks 王弼's reading over 河上公's, which the section above forbids. *(It is separately spoken for: 官 (*guān*) is *officers* at ch 28.)*

**"Warrior"** — what 68 read before, and it made the line cancel itself: *"the master warrior is not martial."* The Chinese says something sharper — the person whose whole position is service is not the one who fights hardest.

**"Scholar," "gentleman," "knight."** *Scholar* is the standard dictionary gloss for later Chinese and imports the imperial examination system, a thousand years downstream. *Gentleman* belongs to 君子 (*jūn zǐ*), which is a different word in this book and a Confucian honorific — handing it to 士 would tangle two rank-words the text keeps apart. *Knight* imports European feudalism wholesale.

**"Servant."** A 士 held rank. The word marks the bottom of the nobility, not the top of the household.

### Working register, and how it flexes

| Chinese | shape | English |
|---|---|---|
| 善為士者 (15, 68) | 善為 + 士 + 者 | **masterful in service** |
| 上士 / 中士 / 下士 (41) | rank word + 士 | **those highest / middling / lowest in service** |

**The rule for anything outside these five lines:** keep *service* as the root and let English grammar move around it. 士 is a **standing**, not an activity — so *in service* rather than *serving*, which would make it something a person does on an afternoon. Where a bare noun is unavoidable, *a person in service* before any rank word.

**The cost of the choice, stated plainly:** it is five English words for two characters at Chapter 41, and it is weak where the Chinese has a solid noun. That was accepted deliberately in exchange for not narrowing the word.

### The seam

**士 named a class of men.** Standing rule 1 governs: render toward the universal, and never erase the male default in silence. *In service* is neutral by design, and the fact that the original is not is recorded here rather than smoothed over.

士 belongs to a family the manuscript is still deciding — **大丈夫** (*dà zhàng fū*, ch 38), **君子** (*jūn zǐ*, ch 26 and 31), and 士 — three male rank-words that have been dissolved in opposite directions. `WORKLIST.md` T4-7 holds that question, and it is the same question as 民 / 人: what to do with a social category when the rank is often what the line is about.

### Left open

**Whether 上士 · 中士 · 下士 are three kinds of person or three grades of one office.** 王弼's 士卒之帥 implies a real hierarchy with posts in it; 河上公's greedy-as-wolves implies a moral spectrum. Our English takes the height words and stays silent on which, which may be more silence than the Chinese keeps.

**Whether ch 68's frame survives.** 善為士者 must match ch 15, but its three companion lines run 善▢者 as *"the master fighter," "the master of overcoming enemies," "the master of using people."* Four instances of one frame now wear two shapes. `WORKLIST.md` T2-47.
