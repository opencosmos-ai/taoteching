## 君 (Jūn) — Ruler

*Say "jwin," level. Six lines across three chapters: alone three times, and three times in 君子 (jūn zǐ — the noble). The one others answer to.*

**Locked: 君 → "ruler," and 君子 → "the noble."** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-20, which gave* sovereign *to 王 (wáng), so that* ruler *is 君's alone. 君子's* the noble *is Shalom's call of 2026-09-07.)* *(Not "sovereign," 王's word. Not "lord," which 主 (zhǔ) wears in the same chapter. Not "master," which turns the office into a skill. Not "king" or "monarch," which add a sex and a throne. For 君子, not "the gentleman" or "the superior man," which keep the male default.)*

### The graph: a hand holding the staff of office, over a mouth

**The Shuowen reads 君 as two parts, and gives each its job:**

> 君，尊也。从尹。發號，故从口。
> *"君 is the honored. From 尹 (yǐn — the governor). It issues commands, and so from 口 (kǒu — the mouth)."*

**尹 is a hand holding a staff**, the staff carried by people in authority, and the Shuowen defines it 治也…握事者也 (*"to govern… the one who grips affairs"*). **Under it, 口, the mouth that gives the orders.** Both graphs stand in the oracle bones. Modern references read 尹 as the sound of 君 as well as its sense, where the Shuowen gives it as sense alone (`dong-chinese`). The Shuowen also keeps an ancient form of 君, which it says 象君坐形 (*"pictures the ruler seated"*). The pictures agree on the essential thing: **a person who holds something, and speaks.**

**Where the picture stops:** nothing in the graph says how the ruler came to hold the staff, and nothing says the ruler is a man. The Shuowen's definition is not a description of the person at all. 尊 (*zūn* — honored) is a wine vessel raised in both hands (`glossary/gui-jian-貴賤.md`): **the ruler is the one others lift up.** The dictionary defines the minister from the other side, 臣，事君也 (*"臣 (chén — minister): one who serves the 君"*). Each word is the other's other half.

### Three lines alone, and the ruler is what the rest answer to

> *The heavy is the root of the light.*
> *Stillness is the ruler of the restless.*
>
> 重為輕根，
> 靜為躁君。
>
> — chapter 26

> *Lightness loses the root.*
> *Restlessness loses the ruler.*
>
> 輕則失本，
> 躁則失君。
>
> — chapter 26

> *Words have an ancestor.*
> *Affairs have a ruler.*
>
> 言有宗，
> 事有君。
>
> — chapter 70

**In none of the three is 君 a king on a throne.** It is the thing a scattered many answer to: stillness for the restless, someone for affairs, as a lineage stands behind words. Wang Bi on chapter 70 glosses it with the book's other word for the one who presides, 君，萬物之主也 — *"the ruler is the lord of the countless things."* Chapter 26 pairs 君 with 根 (*gēn* — root) and then with 本 (*běn* — root), so the ruler stands where the root stands: underneath, holding the rest in place. **That is the book's whole doctrine of rule in one couplet**, and it is why *ruler* serves. It names the relation, the one the others answer to, and leaves the crown out.

### 躁則失君 (zào zé shī jūn): the commentators hear a ruler losing their seat

**The line is double, and both commentators read it the same way.** Line 2 has made stillness the ruler of the restless, so *"restlessness loses the ruler"* can mean that the restless lose stillness, their ruler. **Wang Bi and Heshang Gong instead hear a ruler losing office:**

> 失君為失君位也。
> *"Losing the ruler is losing the ruler's seat."* — Wang Bi, chapter 26

> 王者行躁疾則失其君位。
> *"A king who acts restlessly and in haste loses their ruler's seat."* — Heshang Gong, chapter 26

**Heshang Gong's text makes the reading inevitable.** Where our base prints 輕則失本 (*qīng zé shī běn* — "lightness loses the root"), his prints 輕則失臣 (*qīng zé shī chén* — "lightness loses the ministers"), so his couplet closes on 臣 and 君, minister and ruler, the pair he also reads into chapter 70 (事有君臣, *"affairs have ruler and minister"*). Our base keeps 本, which closes the chapter back onto its first line (`notes/manuscript.md` → *Ch 26*). **The English *loses the ruler* holds both readings**, the ruler as stillness and the ruler as a person in office, and does not choose.

### Two words for the one who rules, and the book keeps them apart

**The book has three words for the one who rules, and the commentators pass freely between them.** 王 (*wáng*) is an axe-head. 主 (*zhǔ*) is a lamp and its flame, the one who presides. 君 is the hand and the mouth. Heshang Gong glosses chapter 26's 萬乘之主 (*wàn shèng zhī zhǔ* — "the lord of ten thousand chariots") as 謂王 (*"means a king"*), and the king's lost office as 君位 (*"the ruler's seat"*): three characters, one person. The Siku Wang Bi prints 萬乘之王 where our base has 主 (`sources/variants.yaml`). Wang Bi glosses 君 with 主 at chapter 70.

**The book itself does not pass between them.** 王 always stands beside 天下 (*tiān xià* — the world), in the cosmic ladder of chapters 16 and 25, or in the rank-pairs 侯王 (*hóu wáng* — nobles and sovereigns) and 王公 (*wáng gōng* — sovereigns and lords). It is the title at the top, and the Shuowen defines it as a direction: 王，天下所歸往也 (*"王 is that to which the world returns and goes"*). 君 is always the ruler *of* something, and never of the world. The *Hanshi waizhuan* (2nd century BCE) sets the two side by side and explains each by its sound:

> 君者何也？曰：群也，為天下萬物而除其害者，謂之君。王者何也？曰：往也。天下往之謂之王。
> *"What is the 君? It is 群 (qún — the flock): the one who removes harm for the world's countless things is called 君. What is the 王? It is 往 (wǎng — to go to). What the world goes to is called 王."* — juan 5

**The sound-glosses are Han political theory**, and scholars caution that no pre-Qin text uses 君 to mean a flock. What they record is the distinction itself: the 君 stands with those it gathers, as the Shuowen's 臣 (*chén* — minister) stands with the 君 it serves, and the 王 is where everything comes. **So the English keeps them apart: *ruler* for 君, which names a relation, and *sovereign* for 王, which names the summit** (Shalom's call, 2026-11-20). Until that day both read *ruler*. The reasoning for 王 is in `glossary/wang-王.md`.

### 君子 (jūn zǐ — the noble)

**Literally a ruler's son: the honorific of the ruling class, and in the *Analects* the name of Confucius's ideal person.** It stands in two chapters.

> *Therefore, the noble travels all day without leaving the heavy wagons.*
>
> 是以君子終日行不離輜重。
>
> — chapter 26

> *The noble at home prizes the left;*
> *using weapons prizes the right.*
>
> 君子居則貴左，
> 用兵則貴右。
>
> — chapter 31

> *Weapons are tools of ill omen, not the noble's tools.*
>
> 兵者不祥之器，非君子之器，
>
> — chapter 31

**The noble is not the sage, and the book keeps them apart.** 聖人 (*shèng rén* — the sage) stands in 25 chapters and never in these two lines. At chapter 26 both Mawangdui silks read 君子 with our base, while the Siku Wang Bi and Heshang Gong print 聖人: somewhere in transmission the noble was absorbed into the sage (`glossary/sheng-聖.md`). Heshang Gong on chapter 31 sets the noble against the soldier: 此言兵道與君子道反 — *"this says the way of arms and the way of the noble are opposed."* At chapter 31 the noble is the person whose tools weapons are not.

**The English keeps the rank and drops the sex.** 君子 named a male class. *The noble* is a rank still, which is what both lines are about: what a person of standing does at home and in war. It names no man (Shalom's call, 2026-09-07; the rank question across 君子, 士 (*shì* — in service) and 大丈夫 (*dà zhàng fū* — the full-grown person) closed 2026-11-19, `WORKLIST-CLOSED.md` T4-7). The seam is named in chapter 26's notes and chapter 31's.

### What is set aside

**"Sovereign," "sovereignty."** 王's word, and the abstract noun drifts toward a political theory. 君 in these lines is a person or a relation, never a doctrine.

**"Lord."** 主 (*zhǔ*) wears it in chapter 26, three lines below 君's *ruler*, and the two must not merge on the page. *Lord* also fails the hymn test in chapter 70, a chapter spoken in the first person about being unknown.

**"Master," "mastery."** It makes the office a skill, as *mastery* once did at chapter 26's 躁則失君, and *master* is 主's at chapter 34 (*does not act as their master*). 善 (*shàn* — masterful) holds the skill words.

**"King," "monarch."** Each adds a sex the graph lacks and a throne the lines never show, as 王's entry argues for its own character.

**"Commander," "command."** The mouth issuing orders is in the graph, but *command* is the English of 令 (*lìng* — to command) at chapter 32 and 命 (*mìng*) at chapter 51 (`WORKLIST.md` T2-29), and a ruler defined by commanding is the opposite of chapter 26's still one.

**For 君子: "the gentleman," "the superior man."** *The gentleman* would have shown the borrowing from Confucian vocabulary, and was declined for its male default (Shalom's call, 2026-09-07). *The superior man* is the nineteenth-century missionary rendering, male and moralizing at once.

**For 君子: "the sage."** 聖人's word in every other chapter. Giving it to 君子 made Laozi's figure and Confucius's one person.

**The flexion is the compound, not the character.** *The noble* is licensed in `flexions:` for chapters 26 and 31, the only chapters 君子 stands in. Chapter 26 holds both forms, three lines apart.

### What no rule can enforce

**"Lord" cannot be forbidden.** 主 (*zhǔ*) prints *lord* in chapter 26, which holds 君. The ruling stands in the prose.

**"The sage" cannot be forbidden for 君子.** The forbidden list keys on the character, and 君 stands in chapter 70, whose last line is 聖人's *the sage*. `concordance.py --english "the noble"` and a reading of chapters 26 and 31 are the check.

**"Master" is ruled out, and left off the list.** The gate matches inside words, so *master* would raise a notice on *masterful*, 善's English, in some fifty lines across the book, and bury the notices worth reading (`WORKLIST.md` T5-5). The list carries *mastery*, the form the old error took.

**主 (*zhǔ*) can still print *ruler*.** It has no entry, and it does at chapter 30 (below). `concordance.py --english "ruler"` is the check.

### Left open

**主 (*zhǔ*) wears *ruler* at chapter 30.** 以道佐人主者 reads *"one who supports a ruler with the Tao,"* so *ruler* is not yet 君's alone, and 主 has five Englishes across its five chapters: *lord* (26), *ruler* (30), *master* (34), *host* (69), *keeper* (78). 主 has no entry to decide them (`WORKLIST.md` T2-92).

**Whether the Han sound-glosses reach back.** The *Hanshi waizhuan*'s 君 as 群 (*qún* — the flock) and 王 as 往 (*wǎng* — to go to) fit the book's lines closely, chapter 35's 天下往 (*tiān xià wǎng* — "the world comes to you") above all. They are attested a century and a half after Guodian. Whether Laozi's audience heard them, or the Han read the book into them, is open.

**Chapter 31's Guodian text is unread here.** Guodian's bundle C carries the end of chapter 31 (`sources/guodian-inventory.yaml`), and nothing from it is recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`, so whether it carries the 君子 lines, and how it writes them, is unchecked (`WORKLIST.md` T4-6).
