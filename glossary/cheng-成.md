## 成 (Chéng) — Complete

*Say "chung," rising. Seventeen lines across fourteen chapters. The word for a thing brought to its end, and in this book completion is almost never something done to a thing. It is what happens to it.*

**Locked: 成 → "complete."** *(Shalom's calls, 2026-11-20.)* **Two declared flexions:** *done* in the formula 功成 (*gōng chéng* — "the work is done") at 2, 17, 34 and 77, and *made* in chapter 15's 新成 (*xīn chéng* — "newly made"). *(Not "compiled," which makes the cosmos a program and slips a programmer in behind it. Not "perfect," which makes completion a Platonic ideal the book does not hold. Not "become," which is a verb English needs, not 成's word.)*

### The graph: an axe and a wall

**成 is 戊 (*wù* — a battle-axe) with 丁 (*dīng*) for its sound.** The Shuowen gives it in two characters:

> 成，就也。从戊丁聲。
> *"成 is to arrive at [its end]. From 戊 (wù — a battle-axe), with 丁 (dīng) for sound."*

**就 (*jiù* — to arrive at, to settle into) is the same gloss Heshang Gong gives at chapter 41**, 成，就也, and it is a word of arriving, not of striking. Ji Xusheng, as Wiktionary reports him, reads the original sense as **city walls**, a protected place with the weapon guarding it, a sense kept in 城 (*chéng* — a city wall), which adds 土 (*tǔ* — earth). The word's family runs the same way: Schuessler sets 成 beside 盛 (*chéng* — to hold in a vessel) and 城, all one sound.

**Where the picture stops.** An axe sits in the graph, and it is tempting to read 成 as *the decisive stroke that ends a process*. That is a story told about the axe, not something any early reader says, and the book's own completing is the opposite of a stroke. It is held loosely here, and nothing in the English depends on it. What the family does show is worth keeping: a wall that encloses, a vessel that holds. To complete is to bring something to the point where it holds.

### Seventeen lines, and completion is what happens to things

> *Something mixed and complete, born before sky and earth.*
>
> 有物混成，先天地生。
>
> — chapter 25

> *Only the Tao is masterful at lending, and at completing.*
>
> 夫唯道，善貸且成。
>
> — chapter 41

> *matter forms them, circumstance completes them.*
>
> 物形之，勢成之。
>
> — chapter 51

> *names without seeing,*
> *and accomplishes without acting.*
>
> 不見而名，
> 不為而成。
>
> — chapter 47

> *Great completeness seems deficient, yet when used, it never wears out.*
>
> 大成若缺，其用不弊。
>
> — chapter 45

> *So presence and absence give birth to each other,*
> *Difficult and easy complete each other,*
>
> 故有無相生，
> 難易相成，
>
> — chapter 2

**In almost every line, nobody does the completing.** Things complete each other (2). The Tao completes, but as a lender does, by letting go of what it gives (41). Circumstance completes (51). The sage completes *without acting* (47). The Tao itself is *mixed and complete* before anything was made (25).

**Wang Bi says outright that this completing is not a craft.** On chapter 41: 成之不如機匠之裁，無物而不濟其形，故曰善成 — *"its completing is not like the cutting of a skilled craftsman; there is nothing whose form it does not carry through, and so it says 'masterful at completing.'"* The craftsman cuts, which is 割 (*gē* — to sever)'s work and the carving that makes vessels out of uncarved wood (`glossary/pu-樸.md`). The Tao completes without cutting. On chapter 47: 明物之性，因之而已，故雖不為，而使之成矣 — *"clear about the nature of things, it simply goes along with them; and so, though it does not act, it lets them be completed."* On chapter 25: 混然不可得而知，而萬物由之以成，故曰混成也 — *"mixed, it cannot be known, yet the countless things are completed through it; so it says 'mixed and complete.'"*

**And the book distrusts completion even as it uses the word.** The great vessel, in the oldest witnesses, is *never completed*, 免成 (*miǎn chéng* — free of completion), where the received text has 晚成 (*wǎn chéng* — completed late) (`notes/manuscript.md`). Great completeness *seems deficient* (45), and Wang Bi says why: 隨物而成，不為一象，故若缺也 — *"it completes by following each thing, and does not make itself one image; so it seems deficient."* People ruin their affairs *just short of completing them* (64), and Heshang Gong names the cause: 常於功德幾成而貪位好名 — *"always at the point of completing their work, they grow greedy for position and love a name."* And the ancients of chapter 15 are *never newly made*, which `chapters/015.md` reads through the full vessel: what is filled is finished, and the ancients stay unfilled.

### The work is done, and no one stays in it

> *The work is done, and the sage does not stay in it.*
>
> 功成而弗居。
>
> — chapter 2

> *The work is done, and it does not name it its own.*
>
> 功成而不名有。
>
> — chapter 34

> *The work is done, and they do not stay in it.*
>
> 功成而不處，
>
> — chapter 77

**Four times the book says 功成, and three times the next words refuse to stay with it.** *Done* is the formula's English in all four, a declared flexion, because the line is about the moment after, not the quality of the result: Heshang Gong glosses it 功成事就，退避不居其位 (*"the work done, the affairs arrived at, they withdraw and do not keep the position"*). The fourth, chapter 17's 功成事遂 (*gōng chéng shì suì* — "the work done, the affairs carried through"), is left open below.

### Chapter 67: chief of the completed vessels

> *Never pushing to be first in the world, and so you can become chief of the completed vessels.*
>
> 不敢為天下先，故能成器長。
>
> — chapter 67

**成 here is *completed*, inside the noun phrase, and every early reader puts it there.** Han Fei, the oldest witness, prints a verb our text lacks, 故能為成事長 (*gù néng wéi chéng shì zhǎng* — "and so can become chief of the completed affairs"), and glosses it: 處大官之謂為成事長 — *"to hold high office is what 'becoming chief of the completed affairs' means."* His 為 (*wéi* — become) is the verb; 成事 (*chéng shì* — completed affairs) is a noun phrase. Wang Bi reads 成器 (*chéng qì*) the same way: 然後乃能立成器為天下利，為物之長也 — *"then they can set up the completed vessels for the world's benefit, and be the chief of things."* Heshang Gong takes 成器長 whole, as a title: 成器長謂得道人也 — *"chief of the completed vessels means those who have attained the Tao."*

**The reading closes a circle with chapter 28.** There, 樸散則為器 (*pǔ sàn zé wéi qì* — "the uncarved wood scatters, and becomes vessels"), and the sage, who was never carved, becomes chief of the officers. Here the one who will not go first becomes chief of the completed vessels: the uncarved presiding over the finished things. *Become* stays in the English, as the verb English needs and the verb Han Fei prints; it is not 成's word (`process/principles/supplied-verb-is-scaffolding.md`). Han Fei's 為 is recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`, beside his 事 (*shì* — affairs) for our 器 (*qì* — vessel).

### And so they can complete it: one frame, three lines

> *Is it not because they have no private self?*
> *And so they can complete their private self.*
>
> 非以其無私邪？
> 故能成其私。
>
> — chapter 7

> *Therefore the sage never does the great,*
> *and so their greatness can be completed.*
>
> 是以聖人終不為大，
> 故能成其大。
>
> — chapter 63

**故能成其X (*gù néng chéng qí X* — "and so can complete their X") stands three times**, at 7 with 私 (*sī* — private self) and at 34 and 63 with 大 (*dà* — great). Where the Chinese repeats its frame, the English repeats its verb: *complete* in all three. **Chapter 7 is the paradox in its sharpest form**: the self is completed by the one who has none. Wang Bi: 無私者，無為於身也。身先身存，故曰能成其私也 — *"having no private self is not acting for the body. The body comes first, the body survives, and so it says they can complete their private self."* Heshang Gong: 聖人無私而己自厚 — *"the sage has no private aim, and the self thickens of itself."*

### What is set aside

**"Compiled," "compile," "the final build."** A rendering from a computational frame laid over the book, in which the universe is software. It fails the pointing test, and it slips a programmer in behind the world exactly as *Heaven* slips in a God. Wang Bi's chapter 41 refuses even the craftsman.

**"Perfect," "perfection."** The usual English for 大成 (*dà chéng* — great completeness) at 45, and a Platonic word: the ideal form that real things fall short of. The book's completion is the reverse, a thing arriving at its own end, and it *seems deficient* when it gets there. *Perfection* also fails the hymn test.

**"Finish," "finished."** Plain and right in sense, and *finished vessels* reads more easily at 67. It would be a seventh word for one character, and *finish* is what 終 (*zhōng* — the end) may need at chapter 64's 慎終如始 (*shèn zhōng rú shǐ* — "careful at the end as at the beginning").

**"Fulfill."** The self-help register for chapter 7's 成其私, and a second verb for the frame chapters 34 and 63 render *complete*.

**"Achieve," "accomplish."** Achievement words, which put the weight on the doer. Most of the book's completing has no doer. *Accomplishes* at chapter 47 is owed a decision (below).

**"Become."** A verb English supplies where 成 stands before a noun, never the character's own. At chapter 67 it renders Han Fei's 為, not 成.

### What no rule can enforce

***Done* is 遂 (*suì* — carried through)'s too.** Chapter 9 reads 功遂 (*gōng suì*) as *"The work done."* Chapter 9 has no 成, so no rule sees two characters sharing one formula's English.

***Finish* is ruled out for 成, and is not forbidden**, so that 終 can take it at chapter 64, which holds both characters.

***Become* is ruled out for 成, and cannot be forbidden.** It stands in chapter 67 as the supplied verb, the 為 (*wéi* — become) Han Fei prints, and a forbidden word would flag it there.

### Left open

**Chapter 17 renders two of its four characters.** 功成事遂 (*gōng chéng shì suì* — "the work done, the affairs carried through") reads *"When their work is done,"* and 事遂 is missing. Chapter 17 holds 成 and 遂 in one line, which forces them apart, and 遂 already wears *done* at chapter 9. Owed its own decision, with 遂.

**Chapter 47's *accomplishes*.** 不為而成 (*bù wéi ér chéng* — "completes without acting") is the one completing verb outside the register. *Completes without acting* would bring it in.

**The frame's voice.** 故能成其X reads active at chapter 7 (*they can complete their private self*) and passive at 34 and 63 (*their greatness can be completed*), each a call of Shalom's. The verb is one word now; whether the voice should be one too is open.
