## 心 (Xīn) — The Heart

*Say "shin," high and level. Eight appearances across seven chapters — 3, 8, 12, 20, 49, 55. Small, and it decides how the whole book understands a person.*

**Locked: 心 → "heart." · 腹 → "belly."** *(Not "mind," not "heart-mind," not "the wise core.")*

The problem is the one that made translators reach for a hyphen in the first place: **English has a heart and a mind, and classical Chinese has neither.** It has 心, which is both and prior to both, and any English word will import a division the Chinese does not make.

### The character, read slowly — an organ, drawn

心 is not a symbol. It is an **anatomical drawing**: the oracle-bone and bronze forms show the outline of a heart, with its chambers and the great vessels leaving the top. The *Shuowen Jiezi* is equally physical — 人心，土藏也，在身之中 — *"the human heart; the earth-organ; at the center of the body."*

And in the medicine and philosophy of the period, that organ in your chest was the seat of **thinking as well as feeling**. Not metaphorically. The brain in the skull was not understood as the place where thought happened; the 心 was. So a word we translate as *heart* was also the word for judgment, intention, attention, memory, and decision.

This is why "heart-mind" exists as a rendering. It is accurate. It is also a scholar's compound rather than an English word, and it announces on every appearance that the reader is handling a foreign object.

### The argument for plain "heart"

Here is what the hyphen assumes and what English actually does: **our own word never fully split, either.** Look at the idioms and the cognition is still there —

- **learn by heart** — pure memory
- **change of heart** — literally, changing your *mind*
- **know it in your heart** — conviction, a judgment held
- **the heart of the matter** — the essence, the crux
- **set your heart on it** — sustained intention
- **take heart** — resolve

English "heart" carries feeling, will, conviction, memory, and essence. The narrowly sentimental "heart" of greeting cards is a recent and shallow layer over a much older word that meant roughly what 心 means. We do not need a hyphen to repair a split that our own idioms have been quietly refusing all along.

And the register argument is the same one that settled 無為 → *non-doing* and 萬物 → *countless*: **"heart" is plain, bodily, Anglo-Saxon; "heart-mind" is a technical term.** This translation is meant to be living verse, not a gloss. Where English cannot carry the whole concept, the glossary carries it — exactly as with 物, where "things" cannot hold an ox and the verbs do that work instead.

### The counter-term: 腹, the belly

心 does not travel alone. Twice it is set against the **belly**, and the pairing is the argument:

> 虛其**心**，實其**腹** (3) — *empty their hearts, fill their bellies.*
> 是以聖人為**腹**不為**目** (12) — *so the sage acts for the belly, not for the eye.*

**腹** (*fù*) is the belly — what actually needs feeding. Against it stand the heart (which grasps, schemes, wants) and the eye (which craves endlessly, since seeing never fills anything). The sage attends to the organ that can be **satisfied** and neglects the organs that cannot.

Note that 虛 (*emptying*) is not an insult here. It is Laozi's highest practice — 致虛極 (16), *go totally empty*. To empty the heart is to unclutter it of grasping, not to make people stupid. (This chapter is often read as advocating a dull populace; the vocabulary argues otherwise, and it is a fair fight worth having in the open.)

**"gut" is on 心's `forbidden:` list, and the reason it had to be.** *(Added 2026-08-30.)* 腹 sits inside this entry as a `covers:` character, and **`covers:` records a `render:` but never a `forbidden:` list** — so for as long as the argument below lived only in this prose, *gut* was unforbidden book-wide, and ch 12 line 19 read *"chooses **gut wisdom**"* one line beneath its own correctly rendered *belly*. The entry named the error, cited this very chapter as its evidence, and could not stop it. Hanging the word on **心** works because 心 is present in ch 3 and ch 12 — both of the chapters where 腹 appears — so the evidence gate fires, and nothing else in either chapter can legitimately claim *gut*.

**Why "belly" and not "gut."** Chapter 3 decides it: 實其腹 is unambiguously about **food** — the line sits in a four-part anatomy of governance (empty the heart, fill the belly, weaken the will, strengthen the bones), and it means *feed them*. You fill a belly; you do not fill a gut. English "gut" has also drifted toward **instinct** (*trust your gut*) and **courage** (*guts*), and the first is precisely what must not be imported here: Chapter 12's contrast is *inner need against outer craving*, not *intuition against analysis*. The belly is the organ that can be **satisfied**; the eye is the one that cannot.

*(A word on "the wise core," a rendering with a real source behind it: later Daoist internal alchemy makes the lower abdomen the **丹田**, the "cinnabar field," the body's energetic center. But 丹田 is Han-era and after, centuries downstream of this text. Reading it back into Laozi's 腹 is the same move as reading "Nature" back into 自然. Laozi wrote a belly.)*

### Chapter 49 — the heart that is not fixed

The book's densest use puts 心 three times in nine characters:

> 聖人無常**心**，以百姓**心**為**心**。
> *The sage has no fixed heart; they take everyone's heart as their own.*

Not "no heart" — **no fixed one.** The sage has no private set of preferences held steady against the world, so the people's disposition can become theirs. It is the political form of 無為: not doing nothing, but not bringing your own agenda to the encounter.

*(Note that 常 here is the shallow sense — *fixed, habitual, one's usual* — not the profound 常 of Chapters 1 and 16. The same character, two altitudes; see `chang-常.md`.)*

The chapter closes on 渾其心 — *makes their heart undifferentiated, muddied, whole again* — and then 聖人皆孩之, *the sage treats them all as children*. The heart that holds no fixed position ends up parental.

### The other appearances

- **8** — 心善淵, *in heart, be good at depth.* Among the water-virtues: the heart like a still pool, deep and unhurried.
- **12** — 令人心發狂, *makes people's hearts go mad.* The five colors blind, the five tones deafen, and the hunt drives the heart wild. Sensory overload as a disorder of the heart, not the eyes.
- **20** — 我愚人之心也哉, *I have the heart of a fool!* Laozi's own confession, and 愚 (*yú*, foolish, uncarved-simple) is a compliment he pays himself.
- **55** — 心使氣曰強, *when the heart drives the vital breath, that is forcing.* The one clearly negative use: the willing self muscling the body's own current. You cannot command the breath into harmony. *(See Ch 55's translation note.)*

### What we set aside

**"Mind."** Imports cold cognition, computation, the disembodied reasoner. It also makes Chapter 3 ("empty their minds") sound like a program of enforced ignorance, and Chapter 8 ("in mind, be deep") sound like advice about intelligence.

**"Heart-mind."** Accurate and honest, and we set it aside with respect. Its cost is that it is a *term of art*: hyphenated, self-conscious, and impossible to say aloud without quotation marks. In eight appearances across a book of verse, it would read as a footnote embedded in the line.

**"Consciousness," "the center of consciousness," "psyche," "soul."** Abstractions in place of an organ, and "soul" imports a body/soul dualism Chinese thought does not hold *(see the overlay audit)*.

**And any mechanical rendering** — 心 as a processor, a control centre, the seat of information. A processor has no belly to be set against, cannot go mad from too much scenery, and cannot be the heart of a fool.

### The working register

- **心** → **heart** (心善淵 → "in heart, be deep as a pool"; 無常心 → "no fixed heart")
- **腹** → **belly** (實其腹 → "fill their bellies"; 為腹不為目 → "for the belly, not the eye")
- 虛其心 → **"empty their hearts"** — keep 虛 positive; it is the same word as Ch 16's *utmost emptiness*

### The tension, left open

Something is genuinely lost, and it should be said plainly rather than argued away.

A reader who meets "heart" in Chapter 55 — *when the heart drives the vital breath, that is forcing* — will most likely picture emotion overriding good sense. The Chinese means something closer to the **willing, deciding self** overriding the body's own motion. English "heart" can carry that (*his heart was set on it*), but it is not the first thing the word does.

So the choice is between a word that is warm and slightly imprecise, and a compound that is precise and slightly dead. We take the warm one, and let this entry hold the precision — on the same principle as 物, where the ox lives in the verbs rather than the noun.
