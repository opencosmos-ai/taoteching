## 常 (Cháng) — The Ever-Present

*Say "chahng," rising like a question. Thirty appearances across nineteen chapters, four of them in the book's first six lines.*

**Locked: 常 → "the ever-present"** (as a name, or before a noun) **/ "ever"** (before a 無X or 有X state) **/ "always"** (before any other predicate). *(Never "eternal," "eternally," "eternity," "everlasting," or "timeless." "Enduring" and "lasting" are ruled out too, but no check can hold that ruling; see* What no rule can enforce *below.)*

常 names what does not lapse: the thing that is there every time you look, the habit that holds, the course that keeps coming round. It is a word about **time running on**, not about a place outside time. The book uses it two ways. As a manner, it says the Tao is *ever* nameless, *ever* non-doing. As a name, it is what returning is called, and knowing it is called clear-seeing (16, 55). The oldest manuscripts write these two uses with two different characters, and that fact settles more about the first line of the book than any dictionary does.

### The graph: a skirt, with a sound on top

**Our vendored 說文解字 (*Shuōwén Jiězì*, c. 100 CE): 下帬也。从巾尚聲** — *"a lower skirt. From 巾 (jīn — cloth), with 尚 (shàng) for sound."*

- **巾 (*jīn*)** is a piece of cloth hanging down: a sash, a towel, a kerchief at the belt. It is the meaning half, and it says *fabric*.
- **尚 (*shàng*)** is the sound half. Xu Shen glosses it 曾也。庶幾也 — *"still; nearly"* — and files it as sound only. It contributes nothing to the sense here, and the old and modern readings agree on that.

The garment is the whole of Xu Shen's definition. Upper and lower dress were two words: his own entry for 衣 (*yī* — clothing) says **上曰衣，下曰裳** — *"the upper is called 衣, the lower 裳."* **裳 (*cháng*)** is the same word with 衣 swapped in for 巾, and 說文 lists it as 常's alternative form. Over time 裳 took the skirt and 常 was left to its other meaning. *(That alternative-form line, and the bronze record below, come from outside this repository's selection of 說文.)*

**How old the graph is.** 常 has not been identified in the Shang oracle bones. Its earliest forms are Western Zhou bronze inscriptions, followed by the Warring States scripts and the small seal. Its phonetic, 尚, is older.

**How a skirt came to mean *always*.** Most modern accounts say it didn't. The *always* sense is a **phonetic loan**: a word for "constant" with no graph of its own was written with the graph for a homophone, a skirt pronounced *\*djaŋ* in Old Chinese. A tidy story runs from "everyday garment" to "everyday" to "constant", but no early text supports it, so we do not tell it. Axel Schuessler suggests, tentatively, that the *always* word is related to a Tibetan word meaning *again, once more*. If he is right, the word was about recurrence before it was about permanence.

**The banner.** One early concrete use is worth knowing because it points at the sky. The 周禮 (*Zhōu Lǐ* — the Rites of Zhou) lists the royal flags: **日月為常** — *"the one bearing the sun and moon is the 常."* The king's banner carried the two lights that never fail to come back. Shirakawa Shizuka proposes the banner as the word's root. That is a proposal, and we print it as a resonance, not as the etymology.

**By the Zhou poems the abstract sense is fully there.** The 詩經 (*Shījīng* — the Book of Songs), in the ode 文王 (*Wén Wáng*), says **天命靡常** — *"the sky's mandate does not stay put."* Even in this early use, 常 is about **whether a thing holds over time.**

### Two words under one: 恒 and 常

The Mawangdui silk manuscripts (copied before about 168 BCE) do not write 常 everywhere the received text does. They use two characters, and the split follows grammar.

- **恒 (*héng*)** stands wherever the word is a **manner** or a **modifier**. Silk A reads 非恒道 and 非恒名 in chapter 1, its 恒無欲 and 恒有欲, 恒使民 (3), 恒善救人 (27), the three 恒德 of 28, 道恒無名 (32), 恒足 (46), 牝恒以靜勝牡 (61), 恒知稽式 (65), 恒與善人 (79), and the same in 49 and 74.
- **常** stands only where the word is a **name**, the thing returning is called: 復命常也，知常明也，不知常…知常容 (16), 和曰常 (55), and 是謂襲常 (52).

The silks write 恒 freely; neither copy avoids it. The received text's single 常 is usually explained as a **Han substitution**. 恒 was the personal name of Emperor Wen (劉恒, *Liú Héng*, r. 180–157 BCE), and later copyists replaced it with its nearest synonym. That merged two words into one graph. Our base text is the received text, and we translate the 常 it prints. But the reader should know that **line 1's word was the manner-word 恒, not the name-word 常.**

**And 恒 is a picture you can point at.** In the oracle bones the graph that later became 恒 shows **a crescent moon between two lines**. 說文 defines 恆 with one character, **常也** (*"constant"*), and preserves an old form written with 月 (*yuè* — moon). As evidence it quotes the Book of Songs: **如月之恆** — *"like the moon's steady waxing."* The constancy of 恒 is the moon's: a thing that changes shape every night and never once fails to come back. That is the opposite of a changeless realm. It is change that keeps its appointment.

### What the oldest readers say it is

**韓非 (*Hán Fēi*, d. 233 BCE), the oldest commentary we carry, defines the word at length on this very line:**

> 夫物之一存一亡，乍死乍生，初盛而後衰者，不可謂常。唯夫與天地之剖判也具生，至天地之消散也不死不衰者謂常。而常者無攸易，無定理。無定理，非在於常所，是以不可道也。

*"Things that now remain and now perish, that die all at once and all at once live, that flourish first and decline after, cannot be called 常. Only what was born together with the splitting open of sky and earth, and when sky and earth disperse neither dies nor declines, is called 常. And 常 has nothing it changes into, and no fixed pattern. Having no fixed pattern, it is not in any fixed place (常所), and that is why it cannot be made a way."*

Three things in this passage decide the rendering:

1. **It has a birth.** 與天地之剖判也具生 — *born along with sky and earth's splitting.* The oldest reader does not make 常 beginningless. It lasts **as long as the world lasts**, without decay. That is duration, not eternity in the strict sense.
2. **What makes it 常 is that it does not decline.** The contrast class is things that 盛 and 衰, *flourish and fade*. 常 is the thing that does not fade.
3. **It is in no fixed place.** 非在於常所 — and Han Fei uses 常 in its everyday sense, *a fixed place*, inside his definition of the deep one. Having no fixed place means it is everywhere. This is the strongest early support for *present* in *ever-present*.

**河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*)** glosses 非常名 as 非自然**常在**之名也 — *"not the name of what is so-of-itself and **always there**."* 常在 (*cháng zài*) is, word for word, *always present*. On the Tao line he reads 常 as 長生 (*cháng shēng* — long life), which fits his interest in longevity. At chapter 16 he reads it as a course: 道之所常行, *"what the Tao always does."*

**王弼 (*Wáng Bì*)**, at chapter 16, describes it by what it lacks: 常之為物，不偏不彰，無皦昧之狀，溫涼之象 — *"as a thing, 常 leans to no side and shows no mark; it has no bright or dim look, no warm or cool feel."* At 55: 物以和為常 — *"things take harmony as their 常."* For him it is the featureless norm things settle into.

None of the three puts 常 outside time. All three describe it as something that **keeps on, here.**

### Line 1: *ever-present*, not *eternal*

**道可道，非常道** → *"The Tao that can be made a way is not the ever-present Tao."*

*Eternal* is often called the literal translation of 常. It isn't. It is the popular one, made familiar by translations such as Gia-fu Feng and Jane English (1972) and Stephen Mitchell (1988). The scholars' translations do not use it: James Legge (1891) has *"the enduring and unchanging Tao,"* Arthur Waley (1934) *"an Unvarying Way,"* D. C. Lau (1963) *"the constant way."* The dictionary sense is *constant, regular, lasting.*

*Ever-present* is the more accurate rendering here, for four reasons:

- **It tracks the word the line actually used.** Line 1's word is 恒, the manner-word: *ever.* *Ever-present* is built on *ever*. *Eternal* is built on Latin *aeternus*, and through two thousand years of theology it now means either *without beginning or end* or *outside time altogether*. Han Fei denies the first, since his 常 is born with the world, and nobody in the tradition says the second.
- **It keeps the chapter's four 常 visible as one word.** Chapter 1 prints 常 four times in six lines: 非常道, 非常名, 常無欲, 常有欲. In English that is *ever-present, ever-present, ever desireless, ever desiring.* Render line 1 *eternal* and the reader cannot see that the Tao's constancy and the observer's constant desirelessness are the same word. In the Chinese they are, and the chapter turns on it: you observe the ever-present by being ever something yourself.
- **It passes the pointing test.** Laozi could point at the moon coming back, at a river that is always there. He could not point at eternity.
- **The commentary backs *present*.** 河上公's 常在 and 韓非's 非在於常所 both say that what is 常 is *there*, everywhere and always.

**As a glossary heading the same holds.** *The ever-present* covers the name-word of 16, 52 and 55, and its *ever* covers the manner-word of 1, 3, 32, 34 and 37. One English root spans both characters the received text merged. *Eternal* fits neither.

### What we set aside

- ***eternal / eternity*** — fails the hymn test outright, since *"the eternal Tao"* is at home in a sermon. It also imports a timeless beyond, which the book never names and its oldest readers contradict. Forbidden.
- ***everlasting*** — closer, because it at least runs on in time, but it is hymn vocabulary (*everlasting arms*, *life everlasting*). It also lays stress on *lasting*, which is 長's word. Forbidden.
- ***timeless*** — the opposite of the claim. 常 is time that does not lapse, not the absence of time. Forbidden.
- ***constant*** — the lexicographers' word, and accurate. Set aside for two reasons. It is a label where the book wants a picture (`process/principles/perform-dont-label.md`), and in English it drifts toward *loyal* (*a constant friend*) and toward the physics of fixed quantities. Not forbidden: nothing in the manuscript uses it, and a reader's ruling is enough.
- ***unchanging / changeless*** — *unchanging* is already spoken for: 獨立不改 (25), *"Solitary. Unchanging"*, renders 不改 (*bù gǎi* — does not alter). And Han Fei's 常 is not unchanging in the sense of static. It is what does not *decline*.
- ***enduring / lasting*** — already spoken for, by two different characters. **久 (*jiǔ*)** is *endure*: 天長地久 (7), *"the sky is lasting, the earth enduring"*, and 道乃久 (16), *"the Tao leads to what endures."* **長 (*cháng*)**, a false friend with the same sound, is *lasting* (說文: 久遠也, *"long and far"*). Chapter 16 sets 常 and 久 in the same chain, and they must stay two words.
- ***the abiding*** — accurate, and once listed beside *the ever-present*. No line uses it, and a second noun for one name would split chapter 16's chain, where 常 is repeated as a subject four times.

### The working register

| Grammar | English | Chapters | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| a name — what something *is called* | **the ever-present** | 16 ×4, 52, 55 ×2 | 知常曰明 → *"knowing the ever-present is called clear-seeing"* |
| before a noun | **ever-present** | 1 ×2, 28 ×3, 46 | 常德不離 → *"the ever-present integrity never leaves you"* |
| adverb, before a 無X or 有X state | **ever** | 1 ×2, 3, 32, 34, 37 | 道常無名 → *"the Tao is ever nameless"* |
| adverb, before any other predicate | **always** | 27 ×2, 48, 51, 61, 64, 65, 74 ×2 | 牝常以靜勝牡 → *"the female always overcomes the male by stillness"* |
| the shallow sense: habit, set custom | **fixed** | 49 | 聖人無常心 → *"the sage has no fixed heart"* |
| folded into the verb | **stays** | 79 | 常與善人 → *"it stays with those who are masterful"* |

**The *ever / always* line is grammatical, not chapter-by-chapter.** *Ever* fuses with a state the way 常 does with 無名, 無欲, 無為 or 無知. That is why 51's 常自然 is *"always so, of itself"*: 自然 (*zì rán*) is a predicate, not a 無X state. *Always* takes a verb. Chapter 64 is the test case: 民之從事，常於幾成而敗之 → *"people always ruin their affairs just short of completing them."* Both excavated copies write 恆 there, and the next line, *"be as careful at the end as at the beginning,"* is the way out of the *always*.

**The shallow sense is the same word at a lower altitude.** 無常心 (49) is a heart with no set habit, and Han Fei's 常所 is a fixed place. These are the everyday 常 of *the usual, the set*. They are not the name of chapter 16, and the English keeps them apart. The silks keep them apart too: chapter 49 there reads 恒無心, *"is ever without a heart [of their own]"*, a different construction recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`.

**Chapter 16 is the hardest line, and it decides the noun.** 復命曰常，知常曰明。不知常，妄作凶。知常容 — 常 is named, then known, then not known, then known again, four times in four lines, each time as a subject or object. Whatever the English is, it must survive being repeated four times as a noun: *"Returning to what is given is called the ever-present. / Knowing the ever-present is called clear-seeing. / Not knowing the ever-present brings reckless action and disaster. / Knowing the ever-present, you contain everything."* *The eternal* would make that a theology. *The constant* would make it a law of physics. *The ever-present* stays something you could notice.

### What no rule can enforce

*Enduring*, *endures* and *lasting* are ruled out for 常, but they cannot go on `forbidden:`. The lock checker keys on chapter presence, and **chapter 16 holds both 常 and 久** (*jiǔ*): 道乃久 is legitimately *"the Tao leads to what endures"*, a few lines below four 常. Forbidding *endure* would flag that line. Chapter 7 has 長 and 久 and no 常, so it would not be flagged, but the 16 case alone makes the ban unenforceable. The reader has to hold this one: **in a chapter with 常, *endure* belongs to 久, and *lasting* to 長.**

### Left open

**The English adds a little.** *Present* foregrounds *here* where 常's core is *still, again, every time*. Han Fei and Heshang Gong license it, but it is their reading, carried into the word. *Ever-present* also has a hymn behind it, Psalm 46's *"a very present help in trouble"*, so it does not pass the hymn test cleanly either. We accept it because every alternative fails worse: *eternal* fails the same test outright, and *constant* fails the pointing test.

**The seam the reader cannot see.** The received text's 常 is two words joined: the moon-word 恒, a manner, and the skirt-graph 常, a name. Our English happens to run *ever* through both, so it holds them together the way the received text does. But it cannot show the reader where the seam is. Chapter 1's first line and chapter 16's chain look like one word in English, and to the people who first wrote them down they were not.
