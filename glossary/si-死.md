## 死 (Sǐ) — Death

*Say "suh," dipping. Eighteen times in sixteen lines across nine chapters. Life's counter-word, and in this book its argument: the soft and the living on one side, the hard and the dead on the other.*

**Locked: 死 → "death" where it is a noun · "die" where it is a verb · "deadly" where it modifies a noun.** *(Shalom's calls, 2026-11-20.)* *(Not "deathless," which turns chapter 6's verb into a permanent attribute. Not "immortal," the later cult of not dying that the book never joins. Not "perish," a second verb for one word.)*

### The graph: bones, and a person parting

**The Shuowen reads it as bones beside a person:**

> 死，澌也，人所離也。从歺从人。
> *"死 is to run dry; it is what a person parts from. From 歺 (è — bare bones) and 人 (rén — a person)."*

**澌 (*sī*) is water used up**, a stream run dry, and it is also a sound-gloss on 死: Xu Shen defines the word by a word that sounds like it. Death is a running out, and a parting. Wiktionary reads the second part differently, as 尸 (*shī*) giving the sound beside 歹 (*dǎi*, the later form of 歺) giving the meaning; either way the bones are what the graph is about. The word itself goes back to Proto-Sino-Tibetan.

**Where the picture stops.** Neither reading puts a mourner, a soul or a passage in the graph. It is remains, and the one who has left them.

### Sixteen lines, sorted by what the word is doing

**A noun: *death*.** Something feared, made light of, found heavy, entered, followed.

> *Emerge into life, enter into death.*
> *Three in ten people are followers of life.*
> *Three in ten are followers of death.*
>
> 出生入死。
> 生之徒，十有三；
> 死之徒，十有三；
>
> — chapter 50

> *When the people do not fear death,*
> *why try to frighten them with death?*
>
> 民不畏死，
> 奈何以死懼之？
>
> — chapter 74

> *The people make light of death*
> *because those over them seek life too thickly.*
>
> 民之輕死，
> 以其上求生之厚，
>
> — chapter 75

> *In life a person is soft and yielding.*
> *In death, hard and strong.*
>
> 人之生也柔弱，
> 其死也堅強。
>
> — chapter 76

> *Let the people find death heavy, and not move far away.*
>
> 使民重死而不遠徙。
>
> — chapter 80

**A verb: *die*.**

> *The spirit of the valley does not die.*
> *This is called the dark female.*
>
> 谷神不死，
> 是謂玄牝。
>
> — chapter 6

> *One who dies but does not vanish lives long.*
>
> 死而不亡者壽。
>
> — chapter 33

> *The people grow old and die*
> *and never once go next door.*
>
> 民至老死，
> 不相往來。
>
> — chapter 80

Chapter 42's 強梁者不得其死 (*qiáng liáng zhě bù dé qí sǐ* — "the strong and rigid do not get their death") is an idiom for dying before one's span, and reads *"do not die a natural death"*; `glossary/de-shi-得失.md` holds the line. Chapter 67's 死矣 (*sǐ yǐ* — "dead, then") closes the undoing of the three treasures as a verdict: *"that is death."*

**Modifying a noun: *deadly*.**

> *Three in ten, though alive, move toward deadly ground.*
>
> 人之生，動之於死地，亦十有三。
>
> — chapter 50

> *Why? Because in them there is no deadly ground.*
>
> 夫何故？以其無死地。
>
> — chapter 50

**死地 (*sǐ dì* — death-ground) is a place where one is likely to die**, Sunzi's word for the ground an army fights on or perishes. 死 modifies 地 (*dì* — ground), and English says that with *deadly*, as of a cliff or a pass. Heshang Gong carries the phrase out of chapter 50 into two other chapters: of those who abandon the treasures, 所行如此，動入死地 (*"acting so, they move into deadly ground,"* 67), and of the people who make light of death, 以求生太厚之故，輕入死地也 (*"because they seek life too richly, they enter deadly ground lightly,"* 75).

**The grammar decides every line.** *Death* and *die* are one word in two parts of speech, and *deadly* is the same word set before a noun. The three are kept to their grammar so that the noun lines, where most of the book's argument happens, all say the same thing.

### Chapter 6: the spirit of the valley does not die

**不死 (*bù sǐ*) is a verb, negated**, and the English keeps it one: *does not die*, as every other verbal 死 in the book reads *die*. *Deathless* made the spirit's not-dying a fixed attribute, in a word from poetic and devotional English (*deathless glory*), and it leaned the line toward an immortal. The book's not-dying is of a piece with chapter 7's living long *because they do not live for themselves* (以其不自生, *yǐ qí bù zì shēng*): something ongoing, said of a valley.

**Heshang Gong reads the line as a practice of not dying:** 谷，養也。人能養神則不死也 — *"谷 means to nourish. If a person can nourish the spirit, they do not die."* That reading takes the valley out of the line, and Shalom's call of 2026-10-25 keeps 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) a noun (`glossary/gu-谷.md`). *Does not die* stays with the valley, and does not promise the reader immortality either.

### Life against death: the book takes a side, and it is not the one the living expect

**Chapter 50 counts.** Three in ten follow life, three in ten follow death, and three in ten *though alive, move toward deadly ground*. Why? *Because they live life too thickly*, 以其生生之厚 (*yǐ qí shēng shēng zhī hòu*). Wang Bi: 善攝生者無以生為生，故無死地也 — *"one masterful at tending life does not make living their life, and so they have no deadly ground."* He gives the picture the chapter goes on to draw: the rhinoceros finds nowhere to drive its horn, the tiger nowhere to set its claws, *"so what deadly ground is there?"* (何死地之有乎). The deadly ground is in the person, and it is the grasping at life.

**Chapter 75 says the same of a people.** They make light of death *because those over them seek life too thickly*: the rulers' 求生之厚 (*qiú shēng zhī hòu*) is chapter 50's 生生之厚 seen from below, and the English now says so in the same words. And chapter 74 says what follows: a people who no longer fear death cannot be governed by it. Heshang Gong: 治國者刑罰酷深，民不聊生，故不畏死也 — *"when the state is governed with harsh punishments, the people cannot bear to live, and so they do not fear death."*

**Chapter 76 makes it a body.** The living are soft, the dead are hard; the living grass is fragile, the dead is withered. 堅強者死之徒，柔弱者生之徒 (*jiān qiáng zhě sǐ zhī tú, róu ruò zhě shēng zhī tú* — "the hard and strong are followers of death; the soft and yielding are followers of life"), chapter 50's *followers* again. Heshang Gong: 人死和氣竭，精神亡 — *"at death the harmonious breath is spent, and the vital spirit is gone."*

**And chapter 33 sets the limit.** 死而不亡者壽 — *"one who dies but does not vanish lives long."* The book does not say anyone escapes dying. Wang Bi: 雖死而以為生之道不亡，乃得全其壽 — *"though they die, the Tao by which they lived does not vanish, and so they complete their span."*

**生 (*shēng*) is the other half of every one of these lines**, and its entry holds its sense there: *life, the living*, beside the mother's *give birth to* (`glossary/sheng-生.md`).

### What is set aside

**"Deathless."** Chapter 6's former English. It turns a verb into an attribute, and an attribute into a halo: *deathless* belongs to elegy and hymn.

**"Immortal," "immortality."** The Han and later practice of not dying, which Heshang Gong's chapter 6 already leans toward. The book's spirit of the valley does not die; it is not promised to anyone who nourishes it. Refused for 長 (*cháng* — long) too (`glossary/chang-長.md`).

**"Perish."** A second verb for one word, and Latin. It would also crowd 亡 (*wáng* — to vanish), the word chapter 33 sets against 死.

**"Demise," "passing," "the grave," "mortality."** Euphemism or abstraction. The graph is bones; the English says *death*.

**"Lethal," "fatal," for 死地.** *Lethal* is a toxicologist's word, and *fatal* means certain death. 死地 is ground where death is likely, which is what *deadly* says of a place.

### What no rule can enforce

***Die* is not 死's alone.** Chapter 39's 將恐滅 (*jiāng kǒng miè* — "likely to be extinguished") reads *"likely to die out,"* for 滅 (*miè* — to put out). Chapter 39 has no 死, so no rule sees it.

***Natural* at chapter 42 is supplied.** It renders the idiom, not a character, and 自然 (*zì rán* — the self-so) is not in chapter 42, so no check compares the two.

### Left open

**Chapter 50's 十有三: three in ten, or thirteen.** The verse follows Wang Bi, 十分有三分, *"three parts in ten."* Heshang Gong counts thirteen, 九竅四關 (*jiǔ qiào sì guān* — "the nine openings and the four gates"): the body's apertures and limbs, which serve life when kept and death when spent. Two readings of one number, and the chapter reads differently under each.

**Chapter 67's verdict.** 死矣 is two characters, and *that is death* makes it a sentence about the outcome. Heshang Gong reads it as movement, *"they move into deadly ground,"* which would tie it to chapter 50's ground. Wang Bi says nothing. The verdict stands.
