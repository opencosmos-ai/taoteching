## 辱 (Rǔ) — Soiled, Disgrace

*Say "roo," falling and rising. Six lines across four chapters. A mark on what should be clean, or on a name, and the book asks you to hold to it.*

**Locked: 辱 → "soiled" beside white (28, 41), and "disgrace" beside favor or as what befalls (13, 44).** *(Shalom's calls: soiled at 28, 2026-10-25; the split and the lock, 2026-11-20.)* **垢 (*gòu* — grime) is covered here**: at chapter 78 the verse follows the silks' 詬 (*gòu* — reviling), *shame* (Shalom's call, 2026-10-25). *(Not "humiliation," which needs someone humbling you. Not "dishonor," which brings a code of honor. Not "ignominy," a court's word. Not "remorse," which is felt within, where 辱 is done in sight of others.)*

### The graph: a hand with a farming blade, and a punishment at the field's edge

**The oracle-bone 辱 is a hand beneath 辰 (*chén*), a farming blade**, in origin a clam-shell hoe, and its first sense was to till or to weed, later written 耨 (*nòu* — to hoe) (`zdic`, `dong-chinese`). The Shuowen keeps the field and gives the word its later sense:

> 辱，恥也。从寸在辰下。失耕時，於封畺上戮之也。辰者，農之時也。
> *"辱 is shame. From 寸 (cùn — a hand) beneath 辰. Whoever missed the ploughing season was 戮 (lù — punished, put to shame) on the field's boundary. 辰 is the farmer's season."*

**The story has two halves, and the word keeps both.** The fault is the farmer's own: they missed the time. The 辱 is what follows, done to them in public, at the boundary mound where everyone can see. It is not a feeling the farmer has. It is a mark put on them. **And it follows their own failure to keep time**, which is the shape of chapter 44, where disgrace follows not knowing when you have enough. *(Shalom asked whether the story points to remorse rather than disgrace. The fault is inward and the consequence outward, and 辱 names the consequence.)*

**The sound family is soil and damp.** 辱 gives the sound to 蓐 (*rù* — the Shuowen's 陳草復生也, *"old grass growing again"*, later a mat of grass) and 溽 (*rù* — 溼暑也, *"damp heat"*). Whether the sound carries the soil, or only the sound, is not known. **Where the picture stops:** the book never shows the field. Its 辱 is a stain on white, or a mark on a name.

### Six lines, and the partner decides

> *Favor and disgrace are both a shock.*
>
> 寵辱若驚，
>
> — chapter 13

> *Know when you have enough, and you are never disgraced.*
> *Know when to stop, and you come to no danger.*
>
> 知足不辱，
> 知止不殆，
>
> — chapter 44

> *The highest integrity seems a valley.*
> *The whitest white seems soiled.*
>
> 上德若谷，
> 大白若辱，
>
> — chapter 41

> *Know the white, hold to the soiled,*
>
> 知其榮，守其辱，
>
> — chapter 28

**Beside a social word, it is disgrace. Beside white, it is a stain.** Chapter 13 sets 辱 against 寵 (*chǒng* — favor), three times, and favor is given by others. Chapter 44 opens on *"Your name or your body: which is dearer?"*, and 辱 is what befalls the name. Chapter 41 sets it against white, and Heshang Gong reads a surface: 大潔白之人若汙辱 (*"the person of great clean whiteness seems sullied"*). Chapter 28 is the fork: our base pairs it with 榮 (*róng* — glory), and the verse follows the silks' 白 (*bái* — white).

**The commentators agree on each side of the split.** On chapter 13, Heshang Gong: 身寵亦驚，身辱亦驚 (*"favored, one is shocked; disgraced, one is shocked"*). Wang Bi: 寵必有辱，榮必有患。驚辱等，榮患同也 (*"favor must carry disgrace, and glory trouble; being startled by disgrace is the same, glory and trouble are one"*). On 44, Heshang Gong: 知足之人絕利去欲，不辱於身 (*"one who knows they have enough cuts off profit and wanting, and is not disgraced in their person"*).

### Chapter 28: white, or glory

**Both Mawangdui silks and the *Zhuangzi*'s last chapter read 知其白，守其辱** (*"know the white, hold to the soiled"*); **the *Huainanzi* (139 BCE) already reads 榮**, spells the valley 穀 (*gǔ* — grain), and tells the line as King Wen of Zhou, who after his release from prison let himself be thought less than he was, *"to wait for the tyrant's failure."* That is disgrace accepted as strategy. The change from white to glory happened between the silks and the *Huainanzi*, inside a century, and it turned a stain into a standing. **The verse follows the silks** (Shalom's call, 2026-10-25; `notes/manuscript.md` → *Ch 28*).

**Even on the received text, Heshang Gong reads the soil:** 辱以喻汚濁 (*"辱 figures the dirty and the murky"*), and 知己之有榮貴，當守之以汚濁 (*"knowing you have glory and rank, hold to it with dirt and murk"*). On chapter 41: 上德之人若深谷，不恥垢濁也 (*"the person of highest integrity is like a deep valley, and is not ashamed of grime and murk"*).

### 垢 (gòu — grime) at chapter 78

> *whoever takes on the shame of the state*
> *is called the keeper of the soil and the grain.*
>
> 受國之垢，
> 是謂社稷主；
>
> — chapter 78

**The received text's figure for accepted disgrace is grime.** The Shuowen: 垢，濁也。从土 (*"垢 is murk; from 土 (tǔ — earth)"*). Heshang Gong: 君能愛國垢濁者，若江海不逆小流 (*"a ruler who can love the state's grime and murk is like rivers and seas, which do not refuse the small streams"*). **But both silks write 訽, a form of 詬 (*gòu* — reviling, shame)**, the Shuowen's 恥 (*chǐ* — shame) built on 言 (*yán* — speech), and the *Zhuangzi* echoes the line as 受天下之詬 (*"take on the world's reviling"*). The verse follows the silks: *shame* (Shalom's call, 2026-10-25; `sources/variants.yaml`, ch 78). 垢 lives here because it is the received text's other word for what chapter 28 holds to, and it stands nowhere else in the book.

### What is set aside

**"Remorse," "regret."** The feeling of one's own fault. The Shuowen's farmer was at fault, and the 辱 is what was done to them on the boundary. Every line in the book is outward: a favor's opposite, a name's injury, a stain seen.

**"Shame" for 辱.** The Shuowen's own gloss, and not wrong. It is kept for 詬 at chapter 78, where the silks put shame into words, so that each English stays one character's.

**"Humiliation," "humiliate."** Requires someone doing the humbling. Chapter 44's disgrace has no agent: it follows from not knowing enough.

**"Dishonor."** Brings a code of honor, and with it a European gentleman's world.

**"Ignominy," "infamy."** A court's and a chronicle's words; they fail the pointing test.

**"Dirt," "dirty," "stained."** *Soiled* keeps both the cloth and the name (*a soiled reputation*); *dirty* keeps only the cloth, and *stained* tilts toward guilt.

### What no rule can enforce

**The split is by partner, not by chapter**, so it is written in `render:` and held by the reader. A line that put *disgrace* beside white, or *soiled* beside favor, would pass every check.

**垢 has no lock of its own.** A cover is not gated. `concordance.py --english "shame"` is the check, and it should find chapter 78 alone.

### Left open

**Jiao Hong's ancient form at chapter 41.** The Siku Wang Bi notes 〔案辱焦竑云古作…〕, *"for 辱, Jiao Hong says it was anciently written as…"*, and the graph is lost in our vendored text. Which graph he named, and whether it changes the soiling, is unread.

**Chapter 13's fork.** Our base reads 寵為下 (*"favor is the lower position"*); Heshang Gong's lemma reads 辱為下 (*"disgrace is the lower"*). The English follows the base with Wang Bi, who makes favor and disgrace one condition (`notes/manuscript.md` → *Ch 13*).

**Whether chapter 28 still holds a name.** *Soiled* keeps a soiled name inside the stain, and the *Huainanzi*'s glory makes the name the whole point. The verse chose the silks' stain; the name is the shadow it casts.
