## 虛 (Xū) — Empty

*Say "shü," level. Five occurrences across five chapters, and four of them are a verb or an adjective.*

**Recommended: 虛 → "empty" (adjective), "to empty" (verb).** *(Never "emptiness," never "the void," never "vacuity," never "nothingness.")* *Chapter 16 is the one line still unsettled; see the close.*

說文解字 (*Shuōwén Jiězì*, c. 100 CE): **大丘也。崐崘丘謂之崐崘虛。古者九夫爲井，四井爲邑，四邑爲丘。丘謂之虛。从丘虍聲。**

*"A great mound. The Kunlun mound is called the Kunlun 虛. In antiquity nine men's allotments made a 井 (jǐng — a well-field), four well-fields made a 邑 (yì — a settlement), four settlements made a 丘 (qiū — a mound). A 丘 is called 虛. From 丘 (qiū — mound), with 虍 (hū — tiger) for sound."*

**虛 is a unit of surveyed land.** The dictionary does not reach for a void; it reaches for the well-field system and counts up from nine households. What makes a settlement-mound *虛* is that the people have gone — the sense later given its own graph, 墟 (*xū* — a ruin, an abandoned site).

So the picture is not boundless space. It is **a bounded place that has been vacated**: walls still standing, nobody home. Everything else in this entry follows from that.

### The book uses it exactly that way, five times

Every occurrence is a container with its contents gone, and every container is a thing you could point at.

**A heart.** 虛其心，實其腹 — *"empty their hearts, fill their bellies"* (ch 3). 虛 is **transitive** here; it takes an object. 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, d. 249 CE) makes the anatomy explicit: 心懷智而腹懷食，虛有智而實無知也 — *"the heart harbors 智 (zhì — clever knowing) and the belly harbors food; empty what has 智, fill what has no 知 (zhī — knowing)."* 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*, Han) gives the method: 除嗜欲去亂煩 — *"remove craving and desire, cast off disorder and vexation."*

**A bellows.** 天地之間，其猶橐籥乎？虛而不屈，動而愈出 — *"between sky and earth, is it not like a bellows? Empty and never bent shut; the more it moves, the more comes out"* (ch 5). 王弼: 橐排橐也，籥樂籥也。橐籥之中空洞無情無為，故虛而不得窮屈 — *"橐 (tuó) is the blacksmith's bellows-bag, 籥 (yuè) is the musical pipe. Inside the bellows and the pipe it is 空洞 (kōng dòng — hollow and cavernous), without feeling, without doing; therefore it is empty and cannot be used up and bent shut."* **He defines 虛 by what is inside a made object.**

**Words.** 豈虛言哉 — *"were those empty words?"* (ch 22). A saying with nothing in it.

**A granary.** 倉甚虛 — *"the granaries are empty"* (ch 53). The most carefully measured container an agrarian state owns, and the line is an indictment: the grain is gone because it was taken.

**And its opposite is a full vessel.** 盈 (*yíng*), 虛's antonym at chapters 4 and 45, is 說文 滿器也 — *"a full vessel. From 皿 (mǐn — a vessel)."* Vessel-language on both sides of the pair.

### Why the noun is a Buddhist import

**English *emptiness* is the standard rendering of śūnyatā**, the central term of Mahāyāna Buddhist metaphysics — the doctrine that phenomena lack inherent existence. In Chinese that term is **空 (*kōng* — empty)**, and 虛空 (*xū kōng*) renders ākāśa, unbounded space.

**空 does not occur in the Tao Te Ching. Not once, in eighty-one chapters.**

Buddhism reaches China in roughly the first century CE; this text is Warring States, some four centuries earlier. So an English reader meeting *emptiness* here is handed a technical term from a tradition that had not yet arrived — and handed it in the one grammatical form, the abstract noun, that turns a description into a doctrine. It is the same failure as 一 (*yī* — one) rendered *the One*, which imports Neoplatonism, and 道 (*dào*) rendered *the Way*, which imports the missionaries.

**It also imports the wrong physics.** Śūnyatā is a claim that things have no self-standing essence. 虛 is a claim about a container: it has room in it. The bellows is not insubstantial. It is a made object with a hollow inside, and the hollow is what makes it work.

### What is set aside

**emptiness** — the abstract noun, and śūnyatā's English. The adjective and the verb carry everything this book needs.

**the void, vacuity** — *the void* is already refused for 無 (*wú* — absence) in `wu-you-無有.md`, and it is worse here: 虛 is a hollow with walls, and a void has none. *Vacuity* additionally carries a slur in modern English that the Chinese does not.

**nothingness** — 虛 is never nothing. It is a something with room in it, which is the whole point of the bellows.

**hollow** — belongs to 窪 (*wā* — a low, sunken place) at chapter 22: 窪則盈, *"be hollowed out, and be filled."* **Chapter 22 contains both 窪 and 虛**, so this is precisely the distinction a `forbidden:` list cannot express — the checker gates on whether a character is present, and both are. It has to be held by the reader.

| Form | Chapters | English |

| 虛 as the object of a verb | 16 | *(unsettled — see below)* |

### The tension, left open

**Chapter 16 is the only place in the book where 虛 has to be a noun**, and it is therefore the only place the Buddhist reading can get in. 致 (*zhì* — to send all the way there) 虛 (*xū*) 極 (*jí* — the far end) puts 虛 in the object slot, and English wants *emptiness* there.

The way out is to decline the noun and let 虛 be what it is in four lines out of five — **a verb**. Chapter 3's 虛其心 licenses the transitive directly, and 河上公 glosses chapter 16 the same way: 捐 (*juān* — shed) 情 (*qíng* — feeling) 去 (*qù* — cast off) 欲 (*yù* — desire)，五內 (*wǔ nèi* — the five viscera) 清 (*qīng* — clear) 淨 (*jìng* — still). What the object should be, and whether it may be supplied at all where the Chinese elides it, is undecided.

**沖 (*chōng*) may not be an emptiness word at all.** 說文 gives it as 涌搖也。从水、中 — *"surging and shaking. From 水 (shuǐ — water) and 中 (zhōng — middle),"* read like 動 (*dòng* — to move). Its empty sense at chapter 4's 道沖 depends on reading it as 盅 (*zhōng* — an empty vessel), a textual judgment `sources/variants.yaml` has not recorded either way. Water that surges and a vessel that is empty are not the same picture.

**寥 (*liáo* — vast, unpeopled)** at chapter 25 is a third word in the same field, and 說文 has no entry for it under any graph the importer can verify.
