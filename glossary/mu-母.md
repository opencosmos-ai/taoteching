## 母 (Mǔ) — The Mother

*Say "moo," the third tone — it dips and then lifts. One of the Tao's truest names, and the one translators most often flinch from.*

Here, for once, the plain English word is exactly right. 母 is *mother*, and no cleverness improves on it. So the danger in this entry is not mistranslation — it is **abstraction**. For centuries, uneasy with a cosmos that has breasts and nurses its young, translators have quietly cooled 母 into "the Source," "the Origin," "the Matrix," "the Ground of Being" — each of them a way of holding the warmth and the body at arm's length. The work of this entry is the opposite of clever. It is to refuse the flinch and let her stay what she is: a mother. Bodily, generative, nursing — and, this is the hardest part, utterly unsentimental.

### The graph

Set the character beside its neighbor. 女 (nǚ) is *woman* — the old graph draws a figure kneeling, arms folded across the body. 母 is that very same figure, with **two dots added at the breast**. That is the entire difference. The mark that turns *woman* into *mother* is the breasts: 母 is specifically the one who has borne a child and *feeds it from her own body*. The character will not let you forget the body. Before "mother" is an idea, it is milk. Whatever cosmology we raise on this word, it rests on a nursing woman.

### What 母 does in the Daodejing

The Tao's oldest and warmest name is not "the Way." It is **the mother**. She is 萬物之母, the mother of the countless things (1); 天下母, the mother of the world (25) — "mixed and complete, born before sky and earth," and able to bear it all. In Chapter 20 the sage marks themselves off from everyone precisely by *how they feed*: while the crowd gorges on the world's spectacle, 我獨異於人，而貴食母 — "I alone differ from others: I prize **feeding from the mother**." The image is exact and unembarrassed — the sage as an infant at the breast, drawing sustenance straight from her, while the world feasts on noise. And in Chapter 59 she is the **root**: 有國之母，可以長久 — "Have the mother of the state, and you can last long": "the Tao of deep roots, firm base, long life."

### The return — womb and refuge (52)

Her deepest chapter is the fifty-second, and it is the most consoling movement in the whole book. 天下有始，以為天下母 — "The world has a beginning, which serves as the mother of the world." 既得其母，以知其子；既知其子，復守其母，沒身不殆 — *"Find our mother, and you know her children. Know her children, return and hold fast to our mother, and to the end of your days, no danger will find you."* Here she is not only where you came *from*; she is where you go *back to*. The countless things are her children (子), the whole manifest world her brood — and the movement of wisdom is to turn from the children back toward the one who bore them: to return, and hold, and rest. Womb and refuge at once — origin and destination, the mother you were born from and the mother you can always come home to. No danger, to the end.

### 始 (shǐ — beginning)

*Say "shr," dipping. Eight lines across seven chapters. Locked: 始 → "beginning," and "begin" as a verb.* *(Shalom's call at chapter 52, 2026-11-19, the one line that read otherwise.)*

> 始，女之初也。从女，台聲。
> *"始: the beginning of a woman. From 女 (nǚ — woman), with 台 (tái) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

**The beginning is filed under the same kneeling woman as the mother.** 母 is 女 with two dots at the breast; 始 is 女 with a sound beside her. What exactly the Shuowen means by *a woman's beginning* is not certain, and this entry does not guess. What the graph shows is enough: the book's word for *beginning* and its word for *mother* are both built on a woman, and the book sets them side by side twice.

> *The nameless is the beginning of sky and earth.*
> *The named is the mother of the countless things.*
>
> 無名，天地之始；
> 有名，萬物之母。
>
> — chapter 1

> *The world has a beginning, which serves as the mother of the world.*
>
> 天下有始，以為天下母。
>
> — chapter 52

**A beginning is a moment. A mother is a body that stays.** Wang Bi reads chapter 1's couplet as two times in one life: 凡有皆始於無，故未形無名之時，則為萬物之始；及其有形有名之時，則長之育之亭之毒之，為其母也, *"all that is begins in what is not. So in the time before form and name, it is the beginning of the countless things; when there is form and name, it grows them, raises them, settles them, matures them: it is their mother."* His four verbs are chapter 51's own (*"grows and raises them, settles and matures them"*). The beginning happens once. The mother goes on feeding.

**Chapter 52 joins the two in one line**, and the joint is 以為 (*yǐ wéi* — "takes as," "serves as"): the world has a beginning, and that beginning serves as its mother. The moment is taken up into a body. That is why the next lines can say *return*: no one goes back to a moment, but you can go back to a mother. Wang Bi: 母，本也；子，末也, *"the mother is the root; the children are the branch tips."* Heshang Gong reads the beginning itself as presence: 始有道也, *"the beginning is: there is the Tao."*

**Everywhere else 始 is a plain beginning, and it never takes a capital or a cosmos.** Chapter 14's 古始 (*gǔ shǐ*) is *the ancient beginning*; chapter 38's 愚之始 (*yú zhī shǐ*) is *the beginning of foolishness*; chapter 64 has it twice, *the thousand-mile journey begins beneath your feet* and 慎終如始 (*shèn zhōng rú shǐ* — "be as careful at the end as at the beginning"), where it stands against 終 (*zhōng* — end). As a verb it is chapter 32's 始制有名 (*shǐ zhì yǒu míng* — "when the carving begins, there are names") and chapter 2's *the sage does not begin them*.

**Set aside, for 始:**

- **"origin."** Chapter 52's English until 2026-11-19. It is the cooling this entry refuses for 母, *the Origin* without its capital: a word with no arms, standing in the same line as the mother it is meant to become. And it breaks the echo. Chapter 52 sets down the same two characters chapter 1 does, and *beginning* lets a reader hear chapter 1 again; *origin* hides it.
- **"in the beginning."** Genesis 1:1, and John 1:1, whose Logos the Chinese Union Bible renders 道 (太初有道). Already refused at chapter 39 (`notes/translation.md`).
- **"the primordial," "genesis," "dawn."** The first was this edition's chapter 14 until it was taken out (`WORKLIST-CLOSED.md` T1-11). The other two are the same move in other coats, a beginning turned into an epoch.

### What is set aside

So we keep every warm and bodily thing the tradition has tried to abstract away. We do not render 母 as "the Source" (a word with no body), "the Origin" (a word with no arms), or "the Ground of Being" (a phrase that has never nursed anything). We keep the breasts the character draws. We keep the milk of 食母. We keep the womb of 玄牝 (the dark female, 6) and the children of 子. The Tao is not an impersonal principle a translator has dressed, for warmth, in a mother's robe. Laozi says the reverse: reach for the truest name you have for what bears all things and clings to none — and the word that comes is *mother*.

### Fierce, not fond

And now the hard honesty, without which this entry becomes a greeting card. **The cosmic mother is not sentimental.** The same book that calls her the mother of the world also says 天地不仁 — sky and earth are *not concerned with humaneness*, not doting; they treat the countless things as straw dogs (5). Her love, if we call it that, is not the hovering fondness we pour into the word "mother." It is 生而不有 — she gives birth and does not possess; she bears you, nurses you, and *lets you go*. She does not cling, does not coddle, does not shield you from the weather. Her tenderness is the fierce, impartial, inexhaustible giving of one who pours out life to all and keeps none of it. That is a deeper tenderness than sentiment — the love that can release what it made. To nurse from *this* mother is to be fed, and then set free.

### Left open

**On the capital, which she does not take.** *(Decided 2026-08-11, after argument; this entry had capitalized her for months without a ruling.)* She is rendered **mother**, lowercase, throughout. Chapter 25 offers her as a role — 可以為天下母, *"it can serve as the mother of the world"* — and then says in the next breath 吾不知其名, *"I do not know its name,"* before coining 道 under protest. All seven occurrences are genitive, possessive, or governed by 為 (*serve as*); she is never addressed and never stands as a bare subject. Heshang Gong makes it a simile outright — 如母之養子, *"like a mother nourishing her child."* And the capital would work against her: in English it confers institutional status rather than intimacy, lifting her out of the nursing body that the graph's two dots exist to draw. 道 keeps its capital because *"Tao"* is transliterated, not translated; every English word here is lowercase. *(Full reasoning in `notes/translation.md` → Typography.)*

**Chapter 2's 始 is a reading, not the received text.** Our base prints 萬物作而弗始 (*wàn wù zuò ér fú shǐ* — "the countless things arise, and the sage does not begin them"), which is the silks' reading. Wang Bi's text and Heshang Gong's both print 萬物作焉而不辭 (*wàn wù zuò yān ér bù cí* — "the countless things arise, and the sage does not refuse them"), with no 始 at all. Nothing in `sources/variants.yaml` records the choice yet (`WORKLIST.md` T2-87).

**The silks put 始 and 母 on the same object.** At chapter 1 both read 無名萬物之始也 (*"the nameless is the beginning of the countless things"*), so the beginning and the mother both belong to the countless things, and Wang Bi's own comment says 萬物之始 too. Our base keeps 天地之始, *of sky and earth* (`WORKLIST.md` T2-80).

Is she a metaphor, or the thing itself? Laozi hedges on purpose. The Tao *can be called* the mother of the world (可以為天下母) — and in nearly the same breath (1) he warns that no name holds it. So the mother is a name that points *truly* without *caging*: the most maternal thing we know, aimed at what has no name. We hold her the way Chapter 52 says to — not gripping the idea, but returning to the reality, and resting there. Her name is not the Tao. But of all the names, it may be the one that loves it best.
