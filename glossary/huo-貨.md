## 貨 (Huò) — Goods

*Say "hwaw," falling. Five appearances across five chapters — 3, 12, 44, 53, 64. Three of them are the same four-character phrase, and that phrase carries one of the book's sharpest political arguments.*

**Locked: 貨 → "goods." · 難得之貨 → "rare goods."** *(Not "treasure," not "the precious," not "wealth.")*

---

### Scarcity is a fact; the value is conferred

Chapter 3 says 不貴難得之貨，使民不為盜 — *"do not prize 難得之貨, and the people do not become thieves."* The line is a causal claim about **what a ruler does**, and the standard English quietly destroys it:

> *"By not valuing **the precious**, no one becomes a thief."*

If the goods are *already precious*, the advice is circular. **Do not prize the prized.** It shrinks to a shrug about austerity, and the ruler drops out of the sentence — which is the one place the sentence is pointing.

The Chinese does not say the goods are precious. It says they are **難得** (*nán dé* — hard to obtain), which is a fact about **acquisition**, not a verdict about **worth**. The argument runs:

> **the goods are hard to get → the ruler prizes them → people steal.**

The scarcity is a fact about the world. **The value is conferred by the prizing.** 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*, d. 249 CE) states the causation in four characters and then follows it to its end: 貴貨過用，貪者競趣，穿窬探篋，沒命而盜 — *"prize goods **beyond their usefulness**, and the greedy race for them: they tunnel through walls, rifle chests, and steal at the cost of their lives."* 貴貨過用 (*guì huò guò yòng*) is the cause, and its subject is the ruler.

河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*, Han) supplies the image, on chapter 64: 聖人不眩為服，不賤石而貴玉 — *"the sage is not dazzled into adorning themselves; does not despise stone and prize jade."* **賤 (*jiàn* — to hold cheap) and 貴 (*guì* — to prize) are both things a person does.** Stone and jade differ in what someone does about them.

---

### The character: a shell that changes hands

貨 is 貝 (*bèi* — a cowrie shell) with 化 (*huà*) above it. 說文解字 (*Shuōwén Jiězì*, c. 100 CE): **財也。从貝化聲** — *"property. It follows 貝, with 化 as the sound."*

**貝 is a drawing of a cowrie shell**, and it is not a metaphor. Cowries were currency in the Shang and early Zhou, strung and counted, and they are why the whole vocabulary of value in Chinese is built on this one radical.

**化 is two people, one of them upside down.** 說文: 教行也。从𠤎从人 — *"teaching put into practice. It follows 𠤎 and 人."* 人 (*rén*) is a person standing; 𠤎 is the same figure inverted. The graph is **a body turning over** — transformation, one state becoming another. It is the 化 of 自化 (*zì huà* — "transform of themselves"), which chapter 37 and chapter 57 make the whole point of good government.

So even taken as a pure sound-element, 化 sits over the shell in a word for **things that circulate**. 貨 is money in motion. Goods that change hands.

### The three 貝 characters, and why English has three words for them

The book uses three characters built on the cowrie, and our English had been shuffling them.

| | 說文 | what it is | our word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **貨** (*huò*) | 財也 — *"property"* | 貝 + 化: a shell that **changes hands** | **goods** |
| **寶** (*bǎo*) | 珍也 — *"a precious thing"* | 宀 (*mián* — a roof) over 王 (jade) and 貝 (cowrie): **a hoard that does not move** | **treasure** |
| **貴** (*guì*) | 物不賤也 — *"a thing that is not cheap"* | a **position on a price scale**, paired against 賤 (*jiàn* — cheap; 說文 賈少也, *"small in price"*) | **precious · prize** |

**貨 moves, 寶 sits still, 貴 is a rating someone assigns.** They are not three words for the same idea, and the differences are exactly what chapter 3 is arguing about. Giving 貨 the word for a hoard (*treasure*) or the word for a rating (*precious*) collapses the very distinction the line depends on.

---

### Where it appears

**Three of the five are one stock phrase**, 難得之貨, always under a verb of prizing or in a list of what corrupts:

> 不貴**難得之貨**，使民不為盜 (3) — *not prizing rare goods, the people do not become thieves.*
> **難得之貨**，令人行妨 (12) — *rare goods make conduct go wrong.*
> 是以聖人欲不欲，不貴**難得之貨** (64) — *so the sage wants the not-wanting, and does not prize rare goods.*

**The other two are ordinary and concrete.** Chapter 44 sets it against the body: 身與貨孰多 — *"the self or goods, which is worth more?"* Chapter 53 puts it at the end of a list of a ruler's excesses: 財貨有餘 — *"wealth and goods in surplus"*, where 財 (*cái* — property) and 貨 form a bound compound and each keeps its own English.

**What the goods actually are** is not left to guessing. 河上公 names them on chapter 3: 好珎寶，黃金棄於山，珠玉捐於淵 — *"fine treasures; gold left in the mountain, pearls and jade abandoned in the deep."* Gold, pearls, jade. **Nobody needs jade.** That single fact decides several of the rejected renderings below.

### The thief was drooling

說文 on 盜 (*dào* — thief), the word chapter 3 says the ruler creates: **私利物也。从㳄，㳄欲皿者** — *"to take private profit from a thing. It follows 㳄 (*xián* — drool), the drooling of one who wants the dish."*

A thief, in the oldest analysis of the graph, is **someone salivating over a vessel.** The theft begins in the wanting, and chapter 3's third line says where the wanting begins: 不見可欲，使民心不亂 — *"do not display what can be desired, and the people's hearts are not thrown into disorder."* Display, then appetite, then theft. The chapter blames the first link, and the character agrees with it.

---

### What is set aside, and why

**"precious" — it is 貴's word, and it makes the line circular.** 貴 renders *precious* at chapters 70, 72 and 75, and *prize* as a verb at 20 and 64. In chapters 3 and 64 貴 is the verb standing directly over 貨, so *"do not prize precious goods"* puts one character's English on top of another and argues in a circle. **Forbidding it here is safe**: 貴 occurs in chapters 3 and 64 *only* inside 不貴, verbal both times, so no legitimate claimant for *precious* exists in either chapter.

**"treasure" / "treasures" — it is 寶's word.** 寶 has chapters 62, 67 and 69 and never shares a chapter with 貨, so the words can be kept apart cleanly. And the senses genuinely differ: 寶 is a hoard under a roof, the three 寶 of chapter 67 that one 持而保之 (*chí ér bǎo zhī* — "holds and keeps safe"). 貨 is the opposite motion.

**"scarce" — it imports a shortage the chapter denies.** *Scarce* is relational: not enough of a thing for what is wanted. But nobody needs jade. Call the goods scarce and the people's wanting becomes **natural, a real shortfall honestly felt** — and then the ruler cannot be blamed for it, which unmakes the whole stanza. Chapter 12 says the same from the other end: 腹 (*fù* — belly), the organ that **can** be satisfied, against 目 (*mù* — eye), the one that cannot. Scarcity is a fact about bellies. These chapters are about eyes. *(Secondarily: scarcity is the founding term of modern economics and drags that apparatus into a Warring States line.)*

**"wealth" — it belongs to 富 (*fù*), and to 財 in the one chapter they share.** 富 carries *wealth · wealthy · enrich* at chapters 9, 33 and 57. Chapter 44 had 貨 wearing that word and it has been swept to *goods*.

**"hard to get" / "hard to come by" — blocked, and instructively.** These are the literal sense of 難得, and they cannot be used: **"hard" is 堅 (*jiān* — rigid)'s word**, settled across chapters 36, 43, 76 and 78, where 堅強 (*jiān qiáng*) is what a corpse is. And 難's own English elsewhere is *difficult* (chapters 2, 63, 73), which will not go attributive in verse without grinding. Both routes close.

**"luxuries," "rarities," "coveted," "costly" — each puts the verdict back in the noun.** *Luxury* is Latin *luxuria*, excess, and it does the chapter's work for it: Laozi's claim is that these are ordinary goods that prizing turns into objects of theft, not that they are inherently excessive. *Coveted* collides directly with 可欲 (*kě yù* — what can be desired), which is *the desirable* two lines below in chapter 3's own triad. *Costly* is a price, and a price is conferred value.

---

### The working register

**貨 → "goods," in all five places, with no flexions** — and that, not *rare goods*, is what the frontmatter locks. **"Rare" is not 貨's rendering**; it renders 難得 (*nán dé* — hard to obtain), which as the closing section says renders no character at all. Putting *rare goods* in the `render:` field would overstate the lock and would push an unglossed Chinese phrase into the generated CSVs. Chapter 53's 財貨 is a bound compound and reads *"wealth and goods,"* 財 keeping its own word.

**難得之貨 → "rare goods," as a unit.** The phrase is lexical in classical Chinese — a **category of thing**, not a fresh description — so it takes a category name in English rather than a relative clause. That also restores something chapter 3 had lost: its three objects are 賢 (*xián* — the talented, one character), 難得之貨 (four), and 可欲 (two), so **the middle line is deliberately the heaviest**, and an English triad of neat two-word abstractions had flattened it.

**貴 keeps *prize* as its verb here.** Chapters 3 and 64 both open the phrase with 不貴, and both now read *does not prize*, matching 貴食母 (*guì shí mǔ* — "prize feeding from the mother") at chapter 20.

---

### What stays open

**"Rare" renders no character.** This is the honest cost of the lock and it should not be smoothed over. 難 (*nán* — difficult) simply goes unspoken in these three lines: *rare* is a fact about **quantity**, 難得 is a fact about **acquisition**. The causal chain still runs — rare, therefore hard to obtain honestly, therefore stolen — but it runs as an inference the reader makes, not as something the line says. 王弼's thieves 穿窬探篋，沒命而盜, tunnelling through walls at the cost of their lives, are no longer visible in the English. Their labour mirrored the trader's, and that mirror is gone.

**It is gone because English will not carry it.** There is **no one-word English adjective meaning "difficult to obtain" that is not also a value word** — because in English, difficulty of acquisition is simply *how we say* a thing is valuable. That gravitational pull is what dragged three separate renderings of one phrase toward *the precious*, *rare treasures* and *precious goods*, independently, in three different chapters. **Rare** is the last word on that slope that stops before the verdict. It is not the whole of 難得, and no available word is.

**The book uses the scarcity-makes-value inference elsewhere, and does not disown it.** Chapter 70 puts the two characters in one couplet: 知我者**希**，則我者**貴** (*zhī wǒ zhě xī, zé wǒ zhě guì* — "those who know me are few; those who pattern themselves on me are precious"). 河上公 draws the link explicitly: 希，少也…惟達道者乃能知我，故為貴也 — *"希 means few… only those who have reached the Tao can know me, and therefore it is precious."*

So **the same commentator who has the sage refuse to let rarity confer value on jade (64) lets rarity confer value on understanding (70).** That is not obviously a contradiction — one is a ruler setting a price that other people will steal to meet, the other is a plain observation about how few readers there are — but the machinery is identical, and the book never says where the line falls. Anyone reading 貨 as a straightforward critique of valuing scarce things has to account for chapter 70. *(See `希` in `glossary/TRIAGE.md`, still owed an entry; whatever English it takes must not be *rare*, which is spent here.)*

**And one word here is doing a job the book does elsewhere with a whole chapter.** 貨 is goods **in circulation** — the shell that changes hands. Chapter 80 imagines a state where 有舟輿，無所乘之 (*yǒu zhōu yú, wú suǒ chéng zhī* — "there are boats and carts, and no occasion to ride them") and neighbours grow old without visiting. That is a world with no 貨 in it, because nothing is moving. Whether the book is against **prizing** goods or against **circulating** them is not settled by this character, and the two readings pull the political chapters in different directions.
