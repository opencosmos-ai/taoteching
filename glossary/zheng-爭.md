## 爭 (Zhēng) — Contend

*Say "jung," level. Nine lines across seven chapters, and every one of them is under negation.*

**Locked: 爭 → "contend / contention."** *(Not "compete," not "competes," not "competing," not "competition.")*

Every English Tao Te Ching renders 爭 as **compete**. The word arrives without friction because the sentences it appears in sound like advice about ambition, and *compete* is what English does with ambition.

It costs two things.

First, it imports an institution. Competition in English is **rule-governed rivalry** — sport, markets, examinations. It has a referee, an agreed prize, and a starting line, and it is broadly understood to be healthy. None of that is in the character, and a chapter that says *do not compete* sounds like advice against careerism rather than a description of what hands do.

Second, and fatally, it collapses at the one line where the book distinguishes 爭 from winning.

### The graph: two hands and one object

爪 (*zhǎo*) sits on top — a hand, turned down, fingers grasping. 彐 sits beneath it — a second hand, coming from the other direction. Between them, in the oldest forms, is the thing: a vertical stroke that survives in the modern character as the hook down its middle. Some read it as a plowshare, gripped from both sides.

The *Shuowen Jiezi* (c. 100 CE) does not define it as rivalry at all:

> 爭，引也。从𠬪𠂆 — ***"爭 is to pull.** From 𠬪 (two hands) and 𠂆."*

Xu Xuan's note adds that 𠂆 is sounded as 曳 (*yè* — to drag), and that dragging is the whole principle of the graph. And the classical gloss makes the direction explicit:

> 凡言爭者，皆謂引之使歸於己 — *"wherever 爭 is spoken of, it means **pulling a thing so that it comes to oneself**."*

That is the term. Not rivalry as a condition, not ambition as a temperament: **the physical act of pulling a shared thing toward your own body.** Two hands, one object, opposite directions. It passes the pointing test, which *compete* does not — nobody can point at a competition, and anyone can point at two people pulling on the same rope.

### It appears only under negation — all nine times

爭 occurs nine times across seven chapters, and **every single occurrence is negated.** The book never once describes anyone contending. It names the word only in order to refuse it.

| Chapter | Line | |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 不尚賢，使民不**爭** | do not exalt the worthy, so the people do not contend |
| 8 | 水善利萬物而不**爭** | water is masterful at benefiting the countless things and does not contend |
| 8 | 夫唯不**爭** | only because it does not contend |
| 22 | 夫唯不**爭** | only because they do not contend |
| 22 | 故天下莫能與之**爭** | so no one in the world can contend with them |
| 66 | 以其不**爭** | because they do not contend |
| 66 | 故天下莫能與之**爭** | so no one in the world can contend with them |
| 68 | 是謂不**爭**之德 | this is called the integrity of not contending |
| 73 | 不**爭**而善勝 | does not contend, yet is masterful at overcoming |
| 81 | 為而不**爭** | does, and does not contend |

A word that exists in a text only as something not done is being used as a name for a disease.

### Chapter 73 decides it

> 天之道，不**爭**而善**勝** — *"nature's Tao does not 爭, yet is masterful at 勝."*

勝 (*shèng* — to overcome, to prevail) is not a word this book refuses. It endorses it, repeatedly and without irony: 柔弱**勝**剛強 (36, *the soft and weak overcome the hard and strong*), 牝常以靜**勝**牡 (61, *the female overcomes the male through stillness*), 自**勝**者強 (33, *to overcome oneself is strength*).

So the book draws a line, and the line falls between **爭** and **勝**. Prevailing is permitted. Pulling toward yourself is not.

**Under *compete*, that line disappears.** "Does not compete yet wins well" is barely a sentence in English, because competing and winning are two halves of one activity — you cannot win a thing you did not compete for, so the line reads as either a contradiction or a boast.

**Under *contend*, it survives intact.** Contending is the pulling; overcoming is what happens. The second does not require the first, which is precisely the claim.

### Chapter 3 says where it comes from

> 不尚賢，使民不**爭** — *"do not exalt the worthy, and the people will not contend."*

爭 is not a constant of human nature in this book. It is **manufactured**, and the mechanism is named: 尚 (*shàng* — to exalt, to raise up). Rank one thing above another and hands begin to reach for it. Remove the ranking and the reaching stops, because there is no longer a single object in the middle.

This is the same shape as Chapter 65, where the ruler's own guile produces the guile they then have to govern. The problem is upstream of the people every time.

### Chapter 68 defines it by what it is not

Four lines, each opening on 善 (*shàn* — masterful at), and then the name for all of them together:

> 善為士者，不**武**；善**戰**者，不怒；善**勝**敵者，不與；善用人者，為之**下**。是謂不**爭**之德。
>
> *"Those masterful in service are not martial. The master fighter does not rage. The master of overcoming enemies does not engage them. The master of using people puts themselves below them. This is called the integrity of not contending."*

Not martial, not enraged, not engaging, underneath. **爭 is the common name for what all four refuse** — and note how far that reaches past ambition. It covers the battlefield. *Compete* cannot carry a chapter about war; *contend* can, because English contends in arguments and in wars alike.

This chapter also fixes the boundary of the lock. It holds 戰 (*zhàn* — to fight) and 勝 (*shèng* — to overcome) **in the same chapter as 爭**, so neither *fight* nor *overcome* can be forbidden for 爭: both are legitimately owed to other characters standing a few lines away.

### What is set aside

**compete, competition** — the institutional frame described above, and the collapse at Chapter 73. It also flatters the thing being refused: competition is a word for something we approve of.

**struggle** — too broad, and solitary. A person can struggle against weather, illness, or themselves; 爭 requires a second hand on the same object. Also spoken for elsewhere in the manuscript.

**fight** — belongs to 戰 (*zhàn*), which has its own chapters and stands beside 爭 in Chapter 68.

**strive** — solitary again, and it loses the object entirely. Striving is about effort; 爭 is about direction.

**vie** — the right meaning and the wrong century.

**quarrel, wrangle, dispute** — verbal only. 爭 is bodily first and became verbal later; running the derivation backwards puts the argument before the hands.

### Working register

| Form | Chapters | English |
|---|---|---|
| 不爭, plain verb | 8, 22, 66, 73, 81 | does not contend |
| 使民不爭, causative | 3 | so the people do not contend |
| 莫能與之爭 | 22, 66 | no one can contend with them |
| 不爭之德, nominal | 68 | the integrity of not contending |

**"Contend with" is a live English idiom** and it does real work in the repeated formula 故天下莫能與之爭, which Chapters 22 and 66 share verbatim: *no one in the world can contend with them.* That is ordinary English for *nobody can take them on*, and it is nearer the Chinese than *compete with*, which suggests entering a contest rather than pulling against someone.

The **noun** is the pressure point. English has no clean nominal for the act — *contention* has drifted toward *a contested claim* — so 不爭之德 takes the gerund: **the integrity of not contending.** Where a noun is unavoidable, *contention* is permitted; it should not become the ordinary form.

### The register we lose

爭 is two hands, a plough, and dirt. *Contend* is Latin, *com-* plus *tendere*, to stretch against — physical at the root but three removes from a farmyard by the time it reaches modern English. The Anglo-Saxon words that keep the object are unusable at this register: **grab**, **grabbing**, **pulling toward yourself**. They are what the character says, and none of them can carry 不爭之德.

So the rendering trades the fingernails for the distinction, and the distinction is worth more. But the loss is real, and a reader who wants the character rather than the word should keep the two hands in view.

### Left open

**Whether non-contention is an ethic or a tactic.** The book will not say, and two chapters make the question sharp:

> 夫唯不爭，故天下莫能與之爭 (22) · 以其不爭，故天下莫能與之爭 (66)
>
> *"only because they do not contend can no one in the world contend with them"* (22) · *"because they do not contend, / no one in the world can contend with them"* (66)
>
> The two lines differ by one character, 唯 (*wéi* — only), and the English by one word.

Read one way, that is a description of someone who has stopped wanting the object, and is therefore beyond the reach of anyone still pulling. Read another, it is an unbeatable technique — the strongest possible position in exactly the contest being renounced. Chapter 66 leans into the second reading and does not flinch: the sage goes low, and 天下樂推 — *the world gladly pushes them forward*. They end up on top. They simply did not pull.

Whether that is a moral difference or a very elegant route to the same place, the text does not resolve, and neither should the English.

**And Chapter 81 keeps the hardest form of it**: 為而不**爭** — *does, and does not contend.* The sage acts. 為 (*wéi*) is already a hand laid on an elephant. So the instruction is not to take the hand away but to keep it there without pulling the thing toward yourself, and where exactly that boundary runs is the working question of the whole book, put in four characters.
