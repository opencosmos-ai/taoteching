## 長 (Cháng / Zhǎng) — Long, Grow, Chief

*Say "chahng," rising, for long; "jahng," dipping, for grow and chief. Sixteen lines across twelve chapters. One word for length in time and space, and for the growing that makes a thing long, and for the one who has grown longest.*

**Locked: 長 → "long," "last" where it is *cháng* · "grow" where it is *zhǎng* and a verb · "chief" where it is *zhǎng* and a noun.** *(Shalom's calls, 2026-11-20.)* *(Not "endure," which is 久 (jiǔ)'s, and chapter 7 sets the two side by side. Not "leader," which puts a person at the front of a line, 先 (xiān — ahead)'s place. Not "immortal," the later cult of long life the book never joins.)* **久 (*jiǔ* — enduring) is covered here, locked to *endure*.**

### The graph: an elder with long hair

**The oldest form is a person drawn with long, loose hair.** Wiktionary reads the oracle-bone and bronze graphs as an old man with long hair, and compares the top of 老 (*lǎo* — old), which carries the same hair. The male in that reading is the dictionary's; the picture shows age and hair. An old variant, 镸 (*cháng* — the old form of 長), survives inside the characters for hair, as in 髟 (*biāo* — long hair), the top of 鬓 (*bìn* — hair at the temples).

**The Shuowen reads the seal form instead, by the time the hair was no longer visible:**

> 長，久遠也。从兀从匕。兀者，高遠意也。久則變化。亾聲。
> *"長 is lasting and far. From 兀 (wù — high) and 匕 (huà — change). 兀 carries the sense of high and far. What lasts, changes. 亾 (wáng — lost) gives the sound."*

**Xu Shen defines 長 by 久.** His first two characters are 久遠 (*jiǔ yuǎn* — lasting and far), and his explanation of the parts, *what lasts, changes*, uses 久 again. The dictionary treats the two words as near-twins, and the Laozi keeps them apart. Where the picture stops: the Shuowen's 兀 and 匕 are a reading of strokes the seal script had rearranged, not the hair the oldest graph drew.

**The three readings are one word.** Wiktionary derives all of them from the root of 張 (*zhāng* — to stretch, to string a bow): *cháng* is the stretched state, *long*; *zhǎng* is the stretching, *to grow*. The tone that tells them apart in modern Mandarin is one of its few surviving grammatical tones. **The chief is the elder, the one who has grown longest**, and that brings the reading back to the picture.

### Sixteen lines, and the reader of the Chinese could not see the split either

The two readings are written with one graph, and a reader of the Chinese decides between them from the grammar, as a reader of the English decides between *lead* the metal and *lead* the verb. **Heshang Gong's edition marks the *zhǎng* reading**, with a sound-gloss after the comment, at three lines: chapters 10, 28 and 67 (at 10 the gloss's second syllable is likely miscopied). It marks none of the *cháng* lines. The English does the edition's work by giving each reading its own word.

**Long, in time and in space: *cháng*.**

> *Long and short shape each other,*
>
> 長短相形，
>
> — chapter 2

> *Hammer a point ever sharper, and it will not keep for long.*
>
> 揣而銳之，不可長保。
>
> — chapter 9

> *The sky is lasting, the earth enduring.*
> *Sky and earth can last and endure*
> *because they do not live for themselves,*
> *and so they live long.*
>
> 天長地久。
> 天地所以能長且久者，
> 以其不自生，
> 故能長生。
>
> — chapter 7

> *They do not boast, and so they last.*
>
> 不自矜，故長。
>
> — chapter 22

> *Those who boast do not last.*
>
> 自矜者不長。
>
> — chapter 24

> *Have the mother of the state, and you can last long.*
> *This is called the Tao of deep roots, firm base, long life, and enduring vision.*
>
> 有國之母，可以長久。
> 是謂深根固柢，長生久視之道。
>
> — chapter 59

Chapter 44 closes on the same 可以長久 (*kě yǐ cháng jiǔ* — "can last long") as chapter 59: *"And so you can last long."*

**To grow: *zhǎng*, a verb.**

> *grows and raises them, settles and matures them, nourishes and protects them.*
> *Birthing without possessing,*
> *acting without relying on it,*
> *growing them without ruling them —*
>
> 長之育之；亭之毒之；養之覆之。
> 生而不有，
> 為而不恃，
> 長而不宰。
>
> — chapter 51

Chapter 10 closes on the same three clauses, and its *"growing them without ruling them —"* is the same line. **Both commentators make the growing the thing's own.** Wang Bi on chapter 10: 物自長足，不吾宰成 — *"things grow to their fullness of themselves; I do not rule them into completion."* Heshang Gong on chapter 10: 道長養萬物，不宰割以為器用 — *"the Tao grows and nourishes the countless things, and does not butcher them into vessels for use,"* and on chapter 51 the same, ending 以為利 (*yǐ wéi lì* — "for profit").

> *Cultivate this in your community, and its integrity grows.*
>
> 修之於鄉，其德乃長；
>
> — chapter 54

**Chief: *zhǎng*, a noun.**

> *The sage puts them to use and becomes chief of the officers.*
>
> 聖人用之，則為官長，
>
> — chapter 28

> *Never pushing to be first in the world, and so you can become chief of the completed vessels.*
>
> 不敢為天下先，故能成器長。
>
> — chapter 67

Wang Bi reads chapter 67's chief as the one things come to: 唯後外其身，為物所歸，然後乃能立成器為天下利，為物之長也 — *"only by putting the body behind and outside do they become what things return to; then they can set up the finished vessels for the world's benefit, and be the chief of things."* Heshang Gong reads a chief of people: 成器長謂得道人也 — *"chief of the vessels means those who have attained the Tao."* Han Fei's text reads 事 (*shì* — affairs) for 器 (*qì* — vessel), and the 長 is the same in both (`notes/manuscript.md`).

**Grammar sorts all but one.** Where 長 takes an object, 之 (*zhī* — them) said or understood, it is *zhǎng*, to grow. Where it closes a noun phrase, 官長 (*guān zhǎng* — chief of the officers) and 器長 (*qì zhǎng* — chief of the vessels), it is *zhǎng*, chief. Everywhere else it is *cháng*, long. **Chapter 54 is the one line the grammar leaves open**, and the commentators decide it.

### Chapter 54: the integrity grows, and all three commentators say so

其德乃長 (*qí dé nǎi zhǎng*) could be read *"its integrity is then long,"* and so *long-lasting*. **None of the three oldest readers takes it as time.** The chapter is a widening series, body, family, community, state, world, and each rung names a larger integrity: 真 (*zhēn* — authentic), 餘 (*yú* — abundant), 長, 豐 (*fēng* — bountiful), 普 (*pǔ* — universal).

**Han Fei counts it:** 治鄉者行此節，則家之有餘者益衆 — *"when one who governs a community practises this, the households with a surplus grow more numerous."* He reads every rung the same way: at the next, 鄉之有德者益衆, *"the communities with integrity grow more numerous."*

**Wang Bi measures it:** 修之不廢，所施轉大 — *"cultivate it without ceasing, and what it reaches grows ever greater."*

**Heshang Gong spreads it:** 修道於鄉，尊敬長老，愛養幼小，教誨愚鄙，其德如是乃無不覆及也 — *"cultivate the Tao in the community: honor the elders, cherish the young, teach the ignorant. Its integrity being so, there is nothing it does not cover and reach."* His 長老 (*zhǎng lǎo* — elders) plays on the line's own character in its other reading.

**Legge (1891) hears the same,** in a verse paraphrase: *"The neighbourhood where it prevails / In thriving will abound."* Growth is the reading, and *grow* is already 長's verb at chapters 10 and 51.

### 久 (jiǔ — enduring)

**久 is the other half of the book's word for duration**, ten lines across eight chapters. The Shuowen reads its graph as a person held back from behind, 以後灸之，象人兩脛後有距也 (*"held from behind, like a person with something braced behind both shins"*): what stays where it is. **Chapter 7 puts the two characters in one line and gives each its own predicate**, 天長地久 (*tiān cháng dì jiǔ* — "the sky is lasting, the earth enduring"), then joins them, 長且久 (*cháng qiě jiǔ* — "last and endure"). The English keeps them apart the same way: **長 *lasts*, 久 *endures*.**

> *Even sky and earth cannot make it endure.*
>
> 天地尚不能久，
>
> — chapter 23

> *One who does not lose their place endures.*
>
> 不失其所者久。
>
> — chapter 33

Chapter 16 closes its ladder on the same word: 道乃久 (*dào nǎi jiǔ* — "the Tao leads to what endures"). **Heshang Gong's gloss on chapter 23's storm uses both characters, one in each half:** 言疾不能長，暴不能久也 — *"the rapid cannot last, the violent cannot endure."* He keeps them distinct inside one sentence, and so does the English.

**久 lives here, not in its own entry,** because every argument about it is an argument about 長: three of its eight chapters (7, 44, 59) print the two together, and its English is decided by what 長's is not. Its two remaining lines speak of time already passed, and take plain English: 其日固久 (*qí rì gù jiǔ* — "its days are long indeed") → *"for a very long time"* (58), and 久矣 (*jiǔ yǐ* — "long ago") → *"long since"* (67).

### The binome 長久 is one word, and keeps *last long*

可以長久 (*kě yǐ cháng jiǔ*) closes chapters 44 and 59, and in both it is a compound, *long-enduring*, with no 且 (*qiě* — and) between the halves. Chapter 7's 長且久 puts the 且 in, and the English there gives two verbs, *last and endure*. **Where the Chinese joins them, the English joins them: *"you can last long."*** Han Fei's reading of chapter 59 pulls the compound apart again, 其建生也長，持禄也久 (*"the life they establish lasts long, the office they hold endures"*), which is why the choice stays open below.

### What is set aside

**"Endure," "enduring," for 長.** 久's English, and chapter 7 needs both words in one line. Giving 長 *endure* at chapters 22 and 24 made the book say with 長 what it says with 久 at 16 and 33.

**"Leader," "lead," "leading."** *Lead* puts a person at the front of a line, which is 先 (*xiān* — ahead)'s position (`glossary/xian-hou-先後.md`), and *leader* is the vocabulary of management. 長 as a noun is the elder, the head of a body of people or things, and *chief* keeps that.

**"Superiority," "the superior."** Legge's reading of chapters 22 and 24 as *zhǎng*, *"he acquires superiority."* It is a real reading, kept open below, but *superiority* adds a verdict on worth the Chinese leaves out (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`). If 22 ever reads *zhǎng*, the word is *chief*.

**"Immortal," "immortality," "everlasting," "eternal life."** 長生 (*cháng shēng* — long life) became the name of a later practice of physical immortality. Heshang Gong already leans that way, glossing chapter 54's descendants with 長生不死 (*cháng shēng bù sǐ* — "long life without death"). Chapter 7 argues the opposite: sky and earth live long *because they do not live for themselves*, 以其不自生 (*yǐ qí bù zì shēng*), and Heshang Gong's own gloss there says so: 以其不求生，故能長生不終也 — *"because they do not seek life, they can live long and not come to an end."* *Live long* says only what the line says. *Everlasting* also fails the hymn test.

**"Master," "mastering," for 長 as a verb.** 長而不宰 (*zhǎng ér bù zǎi*) at chapters 10 and 51 is the mother's verb, beside birthing and rearing. *Leading without mastering* turns a gardener into a ruler and spends the ruling on 長, when the line puts it on 宰 (*zǎi* — to rule, a butcher's word).

**"Foster," "nurture."** 畜 (*xù* — rear) and 育 (*yù* — raise) stand beside 長 in chapters 10 and 51, and 養 (*yǎng* — nourish) in 51. *Grow* is the one verb none of them can claim.

**"Long-lasting," for chapter 54.** The one reading none of its commentators gives.

| 其德乃長 (54) | *zhǎng*, intransitive | *grows* |
| 官長 (28), 器長 (67) | *zhǎng*, noun | *chief* |

**For 久:** *endure* at 7, 16, 23, 33, and as *enduring* in 59's 久視 (*jiǔ shì* — enduring vision); the past-time phrases at 58 and 67 take plain English, as above.

### What no rule can enforce

***Endure* is ruled out for 長, and cannot be forbidden.** Chapters 7, 44 and 59 print 長 and 久 together, and 久 may print *endure* and *enduring* there. `concordance.py --english "endure"` is the check, run by hand: every hit should stand in a line with 久.

***Last* is ruled out for 久, and cannot be forbidden either.** 久 has no frontmatter of its own, and *last* is also 後 (*hòu* — behind)'s declared flexion at chapters 7 and 67. The binome 長久 at 44 and 59 holds *last long* by argument, above.

***Master* is ruled out for 長 as a verb, and cannot be forbidden.** 善 (*shàn* — masterful) prints *masterfully* in chapter 54.

### Left open

**Chapters 22 and 24 could read *zhǎng*.** 不自矜，故長 sits at the end of four 不自X (*bù zì X* — "does not X themselves") clauses, and the other three end in something that comes to the one who refuses it: clear sight, rightness that shows, work that is theirs. A fourth that ended *"and so they become chief"* would fit that series, and Legge reads it so. Both Chinese commentators read time, Wang Bi with 其德長也 (*qí dé cháng yě* — "their integrity lasts") and Heshang Gong at 24 with 不可以長久 (*bù kě yǐ cháng jiǔ* — "cannot last long"), and the verse follows them. Heshang Gong's edition, which marks *zhǎng* at 10, 28 and 67, leaves these two lines unmarked. The other reading is the strongest one this entry declines.

**Whether 長久 at 44 and 59 should read *last and endure*,** two verbs as at chapter 7, so that 久's English stands wherever 久 does. The binome argument kept *last long*; Han Fei's gloss of 59, which splits the pair into *lasts long* and *endures*, is the case for the other way.

**Chapter 54's rung breaks its series' pattern.** The other four rungs read *becomes* and an adjective; this one reads *grows*. The verb is what all three commentators read, and the series still climbs. An adjective of growth that English has never found, *increasing*, *widening*, is the cost of keeping the shape.
