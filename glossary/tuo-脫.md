## 脫 (Tuō) — Slip Away

*Say "twaw," level. Two lines in two chapters. Something held comes out of what held it, and nothing does it to them.*

**Locked: 脫 → "slip away," with *from* where the Chinese names what is left (於 (*yú* — from), ch 36).** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-22.)* *(Not "leave," which three other characters wear. Not "escape," which makes the deep a prison. Not "come loose," which is a commentator's gloss. Not bare "slip," which is a fall or a mistake.)*

### The graph: flesh coming away from the frame

> 脫，消肉臞也。从肉兌聲。
> *"脫 is flesh wasting away. From 肉 (ròu — flesh), with 兌 (duì) for sound."*

**消 (*xiāo* — to dwindle, melt away), 肉 (*ròu* — flesh), 臞 (*qú* — gaunt): a body thinning until the frame shows.** Not decay, but loss, the flesh drawing back from what it filled. **Its nearest relative is 蛻 (*tuì*), which the Shuowen defines as 蛇蟬所解皮也 (*"the skin that snakes and cicadas shed"*)**, built from 虫 (*chóng* — creature) and 挩 (*tuō*) abbreviated, with the same 兌 inside. The family is one motion: what was inside comes out of what held it, as the snake comes out of its skin.

**Where the picture stops:** no flesh wastes in either of the book's lines. By Laozi's time the word is the motion, not the body, and the book uses it for a fish and for a person who holds fast. 兌 here carries sound; the book's own 兌, at chapters 52 and 56, is *the openings*, and nothing in these two lines turns on it.

### Two lines, and in each something comes out of what held it

> *A fish cannot slip away from deep water.*
> *A state's beneficial tools cannot be shown.*
>
> 魚不可脫於淵，
> 國之利器不可以示人。
>
> — chapter 36

> *One who is masterful at planting is not uprooted.*
> *One who is masterful at embracing does not slip away.*
> *By it, descendants will never cease their sacrifices.*
>
> 善建者不拔，
> 善抱者不脫，
> 子孫以祭祀不輟。
>
> — chapter 54

**In both lines 脫 is intransitive, and no one does it to the subject.** The fish is not taken from the deep, and the embracer is not pried loose. 脫 is what the subject does, or does not do, by itself. At chapter 36 the fish comes out of the water that holds it; at chapter 54 the one who embraces does not come out of their own embrace. The English needs a verb with the same shape: no object, no agent, and a going-out from a hold. *Slip away* has all three, and *away from* carries 於.

### What the commentators did with it: at chapter 36, a loss that cannot be undone

All three read the fish's slipping as final.

- **Wang Bi:** 魚脫於淵則必見失矣 (*"a fish that slips away from the deep is surely lost"*), and he joins the state's tools to it with 亦 (*yì* — also): showing them is *also* surely lost (`glossary/ke-可.md`).
- **Han Fei**, the oldest: 勢重者，人君之淵也 … 失則不可復得也 (*"weighty position is the ruler's deep water … once lost, it cannot be got back"*). Power slips from the ruler to the ministers, as the fish from the deep.
- **Heshang Gong:** 不可復制也 (*"it cannot be brought back under control"*).

**The deep is the fish's home, not its cage.** That is why *escape* fails, and why *slip away* works: what slips away from where it lives is gone.

### At chapter 54, the grip that holds

The line is about a person, 善抱者 (*shàn bào zhě* — "one masterful at embracing"), and the commentators say what they keep hold of.

- **Han Fei:** 一於其情，雖有可欲之類，神不爲動，神不爲動之謂「不脫」 (*"one with their own nature: though there are things to want, the spirit is not moved; the spirit not being moved is called 'not slipping away'"*). The one who embraces is not drawn off by what tempts them.
- **Wang Bi:** 不貪於多，齊其所能，故不脫也 (*"they do not crave too much and keep to what they can manage, so they do not slip away"*).
- **Heshang Gong:** 善以道抱精神者，終不可拔引解脫 (*"one masterful at embracing the vital spirit with the Tao can never be pulled out, drawn off, or come loose"*).

**Beside 不拔 (*bù bá* — "is not uprooted"), 不脫 is the second half of one claim:** what is well planted is not pulled up from below, and the one who holds well does not slip from their own hold.

### What is set aside

**"Leave."** It is 遺 (*yí* — to leave behind)'s English, at chapters 9 and 52, and a word already spoken for is not available (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`). 去 (*qù* — to go away) reads *fade away* (2, 21) and 離 (*lí* — to separate) *separate from* (26, 28), so the book's *leave* is one character's. It also says the wrong thing at chapter 54, where *does not leave* sounds like staying put, not holding fast.

**"Escape."** It makes the deep a prison. The fish's water is where it lives, and every commentator reads leaving it as loss.

**"Come loose," "work loose."** Heshang Gong's 解脫 (*jiě tuō* — "come loose") in English, a gloss put into the verse (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`). It also makes the subject a knot or a fastening, where 脫 is a living thing coming out of what holds it.

**"Slip," alone.** In English a slip is a fall or a mistake, and *does not slip* would praise someone for not erring. *Away* makes it a going-out.

**"Be removed."** It brings in a remover, which the grammar refuses.

### Working register

| line | form | English |
|---|---|---|
| 魚不可脫於淵 (36) | intransitive, with 於 naming what is left | *cannot slip away from deep water* |
| 善抱者不脫 (54) | intransitive, bare | *does not slip away* |

***Away* belongs to the verb, not to the line.** English *slip* needs the particle to mean a going-out at all; it carries the motion 脫 already has and adds no place. 於 at chapter 36 is *from*.

### What no rule can enforce

**The reverse direction.** `forbidden:` holds *leave* out of chapters 36 and 54, where no other character claims it. Whether *slip* or *slip away* appears for some other character elsewhere, no gate can see. Run `concordance.py --english "slip"` when a line elsewhere reaches for it.

**The euphemism.** *Slip away* is also what the dying do. The reader holds that, and the next item weighs it.

### Left open

**At chapter 54, *does not slip away* can be heard as *does not die*.** It stands one line before the descendants' sacrifices, and Heshang Gong reads that very sense into the next line: 為人子孫能修道如是，長生不死 (*"descendants who can cultivate the Tao like this live long and do not die"*). So the echo has a commentator behind it, but it narrows a line the Chinese leaves open: the Chinese says only that the embracer does not come out of their hold.

**Silk A reads the fish line without 可, and the oldest witness keeps it.** Silk A reads 魚不脫於瀟 (*yú bù tuō yú xiāo*): no 可 (*kě* — can), and 瀟 (*xiāo* — deep and clear water) for 淵 (*yuān* — the deep). The Shuowen makes 瀟 a river's name and gives *deep and clear* to 潚 (*sù*), which later dictionaries treat as its variant. Silk B reads 魚不可說於淵, writing 說 (*shuō*) for 脫, a graph on the same 兌. Without 可, the line reports what a fish does not do. Han Fei, the oldest witness to the chapter, quotes it with 可, as 魚不可脫於深淵 (*yú bù kě tuō yú shēn yuān* — "a fish cannot slip away from the deep pool"), so by the tie-break order the *cannot* that `glossary/ke-可.md` defends stands. The three readings are forks 131–133 in `sources/variants.yaml`. The silks' are from a Wikisource transcription and not yet checked against a print edition (`WORKLIST.md` T2-95).

**The other witnesses at chapter 54.** Both silks are broken where 抱 and 脫 stand. The Guodian slips attest the chapter whole (bundle B), and their graph at 脫 is not yet read here.
