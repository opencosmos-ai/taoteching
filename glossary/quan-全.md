## 全 (Quán) — Whole

*Say "chwen," rising. Three occurrences in eighty-one chapters, all of them in Chapter 22, and the word English keeps reaching for is a repair verb.*

**Locked: 全 → "whole" — as an outcome, *stay whole*; as a transitive act, *keep whole*.** *(Never "complete," never "perfect," never "flawless," and never any verb of repair.)*

### The live problem: English wants to fix something

Chapter 22 opens 曲則全 (*qū zé quán* — "bend, then whole") and closes 誠全而歸之. Four drafts of that closing line, in this repository, reached for a verb of restoration — *makes you whole*, *restores your wholeness* — and every one of them smuggled in **a breakage the Chinese never concedes.**

The chapter is not about being mended. It is about **not getting broken in the first place.** 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) says so in five characters, glossing 曲則全:

> 曲己從眾，不自專，則**全其身**也
> *"Bend yourself and follow the crowd, do not act on your own authority, then you **keep your body whole**."*

全其身 — **全 as a transitive verb meaning *keep intact***. Not *make whole*. The sense is attested straight through the lexical tradition: *to preserve, to keep whole or intact*. Preservation, never repair.

To *restore* is to bring something back to a former condition, which presupposes the loss. It is *make whole* with better manners. Under this lock, neither can stand.

### The graph: a piece of jade brought in whole

全 is **入** (*rù* — to enter, to bring in) over **玉** (*yù* — jade), written 王 with its dot dropped. The picture is **a piece of jade carried in unbroken.**

The *Shuowen Jiezi* (~100 CE) glosses it 完也 (*wán* — intact), and the tradition attached to the graph reads **純玉曰全** — *"pure jade is called 全."* Not jade that has been polished into perfection: jade that arrived without a crack in it.

That single image carries the whole lock. **You cannot repair jade.** A cracked piece is not restorable; it is a different, lesser object. Wholeness in jade is not an achievement, it is a survival — which is exactly the claim Chapter 22 is making about a person who bends.

> **A caution specific to this repository.** The component at the bottom of 全 is **玉 (*yù* — jade), not 王 (*wáng* — ruler)**, though the two are written alike once 玉 loses its dot. There is no king inside *whole*. Given how much work `DISCOVERIES.md` §4 does to get a throne out of 王, it is worth saying plainly that this graph never had one.

### The evidence — a closed system, and a quotation

**全 occurs three times, all in Chapter 22:** 曲則全, the verbatim quote-back 古之所謂「曲則全」者, and the closing 誠全而歸之. **曲** (*qū* — bent) does the same: twice, both in Chapter 22, both bound to 全. Neither character appears anywhere else in the book.

That seals the pair inside one chapter and makes it a self-contained argument. It also raises a question the chapter answers itself — see the open tension at the foot of this entry.

**The material vocabulary around it, none of which is 全's word.**

| Character | Picture | Where | Rendered |
|---|---|---|---|
| **瑕** (*xiá*) | a **flaw in jade** | 27 · 善言無瑕謫 | *flawless* — already spent |
| **缺** (*quē*) | a **chipped jar** (缶 *fǒu*, an earthen vessel) | 45 · 大成若缺 · 58 | *deficient* |
| **散** (*sàn*) | scattered, broken up | 28 · 樸散則為器 | *carved* |
| **成** (*chéng*) | brought to completion | 14 chapters | *complete* |

The book thinks about integrity in **objects** — jade, jars, timber. 全 belongs in that company and should be rendered from it.

### What is set aside, and why

**"Complete"** belongs to **成** (*chéng*), which runs through fourteen chapters, and the two are not synonyms. *Complete* means **finished**; 全 means **undamaged**. A thing can be whole and unfinished — Chapter 41 insists on it: 大器晚成 (*dà qì wǎn chéng* — *"the great vessel is never completed"*). The book is openly suspicious of completion. It is not suspicious of wholeness.

**"Perfect"** imports an ideal, and usually a moral one. 全 is physical integrity, not attainment. Fails the hymn test on contact: *"bend, and you shall be made perfect."*

**"Flawless"** is the right semantic field and **the wrong chapter** — it is already carrying 瑕 (*xiá* — a flaw in jade) at Chapter 27, 善言無瑕謫, *"masterful speaking is flawless."* Two jade words in one book, and English has one good adjective for them. 瑕 got there first, and *flawless* also drifts toward **appearance**, where 全 is about **condition**.

**"Restore," "make whole," "heal"** — the repair family, rejected above. They concede the damage the chapter denies.

**"Preserved,"** as in the familiar *"yield and you will be preserved,"* fails on **voice**. The English passive hands the preserving to an unnamed outside agent. 全其身 has the person doing it themselves — which is the point of a chapter about what your own bending accomplishes.

**"Unbroken"** is not wrong, and was this edition's first rendering (*"Yield and remain unbroken"*). It was set aside for defining by negation: the jade is not merely *not shattered*, it is **whole and worth something**. The positive value is in the stone.

**"Intact"** is the most exact one-word gloss of the sense and is not used in the verse, because it is clinical — a word for shipping crates and evidence bags. It is the right word for the *definition* and the wrong register for the *poem*, and that gap is worth knowing when reading this entry against the line.

### 曲 (*qū* — bend), treated here

曲 is a picture of something **bent** — the older graph is read as a curved or notched implement, a bamboo frame or bent vessel. It is the ordinary word for *crooked, curved, bent aside*.

**Render it *bend*, and never *yield*.** *Yielding* is the locked English for **弱** (*ruò*), whose graph is two 弓 (*gōng* — a bow) with 彡 (fine strokes) — a bow is useful because it bends. This edition's earlier Chapter 22 read *"Yield and remain unbroken,"* which borrowed 弱's locked word for a character that is not 弱 and does not appear in that chapter. The two ideas are cousins; the two words are not interchangeable.

*Bend* also keeps the physical gesture the chapter needs, because the four opening lines are all bodily: bent, wronged-and-straightened, hollowed, worn out.

### Working register

- as the outcome of the 則 (*zé* — then, thus) construction → **stay whole** (曲則全 → *"Bend, and stay whole"*)
- as a transitive act, chiefly in the commentaries → **keep whole** (全其身 → *"keep their body whole"*)
- as a bare predicate → **whole**
- 曲 → **bend** / **bent**, never *yield*

**Never** *complete*, *perfect*, *flawless*, or any verb that implies the thing was broken first.

### Left open

**Why does a word this load-bearing appear in one chapter and nowhere else?**

全 is the whole argument of Chapter 22 — it opens the poem, it is quoted back, it closes the poem. And in the other eighty chapters, silence. So does 曲. A vocabulary that central ought to recur, and it does not.

**The chapter may be telling us why.** It marks the line as borrowed: 古之所謂「曲則全」者 — *"what the ancients called 'bend, then whole'."* If Chapter 22 is commentary on an older proverb, then **the 曲/全 vocabulary arrived with the quotation** and was never Laozi's own. That would explain the distribution exactly, and it would make this chapter a rare thing in the book: not a teaching, but a defence of somebody else's.

It also leaves a second question genuinely unanswered. **Whole *what*?** 河上公 supplies 身 (*shēn* — the body) and reads the closing line as returning that body to one's parents unharmed — the filial register of his self-cultivation reading, and a Han import this edition declines. The text itself never says. It writes 全, bare, three times, and lets the reader carry it.

We keep it bare too.
