## 百姓 (Bǎi xìng) — Everyone

*Say "bye shing," falling then falling. Four appearances across three chapters.*

**Locked: 百姓 → "everyone."** *(Not "the people," which is 民's. Not "the hundred families" or "the hundred surnames," which are accurate about the words and say nothing about the people.)*

百姓 is the whole population, named without reference to anyone above it. 民 (*mín* — the people) is the population as a ruler sees it: governed, taxed, frightened, or left alone. 百姓 is the same people seen from among themselves, and in three of its four lines they are the ones acting: they speak, they have a heart, they pay attention.

### The words: a hundred clan names

- **百 (*bǎi*)** is *a hundred*, and as with 萬 (*wàn* — ten thousand) in 萬物 (*wàn wù* — the countless things), the number means *all of them*, not a count.
- **姓 (*xìng*)** is a clan name. Our vendored 說文解字 (*Shuōwén Jiězì*, c. 100 CE) defines it 人所生也…从女从生 — *"what a person is born of… from 女 (nǚ — woman) and 生 (shēng — birth)."* The name you inherit is, in the graph, the name of the one who bore you.

**The phrase changed sides.** In the oldest texts only noble lineages had clan names, so 百姓 meant **the nobility**. The *Book of Documents* says of the sage-king Yao 平章百姓 (*píng zhāng bǎi xìng* — "he regulated and distinguished the hundred clans"), and its commentator 孔安國 (*Kǒng Ānguó*) glosses 百姓 there as 百官 (*bǎi guān* — the hundred officials). The commoners, who had no clan names, were 民. **The two words began as opposites.** As surnames spread downward through the Warring States, 百姓 widened to cover everyone. The *Great Commentary* to the *Book of Changes* uses it that way: 百姓日用而不知 (*"everyone uses it daily and does not know it"*).

By Laozi's day the old sense is gone, and both of our commentators read 百姓 as the whole population. 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) on ch 49: 聖人愛念百姓如孩孾赤子 — *"the sage cherishes everyone as one cherishes a newborn infant."* 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) on ch 17: 功成事遂而百姓不知其所以然也 — *"the work is done and the task complete, and everyone does not know how it came to be so."*

### The evidence: the lines where the people act

> 百姓皆謂：「我自然」(17) — *Everyone says, "We did this by ourselves."*
> 以百姓心為心 (49) — *they take everyone's heart as their own*
> 百姓皆注其耳目 (49) — *everyone pours out their attention, all eyes and ears*

**Chapter 17 is the one place in the book where the people speak in their own voice**, and what they say is that the rule they lived under did nothing they could see. That is the chapter's highest praise of a ruler, and it can only be spoken by people who did not experience themselves as governed. The English cannot use the word the book reserves for the governed (民) to say it.

**Chapter 49 makes their heart the sage's.** 聖人無常心，以百姓心為心 — *the sage has no fixed heart; they take everyone's heart as their own.* The sage is defined by what everyone feels, which is the reverse of 民's lines, where the ruler acts and the people respond.

> 聖人不仁，以百姓為芻狗 (5) — *the sage is not concerned with humaneness: they treat everyone as straw dogs*

**Chapter 5 is the exception, and it is built on a parallel.** The line before it reads 天地不仁，以萬物為芻狗 — *sky and earth treat the countless things as straw dogs.* As sky and earth are to all beings, the sage is to all people. *Everyone* answers *the countless things* exactly: no one singled out, no one favoured.

**And chapter 67 shows the line 百姓 draws.** 天下皆謂我道大 is the same frame as chapter 17 (X 皆謂, *X all say*) with 天下 (*tiān xià* — the world) in the subject slot. *The world* says my Tao is great. *Everyone* says we did this ourselves. The world is a public verdict, while 百姓 is the people talking about their own lives.

### What is set aside, and why

**"The people."** It is 民's, and the book uses the two words for two different angles on one population. Rendering both *the people* would erase the one difference the reader could otherwise hear: in chapter 17, the governed get to say that they were not governed.

**"The hundred families," "the hundred surnames."** Accurate about the words, and drained of the people. They point the reader at the phrase's history, which by Laozi's day had turned the other way, and they sound like an exact count. A reader meets an antiquarian term where the Chinese has an ordinary one.

**"The common people," "the masses."** Both bring a class line the phrase no longer draws. *Common* sets them against the uncommon, and *the masses* is rejected for 民 and 眾 (*zhòng* — the crowd) on the same grounds.

### The working register

- as subject → **everyone** (百姓皆謂 → "everyone says"; English *everyone* already holds 皆 (*jiē* — all), so it is not rendered twice)
- possessive → **everyone's** (以百姓心為心 → "they take everyone's heart as their own")
- as object → **everyone** (以百姓為芻狗 → "they treat everyone as straw dogs")

*Everyone* takes a singular verb and a singular-they pronoun, which is the edition's practice for the sage too: *"everyone pours out their attention."*

### Left open

**"Everyone" drops the kinship the phrase is made of.** 姓 is a lineage, and 百姓 is the population as a web of households, which is part of why it can speak and have a heart where 民 cannot. *Everyone* keeps the universality and loses the households. We take that trade because the households were history by the time the book was written, and the universality is what the lines use.

**It also includes the ruler, strictly speaking.** 百姓 does not: the sage stands apart from it in every line, taking its heart, treating it as straw dogs. The English relies on context to exclude the one speaking, as English *everyone* usually does.
