## 欲 (Yù) — Desire, and Wanting

*Say "yoo," falling. Twenty-six appearances in twenty-five lines across fifteen chapters. 無欲 (wú yù — desireless) alone stands in five of them.*

**Locked: 欲 → "desire" as a state, with nothing after it · "want" when a verb follows · "would" in the phrase 將欲 (jiāng yù — "if you are about to want to").** *(Locked 2026-11-19, Shalom's call, after the chapter pass.)* *(Not "craving," which carries a verdict the character does not and is the standard English for the Buddhist root of suffering. Not "wish," which is idle. Not "urge," a drive in the psychologist's sense. Not "lust," "covet" or "the passions," the hymn test's vocabulary of sin.)* **Settled within it, by Shalom's calls:** *ever desireless* and *ever desiring* at chapter 1 (2026-11-14); *wants the not-wanting* at chapter 64. *The desirable* at chapter 3 belongs to 可 (kě — can be), as its flexion.

### The graph: an open mouth, and a valley

> 欲，貪欲也。从欠，谷聲。
> *"欲: greedy wanting. From 欠 (qiàn — a person with the mouth open), with 谷 (gǔ — a valley) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

**欠 is a person with the mouth open.** It is the yawn, the gasp, the gape: breath going out, and a gap that wants filling. Most of the characters built on it are things a mouth does, breathing, singing, drinking, sighing. 欲 is the gape turned toward something.

**谷 is the book's valley.** The Shuowen: 泉出通川爲谷, *"a spring coming out to reach a river is a valley"* (`glossary/gu-谷.md`).

*A calibration.* The Shuowen counts 谷 as sound only, and so does this entry. But the two halves are both openings, a mouth and a valley, and the book sets them side by side more than once. Chapter 15 likens the ancients to a valley and then says they 不欲盈 (*bù yù yíng* — "do not want to be full"); chapter 39 fills the valley and, in Heshang Gong's reading, warns it not to want to stay full. The empty place and the wish to fill it are one graph. We do not claim the scribes meant it. The book's own pairings do the arguing.

### Twenty-five lines, and three grammars

The lines sort cleanly by what follows the character.

**With nothing after it, 欲 is a state, and the English is *desire*.** 無欲 (*wú yù* — without desire), 有欲 (*yǒu yù* — with desire), 寡欲 (*guǎ yù* — little desire), and 不欲 standing alone:

> *Ever desireless, you can observe its subtlety;*
> *Ever desiring, you can observe its direction.*
>
> 故常無欲，以觀其妙；
> 常有欲，以觀其徼。
>
> — chapter 1

> *I do not desire, and the people become plain of themselves.*
>
> 我無欲，而民自樸。
>
> — chapter 57

Also chapter 3's 常使民無知無欲 (*"so the people are ever unknowing, ever desireless"*), chapter 19's 少私寡欲 (*"diminish the self, diminish desire"*), chapter 34's 常無欲 (*"ever desireless, it can be named small"*), and the second and third 欲 of chapter 37.

**With a verb after it, 欲 is wanting to do something, and the English is *want*.**

> *Do not want to be gleaming like jade,*
>
> 不欲琭琭如玉，
>
> — chapter 39

> *The large state wants nothing more than to gather and rear people.*
> *The small state wants nothing more than to enter and serve people.*
> *Both get what they want.*
>
> 大國不過欲兼畜人，
> 小國不過欲入事人。
> 夫兩者各得所欲，
>
> — chapter 61

Also 15 (不欲盈), 37 (欲作), 46 (欲得), 66 (欲上民, 欲先民) and 77 (不欲見賢), each of which takes a verb.

**After 將 (*jiāng* — about to), 欲 is the conditional, and the English is *would*.**

> *If you would draw it in, you must first stretch it out.*
>
> 將欲歙之，必固張之；
>
> — chapter 36

Four times in chapter 36 and once in chapter 29 (*"Those who would take the world and handle it"*). English *would* holds both halves of 將欲 at once, the intention and the nearness, which is why the phrase takes one word and not *wants to be about to*.

**In none of these lines is 欲 evil in itself.** Chapter 1 gives *ever desiring* its own capacity, to observe the Tao's direction. Chapter 61 lets both states get what they want, and approves. Chapter 66 tells anyone who wants to rise above the people how to do it. What the book condemns is particular: 欲得 (*yù dé* — wanting to get) is the greatest fault at chapter 46, and displaying 可欲 (*kě yù* — the desirable) disturbs the people at chapter 3. The character describes. The verdicts come from the lines around it.

### Chapter 37 is the hardest line: three 欲, one chain

> *Transforming, if they want to act, I will stabilize them with the nameless uncarved wood.*
> *With the nameless uncarved wood, they too will be desireless.*
> *Not desiring, and so still, the world will straighten itself.*
>
> 化而欲作，吾將鎮之以無名之樸。
> 無名之樸，夫亦將無欲。
> 不欲以靜，天下將自正。
>
> — chapter 37

The chapter runs a chain, and 欲 is its thread: the things **want** to act (欲作, *yù zuò*); they too will be **desireless** (無欲, *wú yù*); **not desiring** (不欲, *bù yù*), and so still, the world will straighten itself. Three grammars of one character in three lines, each handing the word to the next.

**The grammar sorts all three, and the chapter now reads so** (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19). 欲作 takes a verb, so *want*. 無欲 and 不欲 take nothing, so *desire*: *desireless*, and *not desiring*. One root runs through all three. *Urge*, the old English, rendered no character the line prints, and *relinquishing* supplied a giving-up the Chinese does not have: 不欲 is not wanting, not letting go of a want once held. The same rebuilding restored 化's chain (T3-8) and the subject Heshang Gong gives the second line, the people, not the wood.

**Wang Bi reads the chain as the end of contending.** On 化而欲作: 作欲成也, *"the acting wants to complete itself."* On the uncarved wood's 無欲: 無欲競也, *"desireless: not wanting to compete."* The wanting he means is the wanting to win, which is 爭 (*zhēng* — contend)'s ground (`glossary/zheng-爭.md`).

**The silks read the chapter otherwise.** In the collated silk text the close is 夫將不辱。不辱以靜 (*fū jiāng bù rù. bù rù yǐ jìng* — "they will not be disgraced; not disgraced, and so still"), with 辱 (*rù* — disgrace) where our base has 欲 twice. The chain's thread there is disgrace, not desire. Our base and both commentaries print 欲.

### What the commentators did with it

**Wang Bi gives desire a use.** On chapter 1: 故常無欲空虚，可以觀其始物之妙, *"so the ever desireless, empty, can observe the subtlety by which things begin,"* and 故常有欲，可以觀其終物之徼也, *"so the ever desiring can observe the heading where things end."* Desirelessness sees beginnings; desire sees where things go. Neither is a fault. Heshang Gong keeps only the first: 人常能無欲，則可以觀道之要, *"a person who can be ever desireless can observe the essential of the Tao,"* and his ever-desiring person observes 世俗之所歸趣, *"where the worldly are heading."*

**Wang Bi's chapter 57 is the silks' chapter 57.** Our base reads 我無欲 (*wǒ wú yù* — "I do not desire"). Wang Bi glosses it 上之所欲，民從之速也。我之所欲唯無欲，而民亦無欲而自樸也, *"what those above want, the people follow quickly. What I want is only desirelessness, and the people too are desireless, and plain of themselves."* The collated silk text prints exactly that wanting: **我欲不欲而民自樸** (*wǒ yù bù yù ér mín zì pǔ* — "I want the not-wanting, and the people become plain of themselves"), the formula of chapter 64. **The sage's desirelessness is itself a want**, and the oldest witness and the oldest commentary on our base both say so.

**At chapter 64 the commentators split on the formula.** Wang Bi reads it as the book's reflexive, X-ing the not-X, the shape of chapter 63's 為無為 (*wéi wú wéi* — "do the not-doing"), and adds 好欲雖㣲，争尚為之興, *"a fondness for wanting, however slight, gives rise to contention and esteem."* Heshang Gong reads a comparison: 聖人欲人所不欲。人欲彰顯，聖人欲伏光, *"the sage wants what people do not want. People want to stand out; the sage wants to hide their light."* The verse keeps the reflexive, Shalom's call, on the shape chapters 63 and 64 share.

**Heshang Gong ties little desire to enough.** On chapter 19's 寡欲: 寡欲者，當知足也, *"to want little is to know you have enough"* (`glossary/zhizu-知足.md`). Chapter 46 makes the same join in the Chinese itself, setting 不知足 (*bù zhī zú* — not knowing enough) and 欲得 (*yù dé* — wanting to get) in one couplet.

### What is set aside

**"Craving."** The verdict word. *Craving* is a want gone wrong, and it is the usual English for the Buddhist *taṇhā*, the thirst that is the root of suffering in the second noble truth. Put it on 欲 and the book becomes a sermon against wanting, which chapters 1, 61 and 66 refuse. At chapter 46 the fault is named by 咎 (*jiù* — fault); 欲得 is only *wanting to get*, and English needs nothing harsher (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`).

**"Lust," "covet," "the passions," "concupiscence."** The vocabulary of sin. *Covet* is the tenth commandment; *the passions* and *concupiscence* are Augustine's. Each fails the hymn test on sight, and each makes the people of chapter 3 sinners to be cured.

**"Wish," "no wish."** Too light. A wish can sit idle; 欲 leans toward its object, as the open mouth does. And *no wish* turns a verb into a noun the line does not print: 不欲盈 is *do not want to be full*, not *have no wish to be*.

**"Urge," "drive."** The psychologist's words, Freud's *Trieb* in English. At chapter 37 *urge* also stands where 欲 should and renders nothing the line prints.

**"Relinquish," "renounce," "give up."** They supply a want first held and then surrendered. 不欲 and 無欲 say only that the wanting is not there.

**"Passionless," "dispassionate."** The Stoic sage's *apatheia*, an ideal of calm achieved by reason against feeling. The desireless one in this book is plain like uncarved wood, not composed against an inner storm.

**"Appetite."** Heshang Gong's 嗜欲 (*shì yù* — appetites) is the body's version of the word, and *appetite* is right in a note. In the verse it narrows 欲 to food and sex, and 欲 at chapter 61 is a state's wish for people.

**"Will," "intend," "aim."** *Will* belongs to 志 (*zhì* — will), at chapters 3 and 33 (`glossary/zhi-志.md`). *Intend* and *aim* are plans, and 欲 is the leaning before a plan.

### Working register

| line | grammar | English |
|---|---|---|
| 常無欲 · 常有欲 (1) · 無欲 (3, 34, 37) · 我無欲 (57) | a state, no verb after | *ever desireless · ever desiring · desireless* · *I do not desire*, in ch 57's refrain of *I do not …* (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19) |
| 寡欲 (19) | a state | *diminish desire* |
| 不欲以靜 (37) | a state, no verb after | *not desiring* |
| 可欲 (3) | 可 + 欲, a fixed noun | *the desirable* — 可's flexion |
| 不欲盈 (15) · 不欲琭琭 (39) · 不欲見賢 (77) | 欲 + verb, negated | *do not want to…* (15 and 77 Shalom's call, 2026-11-19) |
| 欲作 (37) · 欲得 (46) · 欲兼畜人 · 欲入事人 (61) · 欲上民 · 欲先民 (66) | 欲 + verb | *want to…* |
| 所欲 (61) | 所 + 欲, what is wanted | *what they want* |
| 欲不欲 (64) | the reflexive formula | *wants the not-wanting* — one verb on both sides, as at chapter 63 |
| 將欲 (29, 36 ×4) | the conditional | *would* |

**The formula outranks the split.** In 欲不欲 the second 欲 stands with nothing after it, which by the rule above would read *desire*. But X-the-not-X takes one English on both sides wherever the book uses it (63's *do the not-doing*, *serve the not-serving*), and the formula is a signature of the book. So chapter 64 reads *want* twice.

### What no rule can enforce

***Wish* and *craving* are forbidden** since chapters 77 and 46 left them (2026-11-19); *urge* and *relinquish* left the verse with chapter 37.

***Want* is not 求 (*qiú* — to seek)'s.** Chapter 75's 以其上求生之厚 (*yǐ qí shàng qiú shēng zhī hòu*) once read *"those over them want their own lives so rich,"* and since 2026-11-20 reads *"those over them seek life too thickly,"* giving 求 its own *seek*, as at chapter 62. 欲 is not in chapter 75, so no rule would see *want* return there; `concordance.py --english "want"`, run by hand, does.

***Desire* is free.** No other character in the book wears it, and nothing needs to be forbidden to keep it so.

### Left open

**No line waits on this entry.** Chapters 15, 37, 46, 66 and 77 were settled on 2026-11-19, all by Shalom's call; 欲 now reads *desire*, *want* or *would* in every one of its lines, by grammar.

**The silks have two readings our base does not, and neither is yet recorded per silk.** Chapter 57's 我欲不欲 and chapter 37's 不辱 were read in Wikisource's collated silk text, which does not say which silk prints which. Both want rows in `sources/variants.yaml` once checked against each silk. The base and both commentaries print 我無欲 and 無欲 / 不欲, and we keep them.

**Whether the sage wants is the real tension, and the book leaves it standing.** Chapter 3 makes the people desireless; chapter 57 makes the sage so. Yet chapter 66 hands the sage a want, to be above the people, and Wang Bi's chapter 57 and the silks make desirelessness itself the thing wanted. The English should let the sage want where the Chinese says so, at 64 and 66, and not tidy the sage into someone beyond wanting. That is the overlay the Stoic and Buddhist words would bring, from the other direction.
