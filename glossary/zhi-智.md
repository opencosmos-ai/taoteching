## 智 (Zhì) — Cleverness

*Say "jr," falling. Eight lines across six chapters. Knowing made into something a person has and uses, and in this book it always faces outward.*

**Locked: 智 → "cleverness" / "clever."** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-16. Not "wisdom," which approves where the book doubts, and puts a hymn into chapter 19. Not "intelligence," a modern measure. Not "cunning," which is already the English of 巧 (qiǎo — craft). Not "guile," which condemns before the line does. One flexion: "the knowers" at chapter 3, where a fork between the witnesses is held open.)*

The book seems to say two things about this word. Chapter 33 ranks it, one rung below seeing yourself clearly. Chapters 18, 19 and 65 condemn it. So is 智 one word or two? **One word, judged by where it points.** Every line aims it outward, at other people, at a state, at things to be handled, and the book's verdict changes with the target, not with the word.

### The graph: knowing, with a mouth in it

The Shuowen has no 智. Xu Shen files the word under its older form, 𥏼 (zhì):

> 𥏼，識詞也。从白从亏从知。
> *"𥏼 is a word for recognizing. From 白, from 亏 (yú — breath going out), from 知 (zhī — to know)."*

The 白 here is not *white*. It is Xu Shen's 白 radical, which he calls another form of 自 (zì — the nose), standing for the breath of speech, which comes out through the nose and helps the mouth. His 知 is the same kind of thing: 詞也。从口从矢 — *"a word. From 口 (kǒu — mouth) and 矢 (shǐ — arrow)."* The arrow is probably only there for its sound. To Xu Shen both are words: to know is to be able to say.

**Older than the Shuowen, the two were one graph.** The oracle-bone form read as this word is commonly analysed as a grown person (大, *dà*), a mouth (口, *kǒu*) and a child (子, *zǐ*): knowing passed by mouth from an adult to a child. Bronze inscriptions add a further part (甘, *gān*, later written 曰 or 白). Over the centuries the person was redrawn as 矢 (*shǐ* — arrow) and the child as 于 (*yú*), which is Xu Shen's 亏, and in the Western Han the 于 dropped away, leaving 智. 知 is the same graph worn down along another path. The reading of the oracle-bone graph is one paleographers' analysis, and it is contested. The end of the story is not: 知 and 智 were written interchangeably for centuries.

**The sound says what the graph only implies.** Reconstructed Old Chinese has 知 as \*tre and 智 as \*tre-s (Baxter and Sagart). The final \*-s is a suffix that turns a verb into a noun: *to know* becomes knowing as a thing one has, a capacity. In later Chinese the suffix became the falling tone, and that tone is the whole difference between *zhī* and *zhì*. Heshang Gong's text marks it at chapter 3, where it writes the graph 知 and adds 知音智 (*zhī yīn zhì* — "this 知 is read *zhì*").

**No early witness separates them in ink.** The Guodian slips (~300 BCE) write 智 for *to know* as well, in the lines our base writes 天下皆知 (*tiānxià jiē zhī* — "everyone under heaven knows," chapter 2), 知止 (*zhī zhǐ* — "knowing when to stop," chapter 32) and 知足 (*zhī zú* — "knowing enough," chapter 46). The Mawangdui silks go the other way, and write 知 in every one of the six chapters where our base has 智. Only the received text keeps two graphs. **The distinction lives in the sound, and what it marks is grammar:** 知 is the act of knowing, 智 the faculty.

**Where the picture stops.** Nothing in the graph doubts. It pictures knowledge handed on, or said aloud. The book's unease comes from what that knowing is aimed at, which no graph can show.

### Eight lines, and every one faces outward

> *One who knows others is clever.*
> *One who knows themselves is clear-seeing.*
>
> 知人者智，
> 自知者明。
>
> — chapter 33

> *Not to value the teacher, not to love the raw material:*
> *however clever, one is greatly lost.*
>
> 不貴其師，不愛其資，
> 雖智大迷，
>
> — chapter 27

> *so the people are ever unknowing, ever desireless,*
> *and the knowers never push to act.*
>
> 常使民無知無欲。
> 使夫智者不敢為也。
>
> — chapter 3

> *When the great Tao is discarded, humaneness and duty appear.*
> *When cleverness and erudition arise, great hypocrisy appears.*
>
> 大道廢，有仁義；
> 智慧出，有大偽；
>
> — chapter 18

> *Cut off sagehood, abandon cleverness, and the people benefit a hundredfold.*
>
> 絕聖棄智，民利百倍；
>
> — chapter 19

> *The people are hard to govern*
> *because they have too much cleverness.*
> *So to govern a state with cleverness robs the state.*
> *Not to govern it with cleverness is the state's good fortune.*
>
> 民之難治，
> 以其智多。
> 故以智治國，國之賊；
> 不以智治國，國之福。
>
> — chapter 65

**In every line, 智 is aimed at something outside the one who has it: other people, a state, things to be handled. Not once does it turn back on the self.** That turn is what 明 (míng — clear-seeing) makes in chapter 33, and the book ranks it higher for making it.

The commentators read it the same way. Wang Bi on chapter 33: 知人者智而已矣，未若自知者超智之上也 — *"knowing others is cleverness and no more; it does not match knowing oneself, which goes beyond cleverness."* Heshang Gong: 能知人好惡是為智 — *"being able to know what people like and dislike: that is cleverness."* It is the skill of reading others. On chapter 27 Wang Bi names the fault exactly: 雖有其智，自任其智，不因物，於其道必失 — *"though they have cleverness, they lean on their own cleverness and do not go along with things, and so are sure to lose their way."* And on chapter 3 he says where it lives: 心懷智而腹懷食，虛有智而實無知也 — *"the heart holds cleverness and the belly holds food: empty the one that holds cleverness, fill the one that holds no knowing."*

**The book does not call it bad. It calls it not enough.** Wang Bi on chapter 19: 聖智，才之善也 — *"sagehood and cleverness are the best of talent."* The chapter's own verdict on the three pairs it severs is 以為文不足 (*yǐ wéi wén bù zú* — "taken as cultural form, not enough"). Chapter 33 sets cleverness one rung down. Chapter 27 says it will not keep you on the road. Only when it governs, at 18 and 65, does it do harm.

The tomb that held the Guodian Laozi also held a text that defines the pair chapter 19 cuts off: 見而知之，智也。聞而知之，聖也 — *"to see it and know it is 智; to hear it and know it is 聖 (shèng — sagehood)."* There, 智 is knowing by the eye, the sense that points away from you. The pairing is argued in `glossary/sheng-聖.md`.

### Cleverness from above teaches cleverness below

**Both commentators read chapters 18 and 65 as one mechanism: a state governed by cleverness makes its people clever.**

Wang Bi on chapter 18: 行術用明以察姦偽，趣覩形見，物知避之，故智慧出則大偽生也 — *"a ruler works by technique and turns sharp sight on wrongdoing; their aims are glimpsed and their shape is seen, and people know how to dodge them. So when cleverness comes out, great artifice is born."* On chapter 65 he follows the chain to its end: 以智術動民，邪心既動，復以巧術防民之偽，民知其術防，隨而避之，思惟密巧，奸偽益滋 — *"stir the people with clever techniques, and once their crooked hearts are stirred, guard against their falsity with craftier ones: the people learn the techniques and the guards, follow them and slip round them, their thinking grows secret and crafty, and deceit multiplies."* He opens that note with a pun: 智猶治也 (*zhì yóu zhì yě* — "智 is like 治 (zhì — to govern)"). The two words sound alike, and he treats cleverness as a way of governing.

Heshang Gong agrees from the other side. Chapter 18: 智惠之君賤德而貴言，賤質而貴文，下則應之以為大偽姦詐 — *"a clever ruler scorns integrity and prizes words, scorns substance and prizes ornament, and those below answer with great artifice and deceit."* Chapter 65: 使智惠之人治國之政事，必遠道德，妄作威福，為國之賊 — *"put clever people in charge of the state's affairs and they will stray far from the Tao and integrity, and deal out punishment and favor at whim: the state's robbers."*

So chapter 65's *"they have too much cleverness"* is the end of a chain the governing began, which is how `notes/reading.md` reads the chapter. **The silks write Wang Bi's pun into the line itself.** Both write 知 (zhī — to know, and also to preside over) for 治 as well as for 智: *"to know the state by knowing is the state's robber."* Where our base ends on 福 (fú — good fortune), they end on 德 (dé — integrity). Our base keeps 治 and 福, which both commentators read (`sources/variants.yaml`).

### The oldest witness drops it from chapter 18 and keeps it in 19

**The Guodian slips carry chapter 18 complete, run on from chapter 17, and they have three couplets, not four.** The line 智慧出，有大偽 (*zhì huì chū, yǒu dà wěi* — "cleverness and quick wit arise, and there is great artifice") is not there. In the oldest copy, when the great Tao is lost, humaneness and duty appear, then devotion and tenderness, then ministers, and cleverness is not on the list.

Chapter 19 sits on the same slips, and there 智 survives: 絕智棄辯 (*jué zhì qì biàn* — "cut off cleverness, abandon disputation"), with 聖 (shèng — sagehood) gone from the line. **So the oldest witness takes 智 out of the chapter that blames it for decline, and keeps it in the chapter that tells you to let it go.** Our base keeps all four couplets of chapter 18, as it keeps its other received readings. The fork is recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`.

### Why "clever": an English that admires and doubts in one word

English *clever* is praise held at arm's length. A clever child is admired. A clever answer can be too clever by half. A clever scheme is a reason to check your pockets. That is this character's standing in the book: a real talent, Wang Bi's 才之善 (*cái zhī shàn* — "the best of talent"), ranked below clear-seeing, and harmful once it runs a state. **Clever carries both without choosing, and the line decides.** *Wisdom* would carry only approval and *guile* only blame (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`).

It passes the pointing test: Laozi could point at the clever trader and the clever minister. And it keeps chapter 19's shock, which depends on the thing abandoned being something everyone admires.

### What is set aside

**Wisdom, wise.** The conventional English, and the reason chapter 19 reads in most translations like a riddle: *abandon wisdom*. The shock is real in the Chinese, since 智 is one of the Confucian virtues 仁義禮智 (*rén yì lǐ zhì* — humaneness, duty, ritual, wisdom). But in English *wisdom* is the highest word for knowing, and in this book that place belongs to 明 (míng — clear-seeing), which chapter 33 ranks above 智. A wisdom that ranks below clear-seeing, cannot keep you on the road (27) and robs a state (65) is not what an English reader hears in the word. It also fails the hymn test. *Wise* is ruled out for 聖 (shèng — the sage) as well, and this list is what holds that ruling in chapters 3, 19 and 27, where the two stand together.

**Intelligence, intelligent.** A modern measure, scored and ranked, and it fails the pointing test. It names a capacity in the abstract, where 智 in this book is always aimed at something.

**Knowledge, knowing.** These are 知's English (zhī — to know): *unknowing* at chapters 3 and 10, *knowing* at 16, *knows* at 33. The witnesses blur the two graphs, but the received text separates them, and so does the English. Chapter 33 puts *knows* and *clever* in the same line, 知人者智 (*zhī rén zhě zhì* — "one who knows others is clever"), and the line needs both.

**Cunning.** Already the English of 巧 (qiǎo — craft) at chapters 19 and 57, and used again at 58. A word doing duty for two characters would be useless for either (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`).

**Guile.** It decides the verdict before the line does, and in chapter 65 it turns the claim into a platitude. Nobody defends governing with guile, so saying it robs the state shocks no one. The Chinese says governing with *cleverness*, a recognised virtue and the minister's skill, robs the state, which is the same shock chapter 19 depends on. The commentators do not license *guile* either: both make artifice what cleverness produces, not what it is. Heshang Gong: 以其智多，故為巧偽 — *"because their cleverness is great, they therefore practise artifice."* Wang Bi sets them side by side, 多智巧詐 (*duō zhì qiǎo zhà* — "much cleverness, craft and deceit"). And the pairing that seemed to call for *guile*, against 愚 (yú) as *guileless*, rests on a mistranslation: Wang Bi's gloss on 愚 is 無知 (wú zhī — unknowing), the book's own phrase at chapters 3 and 10, not "without cunning." Chapter 65 read *guile* until Shalom's call of 2026-11-16.

### What no rule can enforce

**Cunning is ruled out for 智 and cannot go on `forbidden:`.** 巧 (qiǎo — craft) shares chapter 19 and prints *cunning* there, and the checker gates on the chapter, not the line. Only this entry holds it.

**Wise is matched as a bare string**, so it would also fire inside *otherwise* or *likewise* in these six chapters. None prints either today. If one ever does, a `lock-ok` waiver in that chapter's notes is the remedy, not a narrower list.

### Left open

**No English can carry the silks' pun at chapter 65,** 知 (zhī — to know) written for 治 (zhì — to govern). *To know the state by knowing* and *to govern a state with cleverness* are one phrase in the silks' ink and two in any English.

**慧 (huì) beside it at chapter 18 reads *erudition*, and 慧 is not learning.** The Shuowen: 慧，儇也 — *"慧 is nimbleness,"* quickness of mind. Learning is 學 (xué — study), a word of its own at chapters 20 and 48. 智慧 (zhì huì) reads more like *cleverness and quick wits*. That is a change to a verse line, so it is Shalom's call, and it is owed in `WORKLIST.md` T2-2.

**Chapter 3's *knowers* keep a fork open on purpose.** Wang Bi's text writes 智者 (zhì zhě — the clever ones) and reads the operators the line restrains. Heshang Gong's writes 知者 (zhī zhě — those who know) and reads deep, quiet knowers, close to chapter 56's. The English does not choose (`notes/manuscript.md`).

**The oldest witness has no cleverness in chapter 18.** Our base keeps the line. But any reading of chapter 18 as an attack on cleverness rests on a line the copy of ~300 BCE did not carry.
