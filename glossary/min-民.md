## 民 (Mín) — The People

*Say "meen," rising. Thirty-two appearances across thirteen chapters, nearly all of them in the book's political half.*

**Locked: 民 → "the people,"** always with the article, **and "the people" belongs to 民 alone.** *(Not "the masses," "the multitude," "the populace," "subjects," "citizens," "commoners," "peasants," or "the governed." That 人 (*rén* — a person) must not wear "the people" is a ruling no check can hold; see* What no rule can enforce *below. 百姓 (*bǎi xìng*), the other word for the population, is locked to "everyone": `baixing-百姓.md`.)*

民 is the people **as seen from the seat of government**: the population a ruler governs, taxes, frightens, loves, or leaves alone. The word is never neutral about who is looking. In every line but one, someone stands across from 民: a sage, a ruler, a state, those over them. Laozi's whole political teaching is addressed to that someone, and 民 is the name for the ones on the other side of the address.

### The graph: sprouts, or a blinded eye, and nobody knows

**Our vendored 說文解字 (*Shuōwén Jiězì*, c. 100 CE): 眾萌也。从古文之象** — *"the many sprouts. From the shape of its ancient form."*

萌 (*méng*) is a shoot breaking the soil. Xu Shen defines the people as **a field coming up**: numerous, low, alive, and not yet anything in particular. 段玉裁 (*Duàn Yùcái*, 1815) defends the reading against editions that print 氓 (*méng* — the common folk) in its place. 萌 is the original, he says, and the ancients called the people 萌, with many examples in Han writing. 林義光 (*Lín Yìguāng*, 1920) arrived at the same picture from the bronze graphs, which he read as a plant pushing up out of the ground.

**The other reading is the one that circulates, and it is a theory.** 郭沫若 (*Guō Mòruò*, 1930s) read the bronze graph as **an eye pierced by a blade**: captives blinded in one eye and set to work, so that 民 began as a word for slaves. 白川靜 (*Shirakawa Shizuka*) and 藤堂明保 (*Tōdō Akiyasu*) took it up. Its critics, 永澤要二 (*Nagasawa Yōji*) and 加納喜光 (*Kanō Yoshimitsu*), point out that nothing supports it beyond the shape. The oracle-bone graphs once claimed for 民 are themselves disputed, and the form securely identified as 民 begins with the bronzes. Guo was building a Marxist history of ancient China, and a blinded slave fit the slave society that history required. The reading may be right. But it is one scholar's argument, made inside a framework, and it should not be quoted as the meaning of the graph.

**What the two readings share is position.** A sprouting field and a captive labourer are both **low, numerous, and acted upon**. Whichever picture is true, the graph does not draw a person standing upright, as 人 does. It draws a mass seen from above.

### The evidence: 民 always has someone across from it

The pattern is the argument, and it is complete enough to read as a table.

| Ch | Line | Who stands across |
|---|---|---|

| 10 | 愛民治國 | the one asked *can you* |
| 19 | 民利百倍 · 民復孝慈 | the ruler told to cut off and abandon |
| 32 | 民莫之令而自均 | the rulers who could hold to the Tao (侯王) |
| 57 | 民彌貧 · 民自化 · 民自正 · 民自富 · 民自樸 | 我 (*wǒ* — I), the sage speaking |
| 58 | 其民淳淳 · 其民缺缺 | 其政 (*qí zhèng* — its government) |
| 65 | 非以明民 · 民之難治 | the ancients who were masterful at the Tao |
| 66 | 欲上民 · 欲先民 · 民不重 · 民不害 | the one who would rise above them |
| 72 | 民不畏威 | the authority they stop fearing |
| 74 | 民不畏死 · 若使民常畏死 | the one who would kill to frighten them |
| 75 | 民之飢 · 民之難治 · 民之輕死 | 其上 (*qí shàng* — those over them), three times |
| 80 | 小國寡民 · 使民重死 · 使民復結繩 · 民至老死 | the small state that *lets* them |

**Chapter 57 shows the relation working.** The sage speaks four times in the first person, and each time 民 answers in the reflexive: *I do not do, and the people transform themselves.* 民 is defined by being on the receiving end of rule. The teaching is that the best rule gives them nothing to receive.

**Chapter 75 names the direction outright.** 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) comments on the whole chapter in one sentence: 言民之所以僻，治之所以亂，皆由上，不由其下也，民從上也 — *"this says that the reason the people go astray, and the reason order falls into disorder, all comes from above and not from below. The people follow those above."* Hunger, unruliness, and the cheapness of life are all traced upward. 民 is the word for the people as the ones it happens to.

### The witnesses draw the line too

The copying tradition let 民 and 人 change places three times, and each change falls where the distinction is being drawn.

| Ch | Our base | The older reading | A ruler in the line? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 57 | 人多利器 | **民**多利器: Guodian, and the lemmas of both 王弼 and 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) | yes: *the state grows benighted* |
| 60 | 聖人亦不傷人 | 聖人亦不傷**民**: 韓非 (*Hán Fēi*) | yes: the chapter is governing a large state |
| 64 | 民之從事 | **人**之敗也: Guodian, bundle C | no: anyone's affairs |

**In every case the oldest reading puts 民 where a ruler is present and 人 where none is.** Three cases is a pattern worth noticing and too few to rest on alone, but it agrees with the thirty-two lines above. And chapter 57 at Guodian sets the two side by side, a line apart: **民**多利器，而邦滋昏；**人**多智，而奇物滋起. The people's weapons trouble the state; *someone's* cleverness breeds strange contrivances. 河上公 makes that second 人 the rulers themselves, 人謂人君百里諸侯也 (*"人 here means the rulers, the lords of a hundred li"*), while 王弼 glosses it as the people's cleverness. They disagree about who 人 is. Neither treats it as 民.

### What is set aside, and why

**"The masses."** Nineteenth-century class politics, and it is the word the blinded-slave etymology was built to support. It makes 民 a bloc, a demographic, an object of history. Chapter 80's villagers who *find their food sweet, their clothes fine* are not the masses. `zhong-眾.md` rejects the word for the same reason.

**"The multitude."** King James English. It fails the hymn test outright, and it belongs, if anywhere, to 眾 (*zhòng* — the crowd), which is a plurality and not a relation.

**"The governed."** The most accurate label, and the most tempting. It says exactly what the table above shows. That is the problem: it states as a definition what the lines *show* by putting a ruler across from 民 every time. *"I do not do, and the governed transform themselves"* turns a line about people into a line about administration, and *"the governed go hungry"* takes the hunger away. The Chinese performs the relation. It does not name it. `process/principles/perform-dont-label.md`.

**"Subjects," "citizens."** Each brings a constitution with it. *Subjects* have a sovereign, *citizens* have rights, and 民 has neither in this sense. Both carry a theory of the state the book does not share.

**"The populace," "the population."** Census words. They count where 民 is a relation.

**"Commoners," "peasants."** They name a rank and an occupation. Some 民 were peasants. The word does not say so, and chapter 80's people keep records, own boats, and wear fine clothes.

### The working register

- the default, with the article → **the people** (愛民 → "loving the people"; 民之難治 → "the people are hard to govern")
- possessive → **the people's** (使民心不亂 → "the people's hearts are not thrown into disorder")
- under a number word, the article drops by English grammar → **people** (小國寡民 → "a small state, few people")
- **ch 64 alone → people**, bare: 民之從事 → *"people always ruin their affairs."* It is the one 民 in the book with no ruler in sight, and Guodian reads 人 there. The flexion records the line as it reads and why.

### What no rule can enforce

**人 must not render as "the people," and the checker cannot say so.** A `forbidden:` entry works by chapter presence, and chapter 57 holds both characters: 民 five times, legitimately *the people*, and 人 twice. Any ban on *the people* would flag 民's own lines. The ruling is for the reader: **in a chapter with 民, *the people* is 民's, and 人 takes *people*, *a person*, or *others*.** Chapter 57's *"the more sharp weapons the people have"* is not a breach: it renders the 民 that every witness but our base prints, a fork recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`.

**百姓 is held by its own entry.** It is locked to *everyone*, and it forbids *the people*, which the checker can enforce because none of its chapters (5, 17, 49) holds 民.

### Left open

**Chapter 60 fits the pattern, and we keep our base against it.** 韓非 reads 聖人亦不傷**民**, which is exactly where the pattern above would put 民. But both transmitted commentators read 人 in both clauses, and 王弼 builds the next line on the symmetry: 神不傷人，聖人亦不傷人…故曰兩不相傷也, *"the numinous does not harm people, the sage also does not harm people… therefore it says the two do not harm each other."* The couplet needs the same object twice. 韓非 is explaining the line in his own terms, and an explanation can sharpen a distinction the text left flat.

**"The people" has its own echoes.** *We the People*, *a people's republic*, *let my people go*. English hears popular sovereignty, revolution, or a covenant in the phrase, and 民 carries none of them. It is the plainest English the word has, and it fails the hymn test less badly than anything else. But it is warmer and prouder than 民 is, and the reader who hears *the people* as the sovereign hears the opposite of chapter 75, where the people are the ones a sovereign eats.
