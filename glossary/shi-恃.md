## 恃 (Shì) — To Rely On

*Say "shr," falling. Five lines across five chapters, and four of them are the same three characters.*

**Locked: 恃 → "rely on" — the heart putting its weight on something.** *(Not "presume," not "presuming," not "dependency.")*

為而不恃 (*wéi ér bù shì*) is conventionally read **"acts without presuming."** That is not a translation of 恃. It is an inference about what someone who 恃-ed would be doing — and it lands on *presumption*, a fault of manner, when the character is about **where your weight is**.

**恃 is the word for what a child does to a mother**, and the Tao Te Ching uses it in both directions.

### The graph: the heart's version of holding

恃 is **忄 (heart)** beside **寺**. The *Shuowen Jiezi* is one line:

> 恃，**賴**也。从心，寺聲。
> *"恃 is 賴 (lài — to rely). From 心 (heart), with 寺 as the sound."*

寺 is treated as phonetic there, but it is not a random noise. In its older form it is a **hand (又 / 寸) under a foot-graph (之)** — holding, attending, being posted at. And it seeds an entire family of words about holding, distinguished by *which part of you does it*:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **持** (*chí*) | 扌 hand + 寺 | to hold, in the hand |
| **侍** (*shì*) | 亻 person + 寺 | to attend upon, with the body |
| **待** (*dài*) | 彳 step + 寺 | to wait for, to await |
| **恃** (*shì*) | 忄 heart + 寺 | **to hold onto with the heart — to lean your weight on** |

That table is not decoration. **河上公 confirms it from inside the family**, glossing Ch 34 with a bare definition:

> 恃，**待**也。萬物皆恃道而生
> *"恃 is 待 (dài — to await, to depend on). The countless things all 恃 the Tao and so live."*

A Han commentator defines 恃 by its own cousin. The core is not calculation and not manner. It is **the heart resting its weight on something outside itself, and expecting that thing to be there.**

### The mother-word

The oldest use of 恃 in the language is a child's, and it is gendered with total precision. From the *Shijing* 《小雅·蓼莪》, older than the Tao Te Ching:

> 無父何**怙**，無母何**恃**
> *"With no father, whom do I 怙? With no mother, whom do I 恃?"*

怙 (*hù*) went to the father, 恃 to the mother — and classical Chinese later fossilized the pair into idiom: **失恃** (*shī shì*), "to lose one's 恃," is the euphemism for a mother's death. *(The Shijing line is ancient; the 失恃/失怙 idiom is a later crystallization of it. Neither text is vendored in `sources/` — flagged here as a classical locus, not as evidence from this repository's files.)*

**This matters for this book specifically.** 母 (*mǔ* — mother) is one of this edition's locked terms and one of its load-bearing images: 天下母 (*tiān xià mǔ* — mother of the world) at Ch 52, and Ch 20's closing 貴食母 — *"I prize feeding from the mother."* When Ch 34 says 萬物**恃**之以生, *"the countless things rely on it to live,"* it is reaching for the verb whose native object is a mother. The relation the Tao stands in to everything is the one an infant stands in to the person feeding it.

### The asymmetry — the whole finding

恃 occurs five times, and the direction reverses exactly once.

**Once, positive, of the Tao:**
- **34**: 萬物恃之以生而不辭 — *the countless things rely on it to live, and it refuses none.*

**Four times, negative, of the Tao or the sage:**
- **2, 10, 51, 77**: 為而不恃 — *acts yet relies on nothing.*

So the book's claim is a chiasm, and it is the mother relation stated as a principle: **be the thing that is relied on, and rely on nothing.** The mother is leaned on and does not lean back. Ch 34 supplies the other half of the picture in the same breath — 不辭 (*bù cí* — refuses none), 衣養萬物 (*yī yǎng wàn wù* — clothes and feeds the countless things) — and then refuses the credit: 而不為主, *and does not act as their master.*

**王弼 sharpens why the sage has nothing to rely on.** On Ch 10:

> 不禁其性，則物自濟，**何為之恃**
> *"If you do not block their nature, things sort themselves out — so what is there to rely on?"*

The sage cannot lean on their accomplishment because, done rightly, the accomplishment was not theirs. 王弼 finishes: 物自長足，不吾宰成 — *"things grow to their own fullness; I do not master them into being."*

**河上公 reads the unstated object as the repayment**, and does so identically in four places:

> 道所施為，**不恃望其報**也
> *"What the Tao gives out and does, it does not 恃望 (lean toward and watch for) its repayment."*

恃望 is the compound worth keeping in mind: the heart already tipped toward what is owed back. Ch 77 varies it only in the subject — 聖人為德施，不恃其報也, *"the sage gives out integrity and does not rely on its return."*

### The one thing you may rely on

**韓非** (*Hán Fēi*, d. 233 BCE), the oldest commentary in this repository, quotes Ch 64 with 恃 in the positive and names its object:

> **恃**萬物之自然而不敢為也
> *"Rely on the countless things being so of themselves, and do not push to act."*

That single line ties 恃 to two locked terms at once. 自然 (*zìrán* — so of itself) is the ground, and 敢 (*gǎn* — to push, to venture) is what you stop doing once you have that ground. **恃 is the hinge between them:** 無為 (*wúwéi* — non-doing) is possible because there is something that will hold your weight, and what holds it is things going of themselves.

Elsewhere 韓非 uses the word the other way, for the wrong supports — 入山不**恃**備以救害, *"entering the mountains, one does not rely on preparations to save oneself from harm"* (Ch 50); 夫**恃**貌而論情者，其情惡也, *"one who relies on appearance to argue about the substance — the substance is bad"* (Ch 38). And 王弼 on Ch 60 aims it at government: 夫**恃**威網以使物者，治之衰也 — *"to rely on the net of authority to manage things is governance in decay."*

The pattern across all of them: **恃 is neutral; what you put your weight on is not.**

### Why *rely on*, and what it costs

**Set aside: "presume," "presuming."** The retired reading, and the reason this entry exists. It renders an imagined attitude rather than the character, and it makes the line a warning against arrogance when the line is about load-bearing. It also collides with 敢 (*gǎn* — to push, to venture), whose entry already had to note that *presume* was blocked here.

**Set aside: "dependency."** Proposed at Ch 77 and rejected the same day. It is an abstract noun in a line of verbs, and in current English it carries addiction.

**Considered and set aside: "lean on."** This was the working choice for one draft, and it is the most faithful *picture* — it keeps the body and it keeps the mother. It lost on two grounds. First, **倚** (*yǐ* — to lean against) already holds *lean* in the manuscript, at Ch 58's 禍兮福之所倚 — *"disaster is what fortune leans on."* Using one English word for two characters the text keeps distinct blurs them for no gain. Second, 河上公's 不恃望其報 is about **counting on a return**, and *rely* carries that where *lean* underplays it.

**The cost of "rely on," stated plainly.** It is Latinate and its picture is dead: *rely* is *re-* + Old French *lier*, to bind — a body word once, an abstraction now. English readers will hear a mental act where the Chinese has a posture. **That loss is what this entry exists to hold**, and it is why the gloss above says *the heart putting its weight on something* rather than just repeating the dictionary.

There is one further risk. *"Acts yet relies on nothing"* can read as self-sufficiency — the self-made sage, needing no one — which would be an overlay, and a Stoic one. **Ch 34 is the guard against it.** There, the same character says that everything alive relies on the Tao, and the Tao turns none of it away. The sage relies on nothing not because relying is weakness, but because, as 王弼 says, there is nothing there that was theirs to lean on.

**Not forbidden, and deliberately: "depend."** Ch 2 renders 高下相傾 (*gāo xià xiāng qīng*) as *"high and low depend on each other,"* and 傾 sits in the same chapter as 恃. The checker gates on whether a character is present in a chapter, so it cannot express *right for 傾, wrong for 恃, same chapter*. That distinction lives here and in the reader.
