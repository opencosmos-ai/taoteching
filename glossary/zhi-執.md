## 執 (Zhí) — To Grasp

*Say "jr," rising. Nine lines across seven chapters, and the most famous of them is a warning.*

**Locked: 執 → "grasp" — and *seize* at chapter 74, where the seizing is an arrest.** *(Never "cling," never "attachment," never "embody," never "control.")*

### The graph: a hand and a pair of manacles

The *Shuowen Jiezi* does not hedge:

> 執，**捕罪人**也。从丮从幸，幸亦聲。
> *"執 is **to seize a criminal**. From 丮 (jǐ) and 幸 (xìng), 幸 also giving the sound."*

丮 is a hand thrust out to take hold. The element written 幸 here is not 幸 *"fortunate"* but 㚔 (*niè*), and in the oracle-bone and bronze forms of 執 the picture is legible without a dictionary: a kneeling figure with both hands closed inside a wooden restraint. A person, and a pair of manacles.

So 執 is not holding. It is **taking into custody** — the hand that closes on a thing in order to keep it where you put it. Every other word in this family is about keeping something. 執 is about keeping something *that would otherwise get away.*

### The book uses it as a legal word twice, in plain sight

At chapter 74 the sense is the dictionary's, undiluted. 若使民常畏死，而為奇者，吾得**執**而殺之 — *"suppose the people always feared death, and I could 執 whoever steps out of line and kill them."* 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) reads it as statute:

> 以道教化而民不從，反為竒巧，乃應**王法**執而殺之，誰敢有犯者。老子傷時王不先道德化之而先**刑罰**。
> *"If they are taught by the Tao and the people do not follow, turning instead to cunning tricks, then it accords with **the king's law** to 執 them and kill them, and who would dare offend? Laozi grieves that the kings of his day did not transform them by the Tao and integrity first, but by **punishment** first."*

At chapter 79 it is an instrument rather than a person: 聖人**執**左契 — the sage 執s the left half of a split tally, the physical bond that made a debt enforceable before there were written laws.

> 古者聖人執左契合符信也，無文書法律，刻契合符以為信也。 — 河上公
> *"In antiquity the sage held the left tally and matched the token of trust. There were no documents or statutes; they carved tallies and matched them to make trust."*

說文 on 契 is 大約也, *"a great covenant."* Both of these are custody. Neither is a metaphor.

### Which is what 執者失之 has been saying all along

為者敗之，**執**者失之 (29, 64) is usually read as a caution against clinging — a counsel of inner detachment. The character says something harder and more political. The line's object is 天下 (*tiān xià* — the world), and the clause just before it names what the world is: 天下**神器**，不可為也，不可執也 — *"the world is a sacred vessel. It cannot be handled. It cannot be grasped."*

You cannot take a sacred vessel into custody. 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) makes it a statement about what kind of thing the world is, not about the state of your heart:

> 萬物以自然為性，故可因而不可為也，可**通**而不可**執**也。物有徃來而執之，故必失矣。
> *"The countless things have what is so of themselves for their nature. So they can be gone along with but not handled; they can be passed through but not grasped. Things have their coming and going, and to grasp them is necessarily to lose them."*

And he brackets 執 with the book's other blade-word: 不施為**執割**也 — *"imposes no handling, no grasping, no cutting"* — 割 (*gē* — to sever) being ch 28's 大制不割, *"the great carving severs nothing."*

河上公 draws the distinction that matters, on the same line, and refuses to let the verb take the blame:

> 執利遇患，執道全身。
> *"Grasp advantage and you meet trouble; grasp the Tao and you keep your body whole."*

### The two "positive" instances are not exceptions. They are the point.

執古之道，以御今之有 (14) and 執大象，天下往 (35) are instructions, and they use the custody word without apology. Read against chapters 29 and 64 they are not a contradiction but a joke with a straight face: **you cannot take the world into custody, so take the one thing that will not break in your hand.** An English that softens 執 to *hold* in these two lines and hardens it back to *grasp* in the others resolves a tension the Chinese leaves standing, and severs the link between the four chapters.

| ch | line | English |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | 執古之道 | grasp the ancient Tao |
| 29 | 不可執也 · 執者失之 | it cannot be grasped · those who grasp it lose it |
| 35 | 執大象 | grasp the great image |
| 64 | 執者失之 · 無執 | those who grasp it lose it · does not grasp |
| 69 | 執無兵 | grasping where there is no weapon |
| 74 | 執而殺之 | **seize** whoever steps out of line and kill them |
| 79 | 執左契 | grasps the left half of the tally |

**The flexion at 74 is not an exception to the character. It is the dictionary headword.** English *grasp* covers the hand and the metaphor but has no judicial sense at all; *"I could grasp whoever steps out of line and kill them"* is not English. 捕罪人也 is, in three characters, *seize a criminal* — so the one line in the book where the state actually arrests somebody takes the one English word that says so.

### What is set aside

**"cling" and "attachment"** import *upādāna* — the second link of the Buddhist chain of dependent origination — into a text written before Buddhism reached China, and they relocate the problem. Clinging is a condition of the heart; 執 is a thing you do with your hands to something outside you. Chapters 29 and 64 are about **governing**, and a ruler who has stopped clinging has still seized the state.

**"embody"** deletes the hand and reverses the direction: 執 puts the object in your grip, *embody* puts it inside you. At 執大象 (35) the great image is precisely what does not fit inside anybody.

**"control"** is the mechanistic temptation this edition watches for. You control a system; you grasp a vessel, and you can break it. 神器 and 不可執 are one gesture, and an abstraction cuts it in half.

**"possess"** belongs to 有 (*yǒu* — to have), which is locked in `wu-you-無有.md`.

### What no rule can enforce

***"hold"* is not on the forbidden list, and cannot be.** 持 (*chí* — to hold in the hand) stands in chapter 64 beside 執, and *hold* is 持's word on that page. A rule keyed to 執's presence in a chapter would fire on 持's own line. This is the standing limit of the harness — right for that character, wrong for this one, same chapter — and the distinction lives here and in the reader instead. See `chi-持.md`, `shou-守.md`, `bao-保.md`: four characters, four different hands.

### Left open

**Whose half is the left half.** 執左契 keeps 左 (*zuǒ* — left) in the English without saying which party held it. The Han commentators describe the practice without settling the side, and the traditions disagree: on one reading the sage holds the creditor's half and declines to press it, on the other they hold the debtor's half and have no claim to press. The line 而不責於人 — *"and never asks for the other"* — is pointed under the first reading and merely descriptive under the second. 王弼 says only 左契防怨之所由生也, *"the left tally prevents what gives rise to grudges."* We render the character and leave the side to the reader.
