## 牝 (Pìn) — The Female

*Say "peen," falling. Five appearances in three chapters. The book's word for the female as a body: she has a gate, she mates, and she lies beneath.*

**Locked: 牝 (*pìn*) → "the female," in every line, and its partner 牡 (*mǔ*) → "the male."** *(Not "the receptive female," which adds a quality no line names. Not "the feminine," which turns an animal into an abstraction. Not "the Female," since a capital makes her a doctrine.)* *(The English was settled in the verse before this entry, at chapters 6, 55 and 61. The entry argues for it and holds it. That 牝 and 雌 (cí — the hen) get two entries, not one, is Shalom's call, 2026-10-26.)*

### The graph: an animal, and its sex

> 牝，畜母也。从牛匕聲。易曰：畜牝牛，吉。
> *"牝: the female of livestock. From 牛 (niú — cattle), with 匕 (bǐ) as its sound. The Changes says: 'Rearing a cow brings good fortune.'"* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

**The class is 牛 (*niú* — cattle), and the class is livestock, not the ox alone.** The Shuowen's word is 畜 (*chù* — a reared animal), and the quotation it chooses, from hexagram 30 of the Changes, is a cow being raised. 牝 is the word a herder or a priest choosing an animal for sacrifice would use: the female of the herd.

**The Shuowen defines its partner in matching words.** 牡，畜父也。从牛土聲 — *"牡: the male of livestock. From 牛 (niú — cattle), with 土 (tǔ) as its sound."* And it defines the book's other pair the same way, among birds: 雌 (*cí* — the hen) is 鳥母 (*niǎo mǔ* — "the mother among birds") and 雄 (*xióng* — the rooster) is 鳥父 (*niǎo fù* — "the father among birds"). So the Han dictionary holds two pairs of words for sex, one for animals that walk and one for birds, and the Tao Te Ching uses both pairs. This entry is the first. `glossary/` owes the second its own.

### Three chapters, and she is a body in each

> *The spirit of the valley is deathless.*
> *This is called the dark female.*
> *The dark female's gate —*
> *this is called the root of sky and earth.*
>
> 谷神不死，
> 是謂玄牝。
> 玄牝之門，
> 是謂天地根。
>
> — chapter 6

> *They do not yet know the union of female and male, yet their whole being quickens with life — this is vital essence at its utmost.*
>
> 未知牝牡之合而全作，精之至也。
>
> — chapter 55

> *the confluence of the world, the female of the world.*
> *The female always overcomes the male by stillness.*
> *By stillness she is underneath.*
>
> 天下之交，天下之牝。
> 牝常以靜勝牡，
> 以靜為下。
>
> — chapter 61

**In each line she is something you could point at.**

- **Chapter 6: she gives birth.** She has a gate, and sky and earth come out of it. Wang Bi: 門，玄牝之所由也 — *"the gate is where the dark female comes from."* The Chinese names her twice, so the gate is hers, and the verse keeps the repeat (`chapters/006.md`).
- **Chapter 55: she mates.** 牝牡之合 (*pìn mǔ zhī hé* — "the joining of female and male") is what the newborn does not yet know. Heshang Gong moves the animal words onto people: 赤子未知男女之合會而陰作怒者，由精氣多之所致也 — *"the newborn does not yet know the joining of man and woman, yet its hidden part rises; this comes of abundant vital essence and breath."* 男女 (*nán nǚ* — man and woman) is his word. The book's is the herder's.
- **Chapter 61: she lies beneath.** The female overcomes the male by stillness, and by stillness she is below. That is a posture, and the chapter builds a politics on it: the large state should be the downstream flow, the place everything runs to. Wang Bi on 天下之牝 (*tiān xià zhī pìn* — "the female of the world"): 靜而不求，物自歸之也 — *"still and seeking nothing, things come home to her of themselves."*

### Described, never asked

**In all five lines, the female is spoken about.** No line tells the reader to be her, to hold to her, or to become her. She is named (6), she is half of a joining (55), she is a likeness for a state (61).

**雌 (*cí* — the hen) is the opposite.** It stands in two lines, and in both it is asked of you: 守其雌 (*shǒu qí cí* — "hold to the hen," ch 28) and 能為雌乎 (*néng wéi cí hū* — "can you be the hen?," ch 10). 守 (*shǒu* — to hold to) is a post you are answerable for (`glossary/shou-守.md`), and 為 (*wéi* — to be, to serve as) is a part you play. So the book has one word for the female as a body and another for the female as a place a person can stand. That pattern is a reason to look, not a proof (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`). Looking, it holds in every line, and it is why the two characters have separate entries.

### What the commentators did with her

**Both commentators turn the female into qualities, and both merge her with the hen.**

- **Wang Bi on chapter 61 glosses her with the other word:** 牝，雌也。雄躁動貪欲，雌常以靜，故能勝雄也 — *"the female is the hen. The rooster is restless and greedy; the hen is always still, and so can overcome the rooster."* The text's 牡 (*mǔ* — the male) becomes 雄 (*xióng* — the rooster), and the line is retold among birds.
- **Heshang Gong on chapter 10 joins the two in one phrase:** 治身當如雌牝安靜柔弱 — *"in caring for the body, be like the hen and the female: calm, still, soft and yielding."*
- **Heshang Gong on chapter 6 makes her earth and the mouth:** 玄，天也，於人為鼻；牝，地也，於人為口 — *"the dark is sky, and in a person the nose; the female is earth, and in a person the mouth."* In the same note, 魄者雌也 — *"the bodily soul is the hen."*
- **Heshang Gong on chapter 61 calls her yin:** 牝者陰□也，柔謙和而不昌也 — *"the female is yin [one graph lost]: soft, humble, harmonious, and does not lead off."* The printed graph is 昌 (*chāng* — to flourish). His note on chapter 10 says the same thing with 唱 (*chàng* — to lead the singing): 和而不唱 (*hé ér bù chàng* — "harmonious, and does not lead off").

**The text does none of this.** It keeps her an animal, and it keeps 牝 and 雌 apart. 陰 (*yīn*) stands once in all 81 chapters, in chapter 42 beside 陽 (*yáng*), and never names the female (`notes/reading.md`). The commentaries are evidence that readers have always heard stillness and softness in her, and they are right to: chapter 61 says stillness outright. But the stillness is what she does, and the line keeps the animal that does it.

### 牡 (mǔ — the male)

**The male stands in two lines, and never without her.** 牝牡 (*pìn mǔ* — "female and male," 55) and 牝常以靜勝牡 (*pìn cháng yǐ jìng shèng mǔ* — "the female always overcomes the male by stillness," 61). Both times the female comes first in the Chinese, and the English keeps the order: *"the union of female and male,"* not the habitual *male and female*. The male has no line of his own in the book.

### What is set aside

**"the receptive female," "receptive."** No line says she receives. 受 (*shòu* — to receive) is not in any of them. The adjective tells the reader how to feel about her, so that they never have to meet the animal. It also borrows the I Ching's later convention for its hexagram 2, whose judgment praises 牝馬之貞 (*pìn mǎ zhī zhēn* — "the steadiness of a mare").

**"the feminine."** It turns a creature into a quality, which is the move both commentators make and the move the mother entry refuses: `glossary/mu-母.md` keeps every warm and bodily thing the tradition has tried to abstract away. The female in chapter 61 lies beneath the male. *The feminine* cannot lie beneath anything.

**"the Female."** A capital makes her a principle. Only the Tao keeps one.

**"the mother."** 母 (*mǔ* — mother) is another character, with its own entry. Chapter 6's dark female gives birth, but the line calls her 牝, and in chapters 55 and 61 she has borne nothing. Calling her *mother* would spend 母's English where 母 does not stand.

**"mare," "cow," "doe."** The line names no species. 牛 (*niú* — cattle) is the graph's class, and the Shuowen's gloss is the female of any reared animal.

### Working register

| Where 牝 (*pìn*) stands | English | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| a noun, named | *the dark female* | 6 (玄牝 *xuán pìn*, twice) |
| a noun, in a pair, before 牡 (*mǔ* — the male) | *female and male* | 55 |
| a noun, in apposition | *the female of the world* | 61 (天下之牝 *tiān xià zhī pìn*) |
| the subject, with 牡 as object | *the female … the male* | 61 |

**Her pronoun is *she*.** Chapter 61 reads *"By stillness she is underneath."* Standing rule 1 forbids *he* for the sage, because the sage has no sex in the Chinese. The female does, and the line is about it.

### What no rule can enforce

***Yin* is ruled out and cannot go on the `forbidden:` list.** The checker matches strings, not words, and *yin* is inside words the verse prints. It is held here and by the reader.

***Hen* is 雌's English, and cannot be forbidden here for the same reason:** *hen* is inside *when* and *then*. The two characters never share a chapter, so nothing in the book tests it.

***Mother* is ruled out above and left off the list.** A forbidden word fires wherever 牝 or 牡 stands, and the ruling is against borrowing another character's English, which this entry and `glossary/mu-母.md` already argue.

**The lock holds in this book only.** The I Ching vendors these locks as its own, and its 牝 names species: 牝馬 (*pìn mǎ* — a mare) in hexagram 2, and 牝牛 (*pìn niú* — a cow) in hexagram 30, the line the Shuowen quotes. Its hexagram 2 is also conventionally called *the receptive*, so a lock forbidding *receptive* there would flag a name rather than a rendering. So this entry carries `binds: [taoteching]`, and the I Ching rules its own female.

### Left open

**The graph's older story.** The Shuowen reads 匕 (*bǐ*) as sound only. Whether the oracle bones write the female with 匕 beside more animals than cattle, which would make 匕 a mark of the female, and whether that 匕 is the one that writes 妣 (*bǐ* — ancestress), has not been checked against a printed collection. If both hold, chapter 6's root of sky and earth has an ancestress in its graph. Until someone checks, it is a question.

**What *the female* loses.** In English *female* is a word from biology and from forms. In the Chinese it is a farmyard word, and the farmyard does not survive the translation. The entry carries it, on the same principle as 心 (*xīn* — heart) and 物 (*wù* — things).

**Whose stillness wins in chapter 61.** The commentators read it as a virtue: humility, softness, yielding. The line reads it as a posture: she is still, she is below, and she overcomes. Whether the chapter is teaching a quality or describing what an animal does, and then building a state on it, is left to the reader. The English keeps the animal so that both readings stay open.
