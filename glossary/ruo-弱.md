## 弱 (Ruò) — Yielding

*Say "rwaw," falling. Ten lines across six chapters, paired throughout with 柔 (róu — soft).*

**Locked: 弱 → "yielding," and the verb *yield* where it makes something so.** *(Not "weak," not "weaken," not "frail," not "feeble," not "feebleness," not "powerless.")*

**Weak** is the dictionary's first word for 弱, and it is a deficiency word in English. It names an absence — of force, of health, of resolve. Put it in the Tao Te Ching and the book acquires a doctrine it does not hold: that the way to prevail is to *lack* something.

That is not how this book uses the character, and it is not how the commentators read it where the book holds it up. They knew the plain sense. Heshang Gong writes 羸弱 (*léi ruò* — lean and feeble) at ch 29 and 脆弱 (*cuì ruò* — brittle) at ch 64, and Wang Bi writes 民强則國家弱 (*mín qiáng zé guó jiā ruò* — "when the people are strong, the state is weak") at ch 57. So 弱 can mean weak. The claim is narrower: what the book prizes as 弱 is never a lack.

### The graph

**The graph does not settle this.** Three readings are on record, and none of them is decisive:

- **A bending, in the Shuowen (c. 100 CE).** 橈也 (*náo yě* — "to bend"). 上象橈曲，彡象毛氂橈弱也 (*shàng xiàng náo qū, shān xiàng máo lí náo ruò yě* — "the top depicts bending; the 彡 (*shān* — fine strokes) depicts fine hair, bending and pliant"). 弱物并，故从二𢎥 (*ruò wù bìng, gù cóng èr* — "pliant things go together, so it is made of two of the bent element"). Xu Shen names no bow.
- **A bow, in popular etymologies,** because the modern form looks like two 弓 (*gōng* — bow). Wiktionary gives this with 彡, and reads it as "a decorative bow that is functionally inferior," which is itself a deficiency reading.
- **Two people urinating,** in Dong Chinese, which lists Ji Xusheng's *Shuowen xinzheng* among its sources. On this reading the word for weakness is a sound loan, and the graph says nothing about the meaning. We have this secondhand and have not read the book.

**So the case rests on how the word was understood and how the book uses it, not on a picture.** The Shuowen is good evidence for the first: a Han reader defined 弱 by how a thing behaves under load. The word's family points the same way. Wiktionary reports it as probably cognate with 柔 (*róu* — soft), citing STEDT, and reports Schuessler and Hill comparing Tibetan *nyog nyong*, "soft, weak."

### What the commentators actually say

Where the book holds 弱 up, the commentators read capacity, and none of them reads deficiency.

**Wang Bi on ch 40** (弱者道之用 — *"弱 is the Tao's use"*):

> 柔弱同通不可窮極
> *"Soft and 弱 alike **pass through**; they cannot be exhausted."*

通 (*tōng*) is to penetrate, to get through, to be unobstructed. This is a gloss about **capacity**, and about inexhaustibility — the opposite of the thing "weak" names.

**Heshang Gong on ch 78**, where the text splits the compound into two clauses and he answers each with an image:

> 弱之勝強 — 水能滅火
> *"The 弱 overcomes the strong — water can put out fire."*
> 柔之勝剛 — 舌柔齒剛，齒先舌亡
> *"The soft overcomes the hard — the tongue is soft, the teeth are hard; the teeth go first."*

**Heshang Gong on ch 36** (柔弱勝剛強):

> 柔弱者久長，剛強者先亡也
> *"The 柔弱 endure long; the hard and strong perish first."*

Water and a tongue are not weak. They are things that do not resist, and therefore do not break, and therefore are still there afterwards. **Yielding** is the English for that.

**Heshang Gong on ch 3,** on 弱其志 (*ruò qí zhì* — "teach their ambition to yield") itself:

> 和柔謙讓不處權也
> *"Gentle and soft, modest and giving way, not holding on to power."*

讓 (*ràng*) means to give way. This is the only gloss any commentator gives on the causative in ch 3, and it reads yielding, not diminishing.

**The same words are for rulers.** At ch 42 he says rulers and lords 處謙卑，法虛空，和柔 (*chǔ qiān bēi, fǎ xū kōng, hé róu* — "dwell modest and low, model on empty space, gentle and soft"). What ch 3 gives the people is what the sage practices, and 弱者道之用 (*ruò zhě dào zhī yòng* — "Yielding is the Tao's use") at ch 40 is where it comes from.

### Making something yield

Two of the ten lines make something yield, with an object:

- **ch 3** 弱其志，強其骨 — *teaching their ambition to yield and strengthening their bones*
- **ch 36** 將欲弱之，必固強之 — *If you would have it yield, you must first strengthen it*

**弱 here is a stative word made causative,** as its neighbours in ch 3 are: 虛 (*xū* — empty) gives *emptying*, 實 (*shí* — full) gives *filling*, 強 (*qiáng* — strong) gives *strengthening*. Each keeps its root, and so does 弱. English has no one-word causative of *yield*, so each line takes a helper verb. The helper belongs to the construction and not to the character, and it is where the two lines differ: the sage **teaches**, and the strategist **has**.

**Ch 3 reads *teaching their ambition to yield*.**

- ***Yielding their ambition*** fails: with an object, *yield* means produce or surrender, and *produce* reverses the line.
- ***Making*** is accurate, but *make them yield* is the language of force, which is the fault [`collocation-carries-a-verdict`](../process/principles/collocation-carries-a-verdict.md) names.
- ***Letting*** undersells a real causative.
- ***Teaching*** is the commentators' own account. Heshang Gong on ch 42: 言我教衆人使去強為弱，去剛為柔 (*yán wǒ jiào zhòng rén shǐ qù qiáng wéi ruò, qù gāng wéi róu* — "I teach the many, so that they leave the strong and become yielding, leave the unbending and become soft"). Wang Bi on the same line: 我之非强使人從之也，而用夫自然 (*wǒ zhī fēi qiáng shǐ rén cóng zhī yě, ér yòng fū zì rán* — "it is not that I force people to follow; I use what is so of itself"). The Shuowen defines 教 (*jiào* — teach) as 上所施下所效也 (*shàng suǒ shī xià suǒ xiào yě* — "what those above give out and those below take after"). And ch 2, just before this line, has named the teaching without words, which Heshang Gong glosses 以身師導之也 (*yǐ shēn shī dǎo zhī yě* — "teaching and guiding them with one's own body").

**The costs:** *teaching* is not in the line, and a commentator's gloss at another chapter is not the text. It says the paternalism outright, and the couplet loses its run of four single verbs.

**Ch 36 reads *have it yield*.**

- ***Make it yielding*** fails: yielding is the state the book prizes, so the strategist would be doing the object a favor. All four clauses diminish something, and every commentator reads an undoing. Heshang Gong's lemma has an explicit 使 (*shǐ* — cause), with the gloss 先強大之者欲使遇禍患 (*xiān qiáng dà zhī zhě yù shǐ yù huò huàn* — "making it strong and great first means wanting it to meet disaster").
- ***Make it yield*** puts a fist in the sage's vocabulary.
- ***Have*** keeps the causative without the force, and sits close to Wang Bi's 因物之性令其自戮 (*yīn wù zhī xìng lìng qí zì lù* — "following things' nature, causing them to bring themselves down"), where the thing undoes itself.

**The costs:** *have* undersells a real defeat, and it has an older sound, though the chapter's *If you would* frame already does.

Everywhere 弱 describes a state or a quality — ch 40, 55, 76, 78, and 36's own 柔弱勝剛強 — it reads **yielding**.

### The story Han Fei tells

Chapter 36 uses the character twice, and the English repeats it:

> *If you would have it yield, you must first strengthen it.*
> *The soft and yielding overcome the unbending and strong.*
>
> 將欲弱之，必固強之；
> 柔弱勝剛強。
>
> — chapter 36

Han Fei, the oldest commentary there is, does not gloss these lines. He tells a story, and both uses are in it:

> 越王入宦於吴，而觀之伐齊以弊吳。吳兵既勝齊人於艾陵，張之於江、濟，強之於黄池，故可制於五湖。
> *"The king of Yue went into service in Wu, and steered it into attacking Qi, to wear Wu out. Once Wu's army had beaten the men of Qi at Ailing, it stretched itself out along the Yangtze and the Ji and made itself strong at Huangchi, and so could be subdued at the Five Lakes."*

He quotes the line right there, 將欲弱之，必固強之, and closes the passage with the other:

> 處小弱而重自卑損，謂「弱勝強」也
> *"To dwell in the small and yielding and make much of humbling oneself: this is what 'the yielding overcomes the strong' means."*

**Yue yields, and Wu is had to yield.** Yue serves its enemy and lets it grow. Wu, swollen, is subdued. One character, two parties, one story. So the four second verbs of the chapter, *stretch out, strengthen, raise up, give to*, may themselves be ways of giving way, and line 6 may name what "you" were practicing all along while line 2 names what befalls the other side. That is our reading. The story fits it, and no commentator states it.

**What this leaves open.** A word the book gives the sage, and the Tao at ch 40, stands here in a calculating sentence. In Han Fei's hands it is plainly a tactic. Wang Bi hears something closer to how things go. The chapter calls it 微明 (*wēi míng* — the hidden clarity) and does not choose, so neither does the English.

### 柔 and 弱 are two words, and ch 78 proves it

The compound 柔弱 is so common that it is tempting to treat the halves as one idea. **Ch 78 refuses:**

> 弱之勝強，柔之勝剛
> *"The yielding overcomes the strong; the soft overcomes the unbending."*

Two clauses, two pairs, no overlap. That line is the constraint every rendering has to satisfy, and it needs four distinct English words:

| | |
|---|---|
| 柔 (*róu*) | **soft** |
| 剛 (*gāng*) | **unbending** |
| 弱 (*ruò*) | **yielding** |
| 強 (*qiáng*) | **strong** |

So 柔 keeps **soft** and does not get *yielding*, which it had borrowed in ch 52 (守柔曰強) before this entry existed.

**剛 (*gāng*) is *unbending*, not *hard*,** because 堅 (*jiān*) holds *hard* a few lines away in the same chapter. The case is made in the 柔 entry, `rou-柔.md`, with the rest of 柔's.

### Why this matters for ch 76

Chapter 76 works by spending the **admiring** word on a corpse: 堅強 (*jiān qiáng* — hard and strong) is rigor mortis, and four lines later it is an army. That ambush is on 強 alone. An earlier draft of this entry's reasoning claimed the chapter *also* provokes by calling the living state weak — it does not, and translating to protect that invented second provocation is what put **weak** into the verse. Caught by Shalom, 2026-08-26.

### Working register

- **yielding** — ch 36 (柔弱勝剛強), 40, 55, 76, 78
- **yield**, with a helper verb — ch 3 (弱其志), *teaching their ambition to yield*; ch 36 (將欲弱之), *have it yield*

### What is set aside

**weak · weaken · weakness.** The dictionary's plain English, and it costs the book three things. It says *less force* where 弱 says *gives*, which brings back the doctrine that the way to prevail is to lack something. At ch 36 it splits one character into two unrelated English words in six lines, so a reader cannot hear the close answering the opening. At ch 3 it cuts the line off from *Yielding is the Tao's use*, and turns the sage's own practice into a ruler reducing the people. It also spares the sage's word the stratagem of ch 36, which is a comfort the Chinese does not offer.

**frail · feeble · powerless.** All three name a lack. 弱 names a response.

**For ch 3: soften · gentle · bend · surrender · invite · inspire.** *Soften* and *gentle* are 柔's ground, and *gentle* is forbidden there. *Bend* is 曲 (*qū* — bent)'s word (`quan-全.md`), and *bending their ambition* reads as redirecting it. *Surrender* brings in defeat. *Invite* and *inspire* make optional what the Chinese makes causative, and no commentator uses them.

Also never let 柔 take *yielding* — see the four-word table above. That ruling is held by the reader, not the build; `rou-柔.md` says why.
