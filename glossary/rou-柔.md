## 柔 (Róu) — Soft

*Say "roe," rising. Eleven lines across seven chapters.*

**Locked: 柔 → "soft."** *(Not "supple," "pliant," "flexible," "gentle" or "tender," which is 慈's. Not "yielding" either, which is 弱's, but that ruling no check can hold. See the last section but one.)*

*剛 (gāng — unbending), its opposite, stands only twice in the book and is covered here.*

The book never praises 柔 for being kind. It praises it for **winning**. The soft and yielding **overcome** the unbending and strong (36). The softest thing in the world **gallops through** the hardest (43). Holding to the soft is called **strong** (52). The soft and yielding are **followers of life** (76). Nothing is softer than water, and nothing does better against the hard (78). Every line is about what happens when soft meets hard, and in every line soft is still there at the end.

### The graph: wood that bends and comes back

> 柔，**木曲直也**。从木矛聲。
> *"柔 is wood bending and straightening. From 木 (mù — wood), with 矛 (máo — a spear) for the sound."* — 說文解字

段玉裁 (*Duàn Yùcái*) spells out what the four characters mean: 凡木曲者可直，直者可曲，曰柔, *"any wood that, when bent, can be made straight, and when straight, can be bent, is called 柔."* The spear on top gives only the sound, so the meaning is all in the 木 (*mù* — wood) beneath.

**The phrase is older than the dictionary.** The 尚書 (*Shàngshū*)'s 洪範 (*Hóng Fàn*), the oldest list of the five phases, gives each its nature. Water soaks down, fire flames up, and 木曰曲直, *"wood is called bending-and-straightening."* The commentary attributed to 孔安國 (*Kǒng Ānguó*) glosses it 木可以揉曲直, *"wood can be 揉 into bent or straight."* 揉 (*róu*) is the same word with a hand added, and it belongs to the woodworker. In the 周易 (*Zhōu Yì*)'s 繫辭 (*Xì Cí*), the ancient sages 揉木為耒, *"bent wood to make the plough handle."* In the 考工記 (*Kǎogōng Jì*), the wheelwright's 揉輻必齊, *"the spokes he bends must match,"* which 鄭玄 (*Zhèng Xuán*) explains as working the wood over fire.

So the picture behind 柔 is a carpenter's: a length of green or heated wood taking a curve without splitting. It passes the pointing test. Laozi could have pointed at a plough handle.

**The word is older than the graph.** The 柔-and-矛 form is not known from the oracle bones. The word is already in Western Zhou bronze, in the formula 柔遠能邇, *"to 柔 the far and make able the near"*, cast on the 大克鼎 (*Dà Kè dǐng*) and the 番生簋 (*Pān Shēng guǐ*) lid. The 詩經 (*Shījīng*) and the 尚書 repeat it, and the 毛 (*Máo*) commentary glosses it 柔，安也, *"柔 is to settle."* That is the word as a verb, *to make soft*: to settle a distant people the way a woodworker settles a curve into wood. Laozi never uses it that way. In this book 柔 is always a quality and never takes an object.

### What the book does with it

**Water.** 天下莫柔弱於水，而攻堅強者莫之能勝, *"In all the world nothing is softer and more yielding than water, yet at attacking the hard and the strong nothing overcomes it"* (78). On ch 43, 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) names the two ends of the scale: 至柔者水，至堅金石。水能貫堅入剛，无所不通, *"the softest is water, the hardest metal and stone. Water can pierce the hard and enter the unbending; there is nowhere it does not get through."*

**The tongue.** On ch 78's 柔之勝剛, *"the soft overcomes the unbending,"* 河上公 answers with the body: 舌柔齒剛，齒先舌亡, *"the tongue is soft, the teeth are unbending; the teeth go first."*

**The infant.** 骨弱筋柔而握固, *"their bones are yielding and their muscles soft, yet their grip is firm"* (55). 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) says the softness is the reason for the grip: 以柔弱之故，故握能周固, *"because it is soft and yielding, its grip can close all the way round and hold."* A hard hand cannot wrap around a thing. Ch 10 asks for the same body from inside: 專氣致柔，能如嬰兒乎, *"gathering the breath to softness, can you be like an infant?"* 王弼 there: 致至柔之和, *"reaching the harmony of the utmost softness."*

**What is alive.** 人之生也柔弱，其死也堅強。萬物草木之生也柔脆，其死也枯槁, *"In life a person is soft and yielding; in death, hard and strong. In life the countless things, the grasses and trees, are soft and fragile; in death, withered and dry"* (76). Rigor mortis and dry stalks are the argument. Softness is how you tell that something is alive.

**The paradox.** 守柔曰強, *"holding to the soft is called strong"* (52). 王弼 states it in two short clauses: 守強不強，守柔乃強也, *"holding to the strong is not strong; holding to the soft is truly strong."*

**Why it wins.** 王弼 on ch 43 gives the mechanism, and it is the 說文's wood again: 至柔不可折, *"the utmost soft cannot be snapped."* What bends does not break. Ch 76 states the other side, in our base text: 木強則折, *"a strong tree snaps."*

### 柔 and 剛: bending against not bending

剛 (*gāng*) appears twice, both times opposite 柔: 柔弱勝剛強 (36) and 柔之勝剛 (78).

> 剛，彊斷也。从刀岡聲。
> *"剛 is strong enough to cut. From 刀 (dāo — a knife), with 岡 (gāng — a ridge) for the sound."* — 說文解字

**剛 is *unbending*, not *hard*, and a third character is why.** 堅 (*jiān*) also wants *hard*: 至堅 (43), 堅強 (76, twice), 攻堅強 (78). Ch 78 holds both a few lines apart, *"attacking **the hard** and the strong"* for 堅 and *"the soft overcomes **the unbending**"* for 剛. 說文 defines each by the other's neighbour, 堅，剛也 (*"堅 is 剛"*). 堅 is solid hardness, and it keeps *hard*, in twice the lines. 剛 is the edge that will not give, and *unbending* is exactly what 說文's 柔 is not. **The book's contest is bending against not bending, and the pair holds the 說文's picture between them:** 柔 is the wood that bends, 剛 the blade that will not. *(Shalom's call, 2026-10-14.)*

**Ch 78 needs four English words, not two:** 弱之勝強，柔之勝剛, *"the yielding overcomes the strong; the soft overcomes the unbending."* 柔 **soft** · 剛 **unbending** · 弱 (*ruò*) **yielding** · 強 (*qiáng*) **strong**. Where 柔 and 弱 stand together, 柔 comes first every time (36, 76 three times, 78), and the English keeps the order: *the soft and yielding*. The argument for keeping the two apart is in the 弱 entry.

**The I Ching holds the same pair the other way.** Its commentaries, the Wings, make 剛柔 (*gāng róu*) their central pair, with 剛 nearly a hundred times and 柔 nearly seventy, and treat the two as equal: each is right in its own place, and a 剛 line where 剛 belongs is correct. Laozi has 剛 twice, never writes 剛柔 as a compound, and both times takes the side of 柔. The I Ching's 文言 (*Wényán*) has the same superlative as ch 43, 坤至柔而動也剛, *"坤 (kūn — earth, the carrying hexagram) is soft to the utmost, yet in moving it is unbending."* The I Ching's usual English for the pair is *firm / yielding*, and this book can use neither word: *firm* is 固's (55, 59, 67) and *yielding* is 弱's.

### Why "soft"

- **It is the book's own images.** Water, a tongue, an infant's grip, new grass are all soft. None of them is *supple*.
- **It is English's own opposite to hard.** Ch 43's 至柔 against 至堅 is *the softest thing* against *the hardest*, and no other English pair is that plain.
- **It describes and does not praise.** *Soft* is how a thing meets pressure, whether that is a virtue or not. The book supplies the verdict itself (勝, 強, 生之徒), so the word must not supply one first.
- **The bending is carried by the pair.** The 說文's wood that bends and straightens is held in the seam between *soft* and *unbending*, which is where the book puts it.

### What is set aside, and why

**"supple," "pliant," "pliable," "flexible"** are the 說文's picture taken literally, and the strongest rivals. They name a trained or worked capacity, a gymnast's spine or a cane under the woodworker's hand, and they fail on the book's central image: water is not supple. They also cost ch 43 its plain opposites, since *the most supple thing gallops through the hardest* sets two unrelated scales against each other.

**"gentle"** and **"gentleness"** turn a material quality into a manner of conduct, and a manner that English praises. 河上公 reads 柔 that way more than once: 和柔謙讓, *"harmonious, soft, humble, deferring"* (on ch 3), and 柔順, *"soft and compliant"* (on ch 10). That is a commentator's ethics, which is evidence for a reading and not a rendering. The verse lines are about water and bones.

**"weak"** names a lack. It was removed from 弱 for that reason, and it would bring the same deficiency back in through 柔.

**"yielding"** is 弱's. Ch 78 sets the two in separate clauses.

**"tender"** is 慈's (*cí*, *tenderness*, the first of ch 67's three treasures). 慈 shares no chapter with 柔, so the build can hold this one.

### Working register

柔 never takes an object in this book. It describes a state, or names the thing in that state.

### What no rule can enforce

**"yielding" is ruled out for 柔 and is not on its `forbidden:` list, because it cannot be.** 弱 stands beside 柔 in chapters 36, 55, 76 and 78 and prints *yielding* in every one of them.

**"hard" is ruled out for 剛 and cannot be forbidden either.** A `covers:` character carries a rendering but no `forbidden:` list, and 堅, whose English *hard* is, stands in ch 43, 76 and 78. The reader holds this one: in ch 78, *hard* is 堅's and *unbending* is 剛's. See `process/principles/ruling-and-enforcement-are-two-facts.md`.

### Left open

**English *soft* has idioms that point the wrong way.** *Gone soft*, *soft-headed*, *soft on crime*: as a judgement of a person, English *soft* means lacking, which is exactly why 弱 lost *weak*. *Soft* survives here because its first sense is material, to the touch, and the book's images keep it there: water, tongue, sinew, grass. But ch 78 turns from water to whoever takes on the shame of the state, and a reader who hears *soft* as a judgement of character will hear the deficiency there. No English word for 柔 avoids this. *Supple* and *gentle* avoid it only by losing the image.

**The return.** 說文's wood bends **and straightens**. Its 柔 is not only something that gives way under pressure but something that comes back afterwards, and English *soft* does not say that. Bending and coming back is the motion of 反 (*fǎn*) and 復 (*fù*), *return*, which ch 40 calls the movement of the Tao. Whether the book hears that in 柔 is a reason to look and not a reading (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`).

**The verb.** The oldest use of the word is political: 柔遠, *to settle the far*, to bring distant peoples round the way wood is brought round. This book never uses 柔 as a verb, but ch 78 does turn from water to the ruler of the state in the same breath. The verse keeps 柔 a quality, and the political sense stays in the background.
