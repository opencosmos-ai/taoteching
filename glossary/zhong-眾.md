## 眾 (Zhòng) — The Crowd

*Say "jong," falling. Nine appearances across six chapters — and until this entry it was rendered four different ways, including once as nothing at all.*

**Locked: 眾 → "the crowd" (of people) / "the many" (of things).** *(Not "the masses," not "the multitude," not "the people.")*

The word had drifted into four Englishes: *all* in Chapter 1, **dropped entirely** in Chapter 8, *the masses* six times in Chapter 20, *the many* in Chapter 21. The drop is the worst of them — Chapter 8's 處眾人之所惡 (*it dwells where the crowd will not go*) had become simply *"it seeks the lowest places,"* which keeps the water and loses the people the line is measuring the water against.

### The graph: worked and watched

The *Shuowen Jiezi* (~100 CE) analyses 眾 as **多也。从乑目** — *many; from 乑 and 目* — where 乑 is a stack of three 人 (*rén* — person) and 目 is an eye.

But the oracle-bone form is more specific and more uncomfortable. There the element above the people is **日** (*rì* — the sun), and the figures beneath it are **labourers**: a gang of people working in the open, under the sun. The eye of the later graph appears to be the sun's replacement, and it does not soften the picture — it adds an **overseer**. Either way, 眾 in its earliest use names not simply *a lot of people* but **the many who are worked, and watched while working.**

Three people is enough. Classical Chinese uses three for *innumerable* — the same convention that makes 森 (three trees) a forest.

This matters for the register. **"The masses"** imports a nineteenth-century class politics Laozi has no word for, and hands the reader Marx on the way to a text about water. But the oracle bone is a reminder that the neutral-sounding alternatives can under-translate too: 眾 is not an abstract plurality. It is a crowd of specific bodies with someone standing over them.

### The evidence — the crowd is what the sage is not

**Chapter 20 is the word's home**, and it uses 眾人 six times as one half of a contrast, with 我 (*wǒ* — the self seen) as the other:

> 眾人熙熙 · 眾人皆有餘 · 眾人皆有以
> *the crowd is merry · the crowd all has surplus · the crowd all has purpose*

Against which: 我獨若遺 (*I alone seem to have been left out*), 我獨昏昏 (*I alone am dim*), 我獨頑似鄙 (*I alone am stubborn and seem crude*). The crowd is **bright, full, and busy**; the speaker is dim, empty, and useless. The chapter needs the crowd to be genuinely enviable for its reversal to cost anything, which is one more reason to avoid a word that pre-judges them.

**Chapter 8 measures water by them.** 處眾人之所惡 — *it dwells in what the crowd finds distasteful.* 惡 (*è*) is *to dislike, to find repugnant.* Water's virtue is located precisely where the crowd will not stand.

**Chapter 64 sends the sage back to them.** 學不學，復眾人之所過 — *learning to be unlearned, returning to what the crowd has passed by.* Not above the crowd; back over the ground they walked past.

**Chapter 31 counts the dead.** 殺人之眾，以悲哀泣之 — *when the killed are many, weep for them in grief.* Here 眾 is predicative — *many* — and the line is one of the most humane in the book.

**And twice 眾 modifies things, not people.** Chapter 1's 眾妙之門 (*the gate of all subtleties*) and Chapter 21's 眾甫 (*the father of the many*). In both, "the crowd" would be absurd and **the many** or **all** is right.

### What is set aside, and why

**"The masses"** carries a modern political charge — a bloc, a demographic, an object of policy — and a faint condescension the Chinese does not have. Chapter 20's crowd is *merry at a spring festival*, not a proletariat.

**"The multitude"** fails the hymn test outright. *The multitudes* is King James English, and it arrives with loaves and fishes attached.

**"The people"** belongs to a different and far more common character: **民** (*mín*), which appears in thirteen chapters and means the **governed populace** — the ones a ruler acts upon (愛民治國, 民之難治, 使民不爭). 眾 is a plurality; 民 is a political relation. Chapter 17's 百姓 (*bǎi xìng* — the hundred surnames) is a third thing again. Blurring them costs the political chapters their precision.

**"Everyone"** is a reasonable near-miss and is right in some lines — it is arguably the best English for Chapter 8 — but it loses the massed quality that Chapter 20 depends on. A crowd moves together; everyone does not.

### Working register

- of people, as a body → **the crowd** (眾人熙熙 → "the crowd is merry"; 處眾人之所惡 → "where the crowd will not go")
- of things, as a modifier → **all** or **the many** (眾妙之門 → "the gate of all subtleties"; 眾甫 → "the father of the many")
- as a bare predicate → **many** (殺人之眾 → "when the killed are many")

**Never** "the masses," "the multitude," and never the word that belongs to 民.

### Left open

**Is Laozi contemptuous of the crowd, or one of them?**

Chapter 20 can be read either way, and the reading changes the whole voice of the book. Take 我獨 (*I alone*) as pride and the chapter is an aristocrat's complaint: the crowd is shallow, I am deep. Take it as loneliness and the chapter is an admission: everyone else knows how to live and I do not. The Chinese supports both, and the same six-fold contrast carries them equally.

Two things weigh toward the second. The self-descriptions are **not flattering** — 昏昏 (*dim*), 頑 (*stubborn*), 鄙 (*crude*), 若遺 (*as if left out*) — and Laozi elsewhere claims 愚 (*yú* — simple, foolish) for himself as a good. And Chapter 64 has the sage **return** to what the crowd walked past, which is a movement toward them rather than away.

But Chapter 20 also says 我獨異於人 — *I alone am different from people* — and difference is not humility. We keep "the crowd," which is neutral enough to let a reader hear either, and we do not decide for them.
