## 勝 (Shèng) — Overcome

*Say "shung," falling. Sixteen times in fourteen lines across eleven chapters. The word for one thing getting the better of another: the soft over the hard, the female over the male, stillness over heat.*

**Locked: 勝 → "overcome."** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-20.)* **One declared flexion: *win*, in the four battle lines, 31, 67, 69 and 76**, where 勝 names no object and the enemy is understood. *(Not "victory," which adds the triumph chapter 31 refuses to glorify. Not "conquer" or "defeat," which put the loser under a heel the book never names. Not "prevail," an abstraction with nothing to point at.)*

### The graph: strength equal to its load

**勝 is 力 (*lì* — strength) with 朕 (*zhèn*) for its sound.** The Shuowen draws 力 as a body, not a force: 筋也。象人筋之形 — *"sinew; it pictures a person's sinews."* And it defines 勝 by what sinew does:

> 勝，任也。从力朕聲。
> *"勝 is to bear [a load]. From 力 (lì — strength), with 朕 (zhèn) for sound."*

**任 (*rèn* — to bear) is the word for carrying a load, and the old sense lives on in 勝任 (*shèng rèn* — "equal to a task").** So the root of 勝 is strength that is equal to its load, and more than equal: getting the better of something by bearing it, not by striking it. That is how the book's own overcoming works. Heshang Gong on chapter 78's water: 水能懷山襄陵，磨鐵消銅，莫能勝水而成功也 — *"water can surround mountains and rise over hills, grind iron and dissolve copper; nothing can overcome water and succeed."* Water wins by carrying, wearing and outlasting. The dictionary's sense is the book's argument in miniature.

**Where the picture stops.** The Shuowen's 任也 is a definition, not a drawing, and none of the book's sixteen uses means *to bear* on its face. The old sense explains why *overcome* fits; it does not license *bear* in the verse. Silk B writes the sound part alone, 朕, for 勝 at chapter 36 (柔弱朕強 (*róu ruò zhèn qiáng*), recorded unconfirmed in `WORKLIST.md` T2-80). That is a short spelling by sound, and it says nothing about what 朕 does inside 勝 (`process/principles/part-role-is-not-the-reading.md`).

### Ten lines: one quality gets the better of its opposite

> *One who overcomes others has force.*
> *One who overcomes themselves is strong.*
>
> 勝人者有力，
> 自勝者強。
>
> — chapter 33

> *The soft and yielding overcome the unbending and strong.*
>
> 柔弱勝剛強。
>
> — chapter 36

> *Restlessness overcomes cold; stillness overcomes heat.*
>
> 躁勝寒，靜勝熱。
>
> — chapter 45

> *The female always overcomes the male by stillness.*
> *By stillness she is underneath.*
>
> 牝常以靜勝牡，
> 以靜為下。
>
> — chapter 61

> *Those masterful at overcoming enemies do not engage them.*
>
> 善勝敵者，不與；
>
> — chapter 68

> *The way of nature:*
> *it does not contend, yet is masterful at overcoming.*
>
> 天之道，
> 不爭而善勝，
>
> — chapter 73

> *yet at attacking the hard and the strong nothing overcomes it,*
> *for nothing can take its place.*
> *The yielding overcomes the strong.*
> *The soft overcomes the unbending.*
>
> 而攻堅強者莫之能勝，
> 以其無以易之。
> 弱之勝強，
> 柔之勝剛，
>
> — chapter 78

**In every one of these lines, the one that overcomes is the one the world would bet against**: the soft, the yielding, the still, the female, the one who turns on themselves, the one who will not engage. 勝 is the book's word for the reversal, and the English has to state the reversal without cheering for it. *Overcome* is the word for what a wave does, or sleep, or grief: one thing coming over another, with no prize and no crowd.

**Chapter 33 grades the overcoming.** Wang Bi: 勝人者有力而已矣，未若自勝者無物以損其力 — *"one who overcomes others merely has force; that is not like one who overcomes themselves, whose force nothing can diminish."* Heshang Gong: 能勝人者，不過以威力也 — *"one who can overcome others does it with nothing more than intimidation and force."* Overcoming others is the lesser rung of a ladder, and the book still uses the same word for both.

### Four lines: *win*, and the enemy is not named

> *Win, but do not glorify it.*
>
> 勝而不美，
>
> — chapter 31

> *A battle won is placed by the rites of mourning.*
>
> 戰勝以喪禮處之。
>
> — chapter 31

> *Tenderness. Fight with it and you win, guard with it and you stand firm.*
>
> 夫慈，以戰則勝，以守則固。
>
> — chapter 67

> *So when the armies are evenly matched, the side that grieves wins.*
>
> 故抗兵相若，哀者勝矣。
>
> — chapter 69

> *Therefore a strong army does not win,*
> *and a strong tree snaps.*
>
> 是以兵強則不勝，
> 木強則折。
>
> — chapter 76

**Each of these is a battle, and in each 勝 has no object.** The thing overcome is the enemy, and the line leaves it unsaid because there is nothing else it could be. Heshang Gong says it at chapter 67: 以戰則勝敵 (*yǐ zhàn zé shèng dí* — "fight with it and you overcome the enemy"). English says this with *win*, which takes no object and needs none; *overcome* with nothing after it is stiff, and it calls up a twentieth-century hymn, *We shall overcome*.

**The book admits that wars are won, and refuses to celebrate it.** That is chapter 31's whole argument: 勝而不美 (*shèng ér bù měi* — "win, but do not glorify it"), and the general who has won takes the mourner's seat. Heshang Gong: 古者戰勝，將軍居喪主禮之位，素服而哭之 — *"in ancient times, when a battle was won, the general took the place of the chief mourner, dressed in white, and wept."* *Win* is the honest word for what happened, and the line is a funeral.

**And in two of the four, the winning goes to the unlikely side**, as in the ten: tenderness wins (67), the side that grieves wins (69). Wang Bi on 67: 相慜而不避於難，故勝也 — *"they care for one another and do not flee from hardship, and so they win."* Chapter 76 is the reversal from the other side: the strong army does not win. Wang Bi: 強兵以暴於天下者，物之所惡也，故必不得勝 — *"a strong army used to bully the world is what all things hate, and so it cannot win."*

### 勝 is what the book allows, and 爭 is what it refuses

Chapter 73 sets them side by side: 不爭而善勝 (*bù zhēng ér shàn shèng* — "it does not contend, yet is masterful at overcoming"). **Overcoming is permitted. Pulling toward yourself is not** (`glossary/zheng-爭.md`, where this line decided 爭 (*zhēng* — contend) against *compete*). The line is also why 勝 cannot be *win* outside the battle lines: *does not contend, yet is masterful at winning* turns the line back into a contest it has just refused. Chapter 73's 勝 has no object either, and it stays *overcoming*, because the way of nature has no enemy.

### What the commentators did with it

**At chapter 45, Heshang Gong reads 勝 as a turning point, not an overcoming.** 勝極也 — *"勝 means to reach the extreme."* For him, restlessness at its extreme turns to cold, as summer's heat gives way to winter. Wang Bi reads the plain sense: 躁罷然後勝寒，靜無為以勝熱 — *"only when restlessness tires does it overcome cold; stillness, not acting, overcomes heat."* The verse follows Wang Bi, and `glossary/jing-靜.md` holds the chapter.

**At chapter 68, Heshang Gong's text reads 善勝戰者 (*shàn shèng zhàn zhě* — "one masterful at winning battles")** where ours reads 善勝敵者 (*"overcoming enemies"*), and his own gloss goes back to the enemy: 善以道勝敵者 (*"one masterful at overcoming the enemy by the Tao"*). Recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`; the base stands.

### 克 (kè — to shoulder)

**克 stands twice in the book, both in one chain at chapter 59, and it is covered here because its English is decided by what 勝's is not.**

> *a heavy accumulation of integrity is when there is nothing you cannot shoulder,*
> *when there is nothing you cannot shoulder, no one knows its limit,*
>
> 重積德則無不克；
> 無不克則莫知其極；
>
> — chapter 59

**The Shuowen makes it the twin of 勝:** 克，肩也。象屋下刻木之形 — *"克 is to shoulder. It pictures carving wood under a roof."* 勝 is 任也, to bear a load; 克 is 肩也, the shoulder that takes it. The line names the load itself, 重積德 (*zhòng jī dé* — "a heavy accumulation of integrity"), so *shoulder* gives the chain one picture: a heavy store, and a strength equal to anything put on it. The English is active, with the *you* the chapter already speaks to (*"you can have the state"*), because the passive with the Chinese double negative, *nothing cannot be shouldered*, hid the meaning (Shalom's call, 2026-11-20).

**Both commentators who gloss the line hear 勝 in it.** Heshang Gong: 剋，勝也 — *"克 means 勝."* Han Fei: 積德而後神靜……能御萬物則戰易勝敵，戰易勝敵而論必蓋世，論必蓋世，故曰「無不克」 — *"accumulate integrity, and then the spirit is still … able to steer the countless things, in war you easily overcome the enemy; easily overcoming the enemy, your arguments surpass the age; your arguments surpassing the age, so it says, 'there is nothing that cannot be 克.'"* **The verse keeps the capacity they describe and lets go of the conquest**, so that *overcome* stays one character's word. Their reading is the cost, named here and in `chapters/059.md`.

### What is set aside

**"Victory," "victorious."** A noun the Chinese does not have: 戰勝 (*zhàn shèng*) at chapter 31 is a verb, *won in battle*. *Victory* also carries the triumph the chapter sets out to refuse, the garland and the parade, and it fails the hymn test (*"O grave, where is thy victory?"*).

**"Conquer," "conquest."** Takes territory and holds it. None of the book's overcoming keeps anything: water wears the stone away and moves on.

**"Defeat."** Names the loser, and puts the weight of the line on them. In every line of this book the attention is on the one who overcomes, and usually on how unlikely they were.

**"Triumph."** A Roman parade. The same overlay as *victory*, with more brass.

**"Prevail," "prevail over."** The best single word for both grammars, with and without an object, and it was weighed as the one English for all fourteen lines. It is Latin, *to be stronger than*, with nothing to point at, and it would take *"one who overcomes themselves"* away from chapter 33, the most quoted line 勝 stands in.

**"Win" outside the battle lines.** At chapter 61, *the female always wins over the male* would read as charm or seduction, an English that could be quoted about women's place (`CLAUDE.md`, standing rule 1). At chapter 73 it would turn refusing to contend into a contest. At 33, *wins over themselves* is not English.

**"Beat," "best."** A game's words, which bring the contest back in.

### What no rule can enforce

***Win* is ruled out everywhere but the four battle lines, and cannot be forbidden.** A word that is both a declared flexion and forbidden is an error in the build. `concordance.py --english "win"` shows the flexion's scope, and the check is by hand.

***Overcome* is held for 勝 by argument at chapter 59.** 克 (*kè* — to shoulder) is covered here as *shoulder*, but a cover carries no frontmatter of its own, and chapter 59 has no 勝, so no rule would see *overcome* return there. `concordance.py --english "overcome"` is the check, run by hand: every hit should stand in a line with 勝.

***Win over* is 取 (*qǔ* — take)'s at chapters 48 and 57**, 取天下 (*qǔ tiān xià* — "win over the world"). Neither chapter holds 勝, so no rule sees that either.

### Left open

**取's *win over* at 48 and 57.** 取 is *take* everywhere else in the book, including chapter 61, beside 勝. Now that 勝 holds *win*, the phrase is crowded from both sides, and 取 is owed a look of its own.

**Heshang Gong's turning point at chapter 45.** *Restlessness at its extreme turns to cold* is a real reading, a Han reading in the language of the seasons, and the verse declines it. It is the one place a commentator reads 勝 as something other than one thing getting the better of another.
