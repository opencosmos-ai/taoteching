## 氣 (Qì) — Breath

*Say "chee," falling. Three appearances, one in each of three chapters. The book's word for what moves in a living body and between the halves of things: in its plainest sense, a breath, the kind you can see on a cold morning.*

**Recommended: 氣 → "breath," in all three lines.** *(Not "energy" or "vital energy," which bring physics and a twentieth-century register into a book about bodies. Not "vital breath," because* vital *is 精 (jīng — vital essence)'s word, and chapter 55 needs the two kept apart; that ruling no check can hold, see the last section but one. Not "life force," and not "qi" left in pinyin.)*

*精 (jīng — vital essence) is its counterpart, and has its own entry: chapter 55 sets the two against each other, a reserve against a current, and one entry for both would blur the distinction the chapter is built on (`glossary/jing-精.md`).*

### The graph: three spellings, three pictures, one word

The word was written three ways before the modern character settled, and each spelling draws something different.

**Cloud, in the Shuowen's reading.** The Han dictionary keeps two characters where the book prints one:

> 气，雲气也。象形。
> *"气 (qì): cloud vapor. A picture."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

> 氣，饋客芻米也。从米气聲。
> *"氣 (qì): fodder and grain presented to guests. From 米 (mǐ — rice), with 气 (qì — cloud vapor) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi*

Duan Yucai, its great Qing commentator, draws the line from the picture to the word: 气本雲气，引伸爲凡气之偁 (*qì běn yún qì, yǐn shēn wéi fán qì zhī chēng* — "气 is first cloud vapor, and is extended to name every kind of 气").

**But the cloud is a reading of the shape, not a use anyone recorded.** The oracle-bone graph is three horizontal strokes, the outer two later bent so it wouldn't be read as 三 (sān — three). In the oracle bones it is used almost only as a function word: an adverb, "in the end," and sometimes a preposition, "until." In bronze it writes 乞 (qǐ — to pray for) and 訖 (qì — to end). The vapor and the breath are not attested in either. They appear in the Warring States texts, the century of the oldest Laozi. The picture of layered cloud is Xu Shen's analysis, five centuries after the bronzes, and it may be right. The earliest record doesn't show it.

**Grain, in the standard spelling.** 氣 (qì) began as a different word, the gift of grain to a guest, built from 米 (mǐ — rice) with the vapor graph lending its sound. The Shuowen cites the commentary on the Spring and Autumn: 齊人來氣諸侯 (*qí rén lái xì zhū hóu* — "the people of Qi came and presented grain to the lords"). The vapor word took over the grain character, and the gift moved to 餼 (xì — a gift of grain), with 食 (shí — food) added. **So the rice in 氣 is not steam over a rice pot.** It belongs to the guest's provisions and came into the vapor word through a borrowed spelling.

**Fire, in the oldest Laozi.** The Guodian slips, copied around 300 BCE, write the word with 既 (jì — already) for its sound, in all of the roughly ten places it stands in that tomb. The graph in chapter 55's line is transcribed with 火 (huǒ — fire) as its meaning part. So the scribes who wrote the earliest copy of this book spelled 氣 (qì) with fire, the warm vapor that rises from heat. It's the one spelling of the three that draws something Laozi could have pointed at in these lines: the warm breath of a body.

**Where the pictures stop.** None of the three is the word's sense in the book. Cloud and fire are outside a body, and grain is a spelling. In the book, 氣 is what moves in a body, and between yin and yang.

### What the manuscripts print

Every witness that survives agrees on the character in all three lines. The words around it fork.

| Line | Guodian, c. 300 BCE | Mawangdui silk A, c. 200 BCE | Mawangdui silk B, c. 175 BCE | Wang Bi, received |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | not among the slips | lost to damage | 專氣至柔 (*zhuān qì zhì róu* — "gathering the breath, reaching softness") | 專氣致柔 (*zhuān qì zhì róu* — "gathering the breath to softness") |
| 42 | not among the slips | 中氣以為和 (*zhōng qì yǐ wéi hé* — "the breath in the middle makes harmony") | lost to damage | 沖氣以為和 (*chōng qì yǐ wéi hé* — "blending the breath into harmony") |
| 55 | 心使氣曰強 (*xīn shǐ qì yuē qiáng* — "when the heart drives the breath, that is called strong"), in Chu spellings | the same | the same | the same |

**Chapter 55's line is the best attested 氣 (qì) in the book.** It stands in all four witnesses, and the Guodian bamboo carries it a century before the silks. It is also the hardest of the three, so the evidence is strongest where the decision matters most.

**Silk B's 至 (zhì — to reach) for 致 (zhì — to bring to) in chapter 10** is one word in two spellings, 致 being the causative of 至. In silk B's spelling, the softness arrives rather than being brought about, and the English "gathering the breath to softness" reads either way.

**Silk A's 中 (zhōng — middle) in chapter 42 may be a word or a spelling.** 沖 (chōng) is 中 (zhōng) with water added, and scribes of the silks often wrote a sound part bare, so 中 may be a short spelling of 沖. Read as a word, it says something different: yin, yang, and between them "the breath in the middle," which would make the chapter's 二生三 (*èr shēng sān* — "the two births the three") count yin, yang and the breath. The fork belongs to 沖 (chōng), which has no entry yet (`WORKLIST.md` T2-41), and it bears on this one: read as 中, 氣 (qì) is no longer the object of a verb in chapter 42 but the thing that makes the harmony.

### In Laozi's century, 氣 ran from a held breath to the sky

The book's own three lines are a small sample, so the evidence widens to the texts of its time.

- **A held breath.** The Analects describes Confucius entering the hall: 屏氣似不息者 (*bǐng qì sì bù xī zhě* — "holding in his breath as if he were not breathing"). Here 氣 is breath, literally, the thing a person can hold.
- **What fills the body.** Mencius: 夫志，氣之帥也；氣，體之充也 (*fú zhì, qì zhī shuài yě; qì, tǐ zhī chōng yě* — "the will is the commander of the 氣; the 氣 is what fills the body"). And so, he says, 持其志，無暴其氣 (*chí qí zhì, wú bào qí qì* — "hold to your will, and do no violence to the 氣").
- **The empty thing the heart should give way to.** Zhuangzi's fasting of the heart: 無聽之以心而聽之以氣……氣也者，虛而待物者也 (*wú tīng zhī yǐ xīn ér tīng zhī yǐ qì … qì yě zhě, xū ér dài wù zhě yě* — "don't listen with the heart, listen with the 氣 … the 氣 is empty, and waits on things").
- **The weather of the world.** The Zuo commentary: 天有六氣 (*tiān yǒu liù qì* — "the sky has six 氣"), named as yin, yang, wind, rain, dark and light.
- **The sky itself, in Laozi's own tomb.** The Taiyi Sheng Shui ("The Great One Gives Birth to Water"), a text found with the Guodian Laozi and written on slips of the same form as its third bundle: 上，氣也，而謂之天 (*shàng, qì yě, ér wèi zhī tiān* — "above, it is 氣, and it is called sky"). In the same tomb, the Xing Zi Ming Chu ("Nature Comes from the Mandate") says 喜怒哀悲之氣，性也 (*xǐ nù āi bēi zhī qì, xìng yě* — "the 氣 of joy, anger, grief and sorrow is one's nature").

**Breath is the word's plain core, and the word was already wider than breath when the book was written.** The cosmic 氣 (qì) is not a later growth. It lay in the same grave as the oldest copy of the book.

**Chapter 55 takes a side in a live argument.** Mencius puts the will over the 氣 and approves the command, warning only against violence. Laozi says the heart driving the 氣 is 強 (qiáng — strong), the hardness chapter 76 gives the dead. Zhuangzi stands with Laozi: the heart is to give way to the 氣, which is empty and waits. In all three, heart and 氣 are a pair, one that wills and one that moves. The English has to name something a heart can drive, and that, left alone, is empty and moves of itself. Breath does both. Everyone has held a breath under effort, and everyone has felt it go easy when the effort stopped.

### Chapters 10 and 55 are one instruction, given twice

> *Gathering the breath to softness, can you be like an infant?*
>
> 專氣致柔，能如嬰兒乎？
>
> — chapter 10

> *It cries all day and never goes hoarse — this is harmony at its utmost.*
> *Knowing harmony is called the ever-present.*
> *Knowing the ever-present is called clear-seeing.*
> *To force life to increase is an ill omen.*
> *When the heart drives the vital energy, that is called strong.*
>
> 終日號而不嗄，和之至也。
> 知和曰常，
> 知常曰明。
> 益生曰祥。
> 心使氣曰強。
>
> — chapter 55

Chapter 10 asks a person to gather the breath until it comes to 柔 (róu — soft). Chapter 55 says that when the heart drives the breath, that is 強 (qiáng — strong). In that chapter it is the strength of the stiffening thing: "When things reach their prime, they decay," runs the next line, and chapter 76 calls the dead "hard and strong" (`glossary/qiang-強.md`). **The same breath, handled two ways, gives the two outcomes the book cares most about.** Gathered, it ends soft, like an infant. Driven, it ends strong, like the corpse.

Both commentators read chapter 55 this way:

> 心宜無有，使氣則強。
> *"The heart should hold nothing. When it drives the breath, that is forcing."* — Wang Bi, on chapter 55

> 心當專一和柔，而氣實內，故形柔；而反使妄有所為，和氣去於中，故形體日以剛強也。
> *"The heart should be gathered into one, harmonious and soft, with the breath full within, and so the body is soft. If instead it drives the breath recklessly into doing, the harmonious breath leaves the center, and so the body grows hard and unbending by the day."* — Heshang Gong, on chapter 55

Heshang Gong's 專 (zhuān — to gather into one) is chapter 10's verb, so his reading of chapter 55 is chapter 10 in reverse. **And chapter 55 shows what the gathered breath does.** The infant "cries all day and never goes hoarse," and Heshang Gong gives the reason: 和氣多之所致 (*hé qì duō zhī suǒ zhì* — "it comes of abundant harmonious breath"). A voice that lasts all day is a matter of breath, and the line calls it "harmony at its utmost."

In the received text, 氣 is the object of a verb all three times: gathered, 專 (zhuān — to gather, wind onto a core); blended, 沖 (chōng — empty, or blending, its English still open); driven, 使 (shǐ — to make, to drive). In chapters 10 and 55, every witness agrees: **the breath is what a body does something with.**

### Breath and vital essence, in one chapter, must stay two words

Chapter 55 holds both characters, three lines apart. The infant's 精 (jīng — vital essence) is "at its utmost" because none of it has been spent: it is a reserve, kept or squandered, and nothing drives it. The 氣 is a current, the thing that moves and the thing a heart can push. **The chapter's argument lives in the difference.** The infant is full of the one and easy in the other. The forcing adult is driving the second and spending the first.

So the English needs two words that share nothing. *Vital essence* and *vital breath* share their adjective, and the adjective is what a reader carries away. *Vital essence* and *breath* do not.

### What the commentators did with it

**Heshang Gong fuses what the book keeps apart.** He writes 精氣 (*jīng qì* — "essence-breath") as one compound, again and again, where the book never sets the two characters side by side:

> 專守精氣使不亂，則形體能應之而柔順。
> *"Gather and guard the essence-breath so it is not disordered, and the body can answer it and become soft and supple."* — Heshang Gong, on chapter 10

That is his physiology, the practice of nourishing life. It is real evidence for how the Han read the book, and it is not the book's vocabulary. Laozi keeps 精 (jīng — vital essence) for the reserve and 氣 (qì) for the current, and the English follows Laozi.

**He also hears breath in lines that don't print 氣.** On chapter 10's 天門開闔 (*tiān mén kāi hé* — "the gate of the sky opens and closes"), he writes 治身，天門謂鼻孔 (*zhì shēn, tiān mén wèi bí kǒng* — "in tending the body, the gate of the sky means the nostrils"), and reads its opening and closing as breathing. It is a striking reading, and it stays a reading: the verse renders the sky's gate as the sky's gate (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`).

**Wang Bi ties the breath to 自然 (zì rán — of itself).** On chapter 10:

> 專，任也。致，極也。言任自然之氣，致至柔之和，能若嬰兒之無所欲乎。
> *"專 (zhuān) means to entrust oneself. 致 (zhì) means to bring to the utmost. It says: entrusting yourself to the breath that is so of itself, and reaching the harmony of utmost softness, can you be like an infant that wants nothing?"* — Wang Bi

His 專 (zhuān) runs against the graph's spindle whorl, and the verse follows the graph (`notes/translation.md`, chapter 10). His phrase 自然之氣 (*zì rán zhī qì* — "the breath that is so of itself") names what chapter 55's heart overrides, and what Zhuangzi's heart gives way to.

**On chapter 42, Heshang Gong puts the breath in the hollows of things:**

> 萬物中皆有元氣，得以和柔，若胸中有藏，骨中有髓，草木中有空虛，與氣通，故得久生也。
> *"Within each of the countless things there is the original breath, and by it they are harmonious and soft: as the chest has its organs, and the bones their marrow, and grasses and trees their hollows, open to the breath, and so they live long."* — Heshang Gong

Even the plants breathe, through the hollow inside the stem. His image is bodily, like the verse's backs and arms, and it reads 沖 (chōng) as a hollow the breath passes through, which bears on that character's open question.

### What is set aside

**"Energy" and "vital energy."** *Energy* is a nineteenth-century physicist's word, a quantity that is conserved and converted, and in twentieth-century English it became the house word of wellness and the 1970s paperback. It fails the pointing test: no one can point at energy, and anyone can point at breath. It belongs to the mechanistic overlay this edition strips out, the same family as *source code* and *operating system*. And it empties chapter 55's verdict of anything a body feels. *"When the heart drives the energy"* describes nothing in particular. *"When the heart drives the breath"* describes straining, shouting, a held breath under effort, what a body does when a will takes it over.

**"Vital breath."** The adjective is spoken for. *Vital* is 精 (jīng — vital essence)'s English in chapters 21 and 55, and in chapter 55 the two characters argue against each other (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`). *Vital* also adds a word the Chinese doesn't print, and tilts the line toward the gravity of doctrine.

**"Life force."** The popular English for 氣, with Victorian vitalism and the cinema in it. A force is pushed from outside, where breath moves of itself. It sits in the 1971 paperback the imported-register test warns of (`process/principles/imported-register.md`). `glossary/po-魄.md` sets it aside for 魄 (pò — aliveness) for the same reasons.

**"Qi," left in pinyin.** It turns the commonest thing a body does into a foreign technical term, carrying a century of martial arts and acupuncture into the verse. The edition translates, and here the plain English word exists.

**"Spirit."** Taken by 神 (shén — numinous potency) in the chapters that print it, and set aside for 精 (jīng — vital essence) already. Heshang Gong's own formula 愛氣養神 (*ài qì yǎng shén* — "cherish the breath, nourish the spirit") keeps 氣 and 神 two (`glossary/shen-身.md`).

**"The breath of life."** Never, though *breath* stays. The phrase is Genesis, God breathing into Adam's nostrils, and it fails the hymn test outright. Breath in this book is not given from outside. It moves of itself, and a heart can only interfere with it.

**"Vapor" or "steam."** The pictures in the graph, not the word's sense in these lines. Inside a body, the vapor is breath, and English has the word.

**"Air."** Modern Chinese writes the atmosphere as 空氣 (kōng qì — "empty breath"). That sense is later, and air is not what a heart drives.

### Working register

氣 doesn't flex. It takes one English in three lines, with the article English grammar asks for:

| Line | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 專氣致柔 (10) | *gathering the breath to softness* | the object of 專 (zhuān — to gather) |
| 沖氣以為和 (42) | *blending the breath into harmony* | the object of 沖 (chōng — empty, or blending), whose own English is open |
| 心使氣曰強 (55) | *when the heart drives the breath* | the object of 使 (shǐ — to drive) |

**The verb *breathe* stands once in the verse, for another character.** Chapter 49's 歙歙 (xī xī — drawing in the breath) reads *"the sage breathes gently."* 歙 (xī) is a verb of the nose and the lungs, and *breathes* is its word there.

### What no rule can enforce

**"Vital" is ruled out for 氣 and cannot go on the `forbidden:` list.** 精 (jīng — vital essence) stands in chapter 55 beside 氣, and prints *vital* there legitimately, so a forbidden *vital* would fire on a correct line. The checker gates on whether a character is present in the chapter, and cannot say *right for 精, wrong for 氣, same chapter*. That distinction is held by this entry and by the reader (`process/principles/ruling-and-enforcement-are-two-facts.md`).

**"Breath" without 氣 is not a breach in chapter 49,** where it renders 歙歙 (xī xī — drawing in the breath) as a verb.

### Left open

- **Breath is the narrowest of the word's senses in Laozi's time.** The book's contemporaries used 氣 for a held breath, for what fills the body, for the weather and for the sky, and the cosmic sense lay in the same grave as the oldest copy of the book. Chapters 10 and 55 sit at the body's end, where breath fits. Chapter 42 is the cosmic line, and asks the most of the English. The bet is that the plain core, carried faithfully, lets the reader feel the wider word through it, where *energy* would replace the word with an idea.
- **Silk A's 中 (zhōng — middle) for 沖 (chōng) in chapter 42** is undecided (`sources/variants.yaml`, `WORKLIST.md` T2-41). Read as a short spelling, nothing changes here. Read as a word, the line becomes "the breath in the middle makes harmony," and 氣 becomes the third of the chapter's three.
- **The Guodian graph should be checked against the plates.** The fire in the oldest spelling rests on the published transcription of chapter 55's slip and on a study of the tomb's roughly ten occurrences, all with 既 (jì — already) for sound. The glyph itself is the evidence, and the photographs in the 1998 edition are where to see it.
- **The Peking University Han slips are not yet consulted** for any of the three lines.
- **The vapor reading of the oracle-bone graph is Xu Shen's,** and the oracle-bone and bronze uses don't confirm it. The entry says *cloud* only as his reading.
