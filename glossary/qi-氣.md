## 氣 (Qì) — Breath

*Say "chee," falling. Three appearances, one in each of three chapters. The book's word for what moves in a living body, and what the countless things share: something you can see on a cold morning.*

**Recommended: 氣 → "breath," in all three lines.** *(Not "energy" or "vital energy," which bring physics and a twentieth-century register into a book about bodies. Not "vital breath," because* vital *is 精 (jīng — vital essence)'s word, and chapter 55 needs the two kept apart; that ruling no check can hold, see the last section but one. Not "life force," and not "qi" left in pinyin.)*

*精 (jīng — vital essence) is its counterpart, and has its own entry: chapter 55 sets the two against each other, a reserve against a current, and one entry for both would blur the distinction the chapter is built on (`glossary/jing-精.md`).*

### The graph: cloud vapor, written with a gift of grain

The Shuowen keeps two characters where the book prints one.

> 气，雲气也。象形。
> *"气 (qì): cloud vapor. A picture."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

> 氣，饋客芻米也。从米气聲。
> *"氣 (qì): fodder and grain presented to guests. From 米 (mǐ — rice), with 气 (qì — cloud vapor) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi*

The Shuowen supports its grain sense with a line from the commentary on the Spring and Autumn: 齊人來氣諸侯 (*qí rén lái xì zhū hóu* — "the people of Qi came and presented grain to the lords").

**The vapor is the word, and the grain is a spelling.** 气 (qì — cloud vapor) is the picture: in the oracle bones, three horizontal strokes, the middle one shorter so it won't be read as 三 (sān — three). Xu Shen reads the strokes as layered cloud. 氣 (qì) was a different word, the gift of grain to a guest, built from 米 (mǐ — rice) with the vapor graph lending only its sound. Then the two traded places. The vapor word took over the grain character, and the gift of grain moved to a new one, 餼 (xì — a gift of grain), with 食 (shí — food) added.

**So the rice in 氣 is not steam over a rice pot.** The picture is popular, and it is lovely. The dictionary doesn't support it: the rice belongs to the guest's provisions, and it came into the vapor word through a borrowed spelling. What the character pictures is cloud, in the strokes of 气 (qì — cloud vapor) at its top.

**Where the picture stops.** Cloud vapor is a thing in the sky, and the book never uses 氣 for weather. In all three lines it is inside a body.

### Three lines, and in each a body breathes

> *Gathering the breath to softness, can you be like an infant?*
>
> 專氣致柔，能如嬰兒乎？
>
> — chapter 10

> *The countless things carry yin on their backs and embrace yang with their arms, blending the vital breath into harmony.*
>
> 萬物負陰而抱陽，沖氣以為和。
>
> — chapter 42

> *It cries all day and never goes hoarse — this is harmony at its utmost.*
> *Knowing harmony is called the ever-present.*
> *Knowing the ever-present is called clear-seeing.*
> *To force life to increase is an ill omen.*
> *When the heart drives the vital energy, that is called strong.*
>
> 終日號而不嗄，和之至也。
> 知和曰常，
> 知常曰明。
> 益生曰祥。
> 心使氣曰強。
>
> — chapter 55

**In every line 氣 is in a body, and in every line something is done to it.** Chapter 10's body is a person practicing, with an infant held up as the measure. Chapter 42's countless things have backs to carry with and arms to embrace with. Chapter 55's body is a newborn, crying all day. And 氣 is the object of a verb all three times: gathered, 專 (zhuān — to gather, wind onto a core); blended, 沖 (chōng — empty, or blending, its English still open); driven, 使 (shǐ — to make, to drive). **氣 never acts in this book.** It is what a body does something with.

Breath passes the pointing test in all three. Laozi could point at an infant's chest rising, at the breath of an ox in winter, at a person red-faced and straining.

### Chapters 10 and 55 are one instruction, given twice

Chapter 10 asks a person to gather the breath until it comes to 柔 (róu — soft). Chapter 55 says that when the heart drives the breath, that is 強 (qiáng — strong). In this chapter that is the strength of the stiffening thing, not of the soft: "When things reach their prime, they decay," the next line says. Chapter 76 says the same of the dead, who are "hard and strong" (`glossary/qiang-強.md`).

**The same breath, handled two ways, gives the two outcomes the book cares most about.** Gathered, it ends soft, like an infant. Driven, it ends strong, like the corpse. Both commentators read the chapters this way:

> 心宜無有，使氣則強。
> *"The heart should hold nothing. When it drives the breath, that is forcing."* — Wang Bi, on chapter 55

> 心當專一和柔，而氣實內，故形柔；而反使妄有所為，和氣去於中，故形體日以剛強也。
> *"The heart should be gathered into one, harmonious and soft, with the breath full within, and so the body is soft. If instead it drives the breath recklessly into doing, the harmonious breath leaves the center, and so the body grows hard and unbending by the day."* — Heshang Gong, on chapter 55

Heshang Gong's 專 (zhuān — to gather into one) is chapter 10's verb, so his reading of chapter 55 says chapter 10 in reverse. **And chapter 55 shows what the gathered breath does.** The infant "cries all day and never goes hoarse," and Heshang Gong gives the reason: 和氣多之所致 (*hé qì duō zhī suǒ zhì* — "it comes of abundant harmonious breath"). A voice that lasts all day is a matter of breath, and the line beside it is called "harmony at its utmost."

### Breath and vital essence, in one chapter, must stay two words

Chapter 55 holds both characters, three lines apart. The infant's 精 (jīng — vital essence) is "at its utmost" because none of it has been spent: it is a reserve, kept or squandered, and nothing drives it. The 氣 is a current, the thing that moves, and the thing a heart can push. **The chapter's argument lives in the difference.** The infant is full of the one and easy in the other. The forcing adult is driving the second and spending the first.

So the English needs two words that share nothing. *Vital essence* and *vital breath* share their adjective, and the adjective is what a reader carries away. *Vital essence* and *breath* do not.

### What the commentators did with it

**Heshang Gong fuses what the book keeps apart.** He writes 精氣 (*jīng qì* — "essence-breath") as one compound, again and again, where the book never sets the two characters side by side:

> 專守精氣使不亂，則形體能應之而柔順。
> *"Gather and guard the essence-breath so it is not disordered, and the body can answer it and become soft and supple."* — Heshang Gong, on chapter 10

That is his physiology, the practice of nourishing life, and it is real evidence for how the Han read the book. It is not the book's vocabulary. Laozi keeps 精 (jīng — vital essence) for the reserve and 氣 (qì) for the current, and the English follows Laozi.

**He also hears breath in lines that do not print 氣.** On chapter 10's 天門開闔 (*tiān mén kāi hé* — "the gate of the sky opens and closes"), he writes 治身，天門謂鼻孔 (*zhì shēn, tiān mén wèi bí kǒng* — "in tending the body, the gate of the sky means the nostrils"), and reads its opening and closing as breathing. It is a striking reading, and it stays a reading: the verse renders the sky's gate as the sky's gate (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`).

**Wang Bi ties the breath to 自然 (zì rán — of itself).** On chapter 10:

> 專，任也。致，極也。言任自然之氣，致至柔之和，能若嬰兒之無所欲乎。
> *"專 (zhuān) means to entrust oneself. 致 (zhì) means to bring to the utmost. It says: entrusting yourself to the breath that is so of itself, and reaching the harmony of utmost softness, can you be like an infant that wants nothing?"* — Wang Bi

His 專 (zhuān) runs against the graph's spindle whorl, and the verse follows the graph (`notes/translation.md`, chapter 10). His phrase 自然之氣 (*zì rán zhī qì* — "the breath that is so of itself") names what chapter 55's heart overrides: a breath that, left alone, already does what it does.

**On chapter 42, Heshang Gong puts the breath in the hollows of things:**

> 萬物中皆有元氣，得以和柔，若胸中有藏，骨中有髓，草木中有空虛，與氣通，故得久生也。
> *"Within each of the countless things there is the original breath, and by it they are harmonious and soft: as the chest has its organs, and the bones their marrow, and grasses and trees their hollows, open to the breath, and so they live long."* — Heshang Gong

Even the plants breathe, through the hollow inside the stem. The image is bodily, like the verse's backs and arms, and it bears on 沖 (chōng — empty, or blending), the verb before 氣 in that line (*Left open*).

### What is set aside

**"Energy" and "vital energy."** *Energy* is a nineteenth-century physicist's word, a quantity that is conserved and converted, and in twentieth-century English it became the house word of wellness and the 1970s paperback. It fails the pointing test: no one can point at energy, and anyone can point at breath. It belongs to the mechanistic overlay this edition strips out, the same family as *source code* and *operating system*. And it empties chapter 55's verdict of anything a body feels. *"When the heart drives the energy"* describes nothing in particular. *"When the heart drives the breath"* describes straining, shouting, a held breath under effort, the thing a body does when a will takes it over.

**"Vital breath."** The adjective is spoken for. *Vital* is 精 (jīng — vital essence)'s English in chapters 21 and 55, and in chapter 55 the two characters argue against each other (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`). *Vital* also adds a word the Chinese doesn't print, and it tilts the line toward the gravity of doctrine.

**"Life force."** The popular English for 氣, with Victorian vitalism and the cinema in it. A force is pushed from outside, where breath moves of itself. It fails the hymn test's cousin, the 1971 paperback test (`process/principles/imported-register.md`). `glossary/po-魄.md` sets it aside for 魄 (pò — aliveness) for the same reasons.

**"Qi," left in pinyin.** It turns the commonest thing a body does into a foreign technical term, carrying a century of martial arts and acupuncture into the verse. The edition translates, and here the plain English word exists.

**"Spirit."** Taken by 神 (shén — numinous potency) in the chapters that print it, and set aside for 精 (jīng — vital essence) already. Heshang Gong's own formula 愛氣養神 (*ài qì yǎng shén* — "cherish the breath, nourish the spirit") keeps 氣 and 神 two (`glossary/shen-身.md`).

**"Vapor" or "steam."** The graph's picture, not the word's sense in these lines. Inside a body, the vapor is breath, and English has the word.

**"Air."** Modern Chinese writes the atmosphere as 空氣 (kōng qì — "empty breath"). That sense is later, and air is not what a heart drives.

### Working register

氣 doesn't flex. It takes one English in three lines, with the article English grammar asks for:

| Line | English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 專氣致柔 (10) | *gathering the breath to softness* | the object of 專 (zhuān — to gather) |
| 沖氣以為和 (42) | *blending the breath into harmony* | the object of 沖 (chōng — empty, or blending), whose own English is open |
| 心使氣曰強 (55) | *when the heart drives the breath* | the object of 使 (shǐ — to drive) |

**The verb *breathe* stands once in the verse, for another character.** Chapter 49's 歙歙 (xī xī — drawing in the breath) reads *"the sage breathes gently."* 歙 (xī) is a verb of the nose and the lungs, and *breathes* is its word there.

### What no rule can enforce

**"Vital" is ruled out for 氣 and cannot go on the `forbidden:` list.** 精 (jīng — vital essence) stands in chapter 55 beside 氣, and prints *vital* there legitimately, so a forbidden *vital* would fire on a correct line. The checker gates on whether a character is present in the chapter, and cannot say *right for 精, wrong for 氣, same chapter*. That distinction is held by this entry and by the reader (`process/principles/ruling-and-enforcement-are-two-facts.md`).

**"Breath" without 氣 is not a breach in chapter 49,** where it renders 歙歙 (xī xī — drawing in the breath) as a verb.

### Left open

- **The oracle-bone reading wants checking against the paleographers.** The three-stroke graph is identified as 气 (qì — cloud vapor) in the standard references, and in the oracle bones it writes borrowed words, among them the one later spelled 乞 (qǐ — to ask for). The identification and its uses should be confirmed against a paleographic dictionary, such as Yu Xingwu's, before this entry is locked. The cloud is Xu Shen's reading of the shape.
- **沖 (chōng) before 氣 in chapter 42 is unsettled** (`WORKLIST.md` T2-41). Chapter 4 reads it *empty*; chapter 42 reads it *blending*; the Shuowen says 涌搖也 (*yǒng yáo yě* — "surging and shaking"). Heshang Gong's reading above, breath passing through the hollows of things, is evidence for the hollow. Whatever 沖 becomes, the line's breath stays breath, and the verb beside it may change.
- **The witnesses for all three lines are unrecorded** in `sources/variants.yaml`. Guodian carries chapter 55, the only one of the three in the bamboo, and the silks carry all three. Whether they print 氣 the same way, and what Mawangdui reads for chapter 42's 沖, is still to be checked.
- **Breath is smaller than 氣 became.** In later Chinese thought, 氣 is the stuff of everything: weather, temperament, the cosmos. Zhuangzi says 通天下一氣耳 (*tōng tiān xià yī qì ěr* — "all under the sky is one breath, and that is all"). Laozi's three uses are all inside bodies, so *breath* fits this book. The cost is that a reader who knows 氣 as the cosmos's substance may find it shrunk, and chapter 42, the one line about the countless things, has to carry that. The English bets on the book's usage over the tradition that grew from it.
