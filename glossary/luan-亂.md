## 亂 (Luàn) — Disorder

*Say "lwahn," falling. Four lines across four chapters, and the dictionary defines it as its own opposite.*

**Locked: 亂 → "disorder."** *(Never "chaos," never "chaotic.")*

### The graph: a tangle with hands in it

> 亂，**治**也。从乙，**乙，治之也**；从𤔔。
> *"亂 is to bring to order. From 乙 (yǐ), and **乙 is what orders it**; and from 𤔔 (luàn)."* — 說文解字

Read that twice, because it says the opposite of what the character is used to mean. **說文's headword gloss for 亂 is 治 — to govern, to put in order** — and it then singles out one component and names it *the thing that does the ordering*. The tool is inside the graph.

𤔔 is the rest of it: a hank of **silk** with a hand above and a hand below. 說文 draws both hands in our own corpus — 爪, **覆手曰爪**, *"a covering hand,"* reaching down; 又, **手也**, a hand, reaching up. The bronze forms show the threads on a frame, one hand holding, one working a shuttle through.

**And the hands are untangling, not tangling.** This is the fact that reorders the whole entry, and the dictionary states it twice. Besides 亂，治也, 說文 defines the 𤔔 element outright — in the entry for a character **this book uses**:

> 辭，訟也。从𤔔，**𤔔猶理辜也。𤔔，理也。**
> *"辭 (cí) is pleading a case. From 𤔔, because 𤔔 is like **putting right** (理) a wrong. **𤔔 means 理 — to put in order.**"*

辭 stands at chapter 34 — 萬物恃之以生而不**辭**, *"the countless things rely on it to live, yet it refuses none."* So the Laozi already contains one 𤔔-character, and the dictionary explains it as sorting out a dispute.

*A divergence, recorded rather than settled: a minority reading takes the bronze graph as silk on a loom that* cannot *be sorted — the hands present but defeated. Either way the hands are at work on the tangle, not making it.*

**So 亂 does not name a mess. It names a mess somebody has their hands in.** That is why chapter 64 can say 治之於未亂 at all: the tangle is addressable, and the counsel is to get to it early.

### Where it stands, and what it is set against

| ch | line | set against |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 不見可欲，使民心不**亂** | 聖人之**治** three lines later, and 無不**治** at the close |
| 18 | 國家昏**亂**，有忠臣 | 昏 (*hūn* — clouded) beside it; loyal ministers as the symptom |
| 38 | 忠信之薄，而**亂**之首 | 忠信 (*zhōng xìn* — loyalty and trust), worn thin |
| 64 | 為之於未有，**治**之於未**亂** | **治 itself, in the same clause** |

**Chapter 3 shows what it does to a person.** 亂 there is something done **to** the heart from outside, not a state it generates: 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) glosses the line 心無所亂也, *"the heart has nothing to be disordered by"* — remove the display and the disordering has no purchase. 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) pairs it with 惑 (*huò* — led astray): 不惑亂也. Both read it as agitation rather than as misunderstanding, which is why *confused* is wrong for it: that word puts the damage in the knowing, and this chapter has just spent two lines separating knowing from the heart. *(This was established during the ch 3 rebuild of 2026-08-31 and is recorded in `notes/translation.md` → Ch 3.)*

**Chapter 64 prints the pair the dictionary describes** — *"Order it before it falls into disorder."* Two of the four chapters set 亂 directly against 治, and the English keeps them visibly related, which *chaos* could not do.

**王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) reads 64 as a matter of scale, not of kind**: 治之於未亂 gets the gloss 謂㣲脆也, *"this refers to what is faint and brittle."* The disorder is not a different condition arriving from outside. It is the same thing, further along — a thread or two before it is a knot.

### The 反訓, and why the verse does not show it

A character that faces both ways is called a 反訓 (*fǎnxùn*), and 亂 is the standard example: the tangle and the untangling in one graph. It is the most interesting thing about the word. **It is also not what this book does with it.**

All four of Laozi's lines sit on the disorder side — a heart stirred by what it was shown (3), a state come apart (18), where the rot starts (38), the thing to catch early (64). **The 治 sense is the dictionary's, not the text's**, and an English that reached for it would be rendering a fact about the character over a fact about the sentence. The graph belongs in this entry; the verse says what the verse says. Same arrangement as 萬 (*wàn*), which is a scorpion here and *the countless things* on the page.

### What is set aside

**"chaos"** was the rendering at chapter 18, and it is the one to refuse.

It is a **cosmological** word in English — Hesiod's Χάος, the void before creation, *chaos and old night* — and it points at the wrong end of this book's cosmology. Where Laozi does describe an undifferentiated beginning he uses 混成 (*hùn chéng*, ch 25), rendered *"something formed in the undifferentiated."* 亂 is not that. It is **political and domestic**: a state coming apart, a household out of harmony, a heart stirred up.

And it is the wrong shape for the graph. **Chaos is a condition with nobody in it.** 亂 has two hands in it. You cannot catch chaos early; you can catch a tangle early, which is the whole of chapter 64.

**"unravelled"** is the near miss, and it is worth the paragraph.

It **performs the graph** — silk coming undone — and in English it faces both ways exactly as 亂 does, since one unravels a mystery and a plan unravels. It would carry the 反訓 natively, with no help from this entry. *"Order it before it unravels"* (64) and *"the people's hearts do not unravel"* (3) are both better lines than the locked ones.

It fails at **chapter 38**, and the failure is structural rather than a matter of taste. 亂之**首** is *the head of* 亂, and *"the head of the unravelling"* is not English. The obvious repair — *"where the unravelling begins"* — spends 首 (*shǒu*) on *begins*, and **首 and 始 (*shǐ*) were separated in that exact couplet during Pass D**, 而亂之首 against 而愚之始, because they had been sharing one English. Restoring that collision to gain a better line elsewhere is a bad trade. One chapter should not decide four, but a rendering that breaks in one of four is not a lock.

**"untangled"** is the opposite error, and naming it is the point. *Untangle* is the **治 half** of the 反訓 — the hands succeeding — and that is the half this book never uses. Put it in any of the four lines and the meaning inverts: chapter 18 would have nations becoming *untangled* as loyal ministers appear, which reverses the chapter's whole argument. It is the rendering the **graph** supports and the **text** forbids, which is a useful thing for a reader to see stated rather than left as a puzzle.

**"turmoil"** is better than *chaos* and still wrong: a word about agitation and feeling, so it carries the heart at ch 3 and drops the constitutional sense at 18 and 38. It has no relation to *order*.

### The working register

- as the state a thing falls into → **disorder** (亂之首 → "the head of disorder")
- as what a thing is kept out of → **thrown into disorder** (使民心不亂 → "the people's hearts are not thrown into disorder")
- beside 治 → keep the two English words visibly related (治之於未亂 → "order it before it falls into disorder")

**Never** "chaos," "chaotic," "untangled."

### Settled alongside

**治 (*zhì*) was locked on 2026-09-23** — *govern*, with a declared flexion to *order* at chapters 8 and 64 — so the pairing this entry rests on is now held at both ends. `zhi-治.md`. 說文 files 治 as **a river**, which is the other half of this entry's picture: 亂 is the tangle, 治 is what you do to a watercourse, and 王弼 says what that consists of at ch 60 — **不擾也**, *"it is not to disturb."*

**正 (*zhèng* — upright) stays out of it.** *Order* is also worn by 正 at chapter 45 — 清靜為天下正, *"clear stillness brings order to the world"* — and 正 is one of the calls Shalom has deferred (`WORKLIST.md` T4-2, 正/奇). It is named here so that a reader who notices the third claimant knows it was seen and left alone.
