## 神 & 靈 (Shén, Líng) — Spirit, and Spirit at Work

*Say "shun," rising, and "ling," rising. 神 stands eight times in seven lines across four chapters; 靈 twice, both in chapter 39, and both times as what 神 becomes. Neither is ever a god.*

**Recommended: 神 → "spirit" · Settled: 靈 → "potent"** (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19, at chapter 39). *(Not "God," "the divine" or "deity," which the missionaries put into 神. Not "holy," which is 聖 (shèng — the sage)'s overlay. Not "soul," which leaves the body. Not "numinous," a theologian's word for the holy that no one could point at.)* **One flexion:** *sacred* at chapter 29, where 神 qualifies a made thing. **Covered here:** 鬼 (guǐ — ghost) → *ghost*, its partner at chapter 60.

### The graph: lightning at an altar, and a shaman wearing jade

> 神，天神，引出萬物者也。从示，申聲。
> *"神 is the spirits of the sky, which draw out the countless things. From 示 (shì — the altar), with 申 (shēn — lightning) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

**申 is lightning.** Its oldest graph is a bolt branching from the sky, and it is the parent of two characters: 電 (*diàn* — lightning), which took the rain radical, and 神, which took the altar. In the oracle bones and in some bronzes the spirit is written with the lightning alone; 示 (*shì* — the altar) was added later, to say which sense was meant. The Shuowen's own line for 示 says what the altar is for: 天垂象，見吉凶，所以示人也, *"the sky hangs down signs, showing good fortune and ill, and so it shows people."*

**So the picture is a flash from the sky, received at an altar.** A spirit, in this graph, is not a person sitting somewhere. It is a force that shows itself, and a place where people meet it.

> 𩆜巫，以玉事神。从玉，霝聲。
> *"靈: a shaman, who serves the spirits with jade. From 玉 (yù — jade), with 霝 (líng — drops of rain) for the sound."* — Shuowen, under 𩆜, the old form of 靈

**The Shuowen files 靈 under jade, and the bronzes could not agree on what to put beneath it.** Over a constant 霝 (*líng* — drops of rain falling), the lower half is 玉 (*yù* — jade) in the Shuowen, 巫 (*wū* — a shaman) in the form we print, and 心 (*xīn* — the heart) or 示 (*shì* — the altar) in older bronzes. Four answers to one question: where does spirit arrive? In a precious stone, in a dancer, in a heart, at an altar.

*A calibration.* 霝 counts as sound in the Shuowen, and the rain in it may be only that. The popular reading of 靈 as a rain-shaman is attractive and unproved, and this entry does not lean on it. The silks point the same way: in the collated silk text, chapter 39 writes 神得一以霝 (*shén dé yī yǐ líng*), the bare sound part with nothing beneath it, which is how a scribe writes a word by its sound. What the graph does show is a person, or a thing, through which the spirits are served.

### The word before the book: falling rain, a good end, a spirit made bright

The Shuowen gives the graph. The word is older, and its early uses, gathered in the Kangxi Dictionary (康熙字典, *Kāngxī Zìdiǎn*, 1716), say what it meant before Laozi used it.

**霝, the top of the graph, is rain coming down in drops.** The Shuowen's own entry for it, as the Kangxi quotes it: 霝，雨零也。从雨，𠱠象零形, *"霝: rain falling. From 雨 (yǔ — rain); the three mouths picture the falling drops."* In the 靈 entry the Shuowen counts 霝 as sound; in its own entry it is a picture. The Book of Odes has 霝雨其濛 (*"the falling rain drizzles"*), and the Stone Drums have 霝雨.

**The oldest written 靈 here is a blessing on how a life ends.** The 逑盤 (*Qiú pán*), a Western Zhou bronze basin, closes its prayer 永令、霝冬, *"a lasting mandate, and a 霝 end,"* which its transcribers read as 靈終 (*líng zhōng* — a 靈 ending). Elsewhere the same wish is 令終 (*lìng zhōng*), *a good end*, and 令 (*lìng*) and 靈 trade places by sound.

**In the Odes, 靈 is good, and it is rain.** 靈雨既零 (*líng yǔ jì líng* — "the 靈 rain has fallen," 鄘風 (*Yōng Fēng*), *Dìng zhī fāng zhōng*), and Zheng Xuan's gloss: 靈，善也, *"靈 means good."* The rain the farmers prayed for, fallen when it should. The *Guangyun* (廣韻, *Guǎngyùn*) adds 福也, *"blessing,"* and 巫也, *"a shaman."*

**In the Documents and the Odes' commentary, 靈 is spirit at its brightest.** 惟人萬物之靈 (*wéi rén wàn wù zhī líng* — "humankind alone is the 靈 of the countless things," *Shàngshū*, *Tài Shì*), glossed 靈，神也, *"靈 is spirit."* On King Wen's 靈臺 (*líng tái* — the 靈 terrace), Mao's commentary: 神之精明者稱靈, *"what is most essential and bright in spirit is called 靈."*

**The Dadai Liji pairs it with 神 as two halves:** 陽之精氣曰神，陰之精氣曰靈, *"the essence-breath of yang is called 神; the essence-breath of yin is called 靈."* That is the pairing this entry is built on, already made in the Han.

**And 靈 belongs to the dead as much as the living.** The posthumous-name rules: 死見神能曰靈, *"one who shows spirit-power after death is called 靈."* The *Liji*'s 芻靈 (*chú líng*) are straw figures buried with the dead; the later 靈柩 (*líng jiù*) is the coffin and 靈位 (*líng wèi*) the spirit tablet.

**One thread runs through all of it: spirit where it takes effect and shows.** Rain that falls when it is prayed for. An end that comes out right. A shaman the spirit enters. A spirit bright enough to answer, before death or after it. 靈 is not life, and it is not the stuff of life. It is the moment the unseen acts on the seen.

### Seven lines, and 神 is never a god in any of them

> *The spirit of the valley is deathless.*
>
> 谷神不死，
>
> — chapter 6

> *The world is a sacred vessel. It cannot be handled. It cannot be grasped.*
>
> 天下神器，不可為也，不可執也。
>
> — chapter 29

> *Spirit received the one, and so became potent.*
>
> 神得一以靈；
>
> — chapter 39

> *Spirit with no way to be potent is likely to cease.*
>
> 神無以靈，將恐歇；
>
> — chapter 39

> *Oversee the world with the Tao, and the ghosts lose their potency,*
> *not only do ghosts lose their potency, their potency causes no harm to people,*
> *not only does their potency do no harm, the sage, too, does no harm to people.*
>
> 以道莅天下，其鬼不神；
> 非其鬼不神，其神不傷人；
> 非其神不傷人，聖人亦不傷人。
>
> — chapter 60

**In every line, 神 is something in the world, and nothing above it.** At chapter 6 it belongs to a valley. At chapter 39 it stands in a list with the sky, the earth, the valley, the countless things and the rulers, and like each of them it needs the one, or it stops. At chapter 60 it belongs to the ghosts, and the Tao reaches it the way it reaches the people. A god who needs something else in order not to cease is not God, and the book never asks 神 to be one.

### Chapter 39 is the hardest line: 靈 is what 神 is when the one is in it

Chapter 39 gives each thing the one, and each comes into its own quality:

| the thing | with the one | without it |
|---|---|---|
| 天 (*tiān* — sky) | 清 (*qīng*) — *clear* | 裂 (*liè*) — *split* |
| 地 (*dì* — earth) | 寧 (*níng*) — *settled* | 發 (*fā*) — *burst* |
| **神** (*shén* — spirit) | **靈** (*líng*) — ? | **歇** (*xiē*) — *cease* |
| 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) | 盈 (*yíng*) — *full* | 竭 (*jié*) — *run dry* |
| 萬物 (*wàn wù* — the countless things) | 生 (*shēng*) — *come to life* | 滅 (*miè*) — *die out* |
| 侯王 (*hóu wáng* — rulers) | 貞 (*zhēn*) — *the standard* | 蹶 (*jué*) — *topple* |

**Every other quality is one you could point at.** A clear sky, settled ground, a full valley, a sprouting field. Each failure is the visible end of its own quality: the clear sky splits, the full valley runs dry. So 靈 must be the quality that belongs to spirit as clearness belongs to sky, and its failure is 歇 (*xiē* — to stop, to rest, to breathe out and not breathe in). **A spirit that is 靈 works. A spirit that is not, stops.**

**The early texts say what working means.** The *Songs of Chu* (楚辭, *Chǔ Cí*), in Wang Yi's commentary, use 靈 for both ends of the rite:

> 靈，巫也。楚人名巫為靈子。
> *"靈 is a shaman. The people of Chu call a shaman a 靈 child."* — Wang Yi, on 靈連蜷兮既留 (*líng lián quán xī jì liú* — "the 靈 sways and lingers")

> 靈，謂雲神也。
> *"靈 means the spirit of the clouds."* — Wang Yi, later in the same song, on 靈皇皇兮既降 (*líng huáng huáng xī jì jiàng* — "the 靈, resplendent, has come down")

**The word names the dancer and the spirit who comes down into the dance.** A 靈 is spirit made present and effective: the rite answered, the rain fallen, the medicine working. The later language keeps exactly this sense in 靈驗 (*líng yàn* — efficacious, proved by its result) and 靈藥 (*líng yào* — a remedy that works).

**Heshang Gong reads the line as change:**

> 言神得一故能変化無形
> *"It says: spirit received the one, and so it can transform without form."*

> 言神當有王相囚死休廢，不可但欲靈無巳時，將恐虚歇不為神也
> *"It says: spirit must have its seasons of rising and falling. It cannot want only to be 靈, without end, or it may empty and cease and no longer be spirit."*

His seasons, 王相囚死休廢 (*wàng xiàng qiú sǐ xiū fèi* — thriving, ascending, confined, dead, resting, spent), are the vocabulary of the five-phase cycle, which this book never uses. Where the picture stops: the five phases are his. What he adds that holds is that 靈 is an activity, which can wear out. Wang Bi says only 各以其一致此清寧靈盈生貞, *"each by its one arrives at this: clear, settled, 靈, full, alive, the standard."*

**So the English wants a quality that shows in its effect, and whose failure is to stop.** *Potent* is that word. A potent remedy is one that works, and a spirit that is no longer potent goes quiet. It passes the pointing test the way 清 does: you point at the effect, as you point at the sky to show it is clear.

> *Spirit received the one, and so became potent.*
> *Spirit with no way to be potent is likely to cease.*

### Chapter 60: the spirit and the sage, and which two do no harm

Chapter 60 puts 神 into motion. It is a verb in 其鬼不神 (*qí guǐ bù shén* — "its ghosts do not 神") and a noun in 其神 (*qí shén* — "their 神"), twice. The commentators agree on the Tao and divide on the spirit.

**Heshang Gong makes it the ghosts' own force, held back:**

> 以道德居位治天下，則□不敢見其精神以犯人也
> *"Ruling the world from one's seat with the Tao and integrity, the ghosts do not dare show their vital spirit to trouble people."*

> 其□非无精神也
> *"It is not that the ghosts lack vital spirit."*

*(□ is the Song print's 鬼, written in a variant its transcribers could not encode. The rest of the second comment, 非不八正不能傷自然之人, is corrupt as printed, and this entry does not mend it.)*

**Wang Bi makes 神 a party of its own, set beside the sage:**

> 神不害自然也。物守自然，則神無所加；神無所加，則不知神之為神也
> *"Spirit does not harm what is so of itself. When things keep to what is so of themselves, spirit has nothing to add; and when spirit has nothing to add, no one knows spirit as spirit."*

> 神不傷人，聖人亦不傷人……故曰兩不相傷也。神聖合道，交歸之也
> *"Spirit does not harm people, and the sage does not harm people either… so it says the two do not harm each other. Spirit and sage join the Tao, and both return to it."*

For Wang Bi, *the two* of 兩不相傷 (*liǎng bù xiāng shāng* — "the two do not harm each other") are the spirit and the sage. For Heshang Gong they are the ghosts and the sage. Han Fei, the oldest reader, makes them the people and the ghosts: 治世之民，不與鬼神相害也, *"the people of a well-ruled age do no harm with ghosts and spirits,"* and he says plainly what a ghost's harm is, 鬼祟也疾人之謂鬼傷人, *"a ghost haunting a person and making them ill: this is called a ghost harming people."*

**One English for 神 across the chapter keeps all three readings open.** *Spirit* can be the ghosts' own force, which is Heshang Gong, or a power standing by the sage, which is Wang Bi, and the reader can find *the two* either way. *Potency* can only be the first: a quality the ghosts have, never a party who could stand beside the sage. Wang Bi's best sentence on the chapter, that the height of the Tao is 使不知神聖之為神聖, *"to bring it about that no one knows spirit and sage as spirit and sage,"* needs 神 to be someone. This is why the entry recommends *spirit* at chapter 60 too. The line rewrite it needs belongs with the chapter's larger repair (see *Left open*).

### Chapter 29: a vessel for the spirits

神器 (*shén qì*) is the one place 神 qualifies a made thing, and the thing is concrete. In the late Zhou a 神器 was a **ritual bronze**, above all the nine cauldrons whose possession was the warrant to rule (`glossary/qi-器.md`, and the reading note at chapter 29 in `notes/reading.md`). It was a vessel in which food was offered to the spirits, its inscription cast on the inside, facing them.

The commentators each read 神 here through the word's own sense. Wang Bi: 神，無形無方也。器，合成也。無形以合，故謂之神器也, *"spirit has no form and no fixed place; a vessel is something put together. The formless, joined in something put together: so it is called a spirit-vessel."* Heshang Gong: 人乃天下之神物也。神物好安靜，不可以有為治, *"people are the world's spirit-things, and spirit-things love calm and cannot be governed by doing."*

***Sacred* is what English calls a thing set apart for the spirits**, and it is the exact word for a bronze made to feed them. *Spirit vessel* reads in English as a bottle with a spirit in it, a genie's lamp, an urn. The flexion is grammar: a noun becomes the adjective English needs. The character has not changed.

### 鬼 (guǐ — ghost)

> 鬼，人所歸爲鬼。从人，象鬼頭。鬼陰气賊害，从厶。
> *"鬼: what a person returns to is a 鬼. From 人 (rén — a person), picturing a ghost's head. Ghosts are yin breath that does harm, hence 厶 (sī — the crooked)."* — Shuowen

In the oracle bones 鬼 is a figure with an outsized head, usually read as a mask: the dead, or someone standing in for them (`glossary/po-魄.md`). The Shuowen defines it by a pun, 鬼 (*guǐ*) as 歸 (*guī* — to return), which `glossary/gui-fu-fan-歸復反.md` cites as 歸's naming of an ending. **And the Shuowen says what ghosts do: 賊害, harm.** Chapter 60 is the case where they do not. A ghost is a person who has died, and in this book nothing worse.

鬼 stands twice in the book, both in chapter 60, and it lives in this entry because it never appears without 神: its whole part in the chapter is to be the thing whose spirit is held from harm. **Chapter 60 ends 故德交歸焉** (*gù dé jiāo guī yān* — "so their integrity converges and returns"), and the Shuowen would hear its 歸 in the 鬼 at the chapter's start. We do not claim Laozi meant the chime. The Shuowen's ear heard it.

### What is set aside

**"God," "a god," "the gods."** The Term Question was the missionaries' long argument over which Chinese word would be made to mean the Christian God, and 神 was one of the two candidates; the Chinese Union Bible was printed in a 神 edition and a 上帝 (*shàng dì* — the ancestor-lord above) edition because the parties never agreed (`process/overlay-audit.md`, `glossary/di-帝.md`). Read back into Laozi, *God* turns chapter 39 into nonsense, a God who would cease without the one, and turns chapter 60 into a theodicy. Forbidden lowercase, so it catches *gods* and *godlike* as well.

**"The divine," "divine."** An abstract noun of essence where the Chinese has a force at an altar. It is also the word the watchlist names for 神.

**"Deity."** It names a person with a cult and a name. 谷神 (*gǔ shén*) as *the valley deity* makes chapter 6 a shrine to a goddess; the line prints a valley and its spirit.

**"Holy."** 聖 (*shèng*)'s overlay, already forbidden there. In modern Chinese the Holy Spirit is 聖靈 (*shèng líng*), so the Bible spent both 聖 and 靈 on its third person (`glossary/sheng-聖.md`). That is a second reason 靈 cannot simply be *spirit*: the word had been made to say *Spirit* with a capital.

**"Soul."** The thing that leaves the body. Heshang Gong does put 神 in the body, 心藏神, *"the heart stores the spirit,"* one of his five organ-spirits at chapter 6, but even there it is housed and nourished, never a separable immortal self. Already forbidden for 精 (*jīng* — vital essence) and 魄 (*pò* — aliveness).

**"Numinous," for 靈.** Latin *numen*, a nod of the head, the assent of a god, and then a god's power. Rudolf Otto made *the numinous* the word for the holy as it is felt, in *Das Heilige* (1917), translated as *The Idea of the Holy* (1923). It fails both tests at once. It is a theologian's word for the holy, and no one could point at it. Forbidden since chapter 39 took *potent*.

**"Alive," "come alive," "living."** Three reasons, and the first is the etymology. **靈 is not life.** It belongs to the dead as readily as to the living: the posthumous 靈 who shows spirit-power after death, the straw 靈 in the grave, the spirit tablet. *Alive* would deny it to half the beings it names. **Second, chapter 39 needs *alive* for another word.** Its list gives six things six qualities, and Wang Bi names them as six, 清寧靈盈生貞; 靈 and 生 (*shēng* — to live) are two of them, two lines apart. *Spirit… became alive* above *the countless things… came to life* would make spirit and the countless things receive the same gift, and erase the difference the list exists to make. **Third, the word is spoken for twice over:** 生 prints *live* and *alive* in nine lines (7, 33, 34, 39, 50, 73, 75), and 魄 (*pò*) holds *aliveness* at chapter 10 (`glossary/po-魄.md`).

**"Vital."** The strongest rival, and the Dadai Liji gives it its case: 陰之精氣曰靈, 靈 is the *essence-breath* of yin. But that line defines 靈 by its kind, the way a dictionary names a genus, and giving 靈 the genus's English is rendering a gloss (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-rendering.md`). **The English is also taken**: *vital essence* is 精 (*jīng*)'s locked English (21, 55), and `glossary/po-魄.md` already set *vitality* aside for that collision. **And *vital* has the double meaning *agitation* had.** It means *full of life*, and it means *essential*, as in *vital to*. *Spirit received the one, and so became vital* reads first as *became necessary*. Neither sense is 靈's: not life, and not importance.

**"Blessed," "good."** The Odes' 靈，善也 and the *Guangyun*'s 福也 are real senses. *Blessed* fails the hymn test outright; *good* is right for the rain and empty for a spirit.

**"Quick."** The old English sense is exact, *alive and responsive*, and it is the King James Bible's: *the quick and the dead*. It fails the hymn test, and a modern reader hears *fast*.

**"Awake," "awakened."** Buddhist awakening, the 1971 paperback on consciousness. The register imports a doctrine the chapter does not hold.

**"Present," "presence."** *Presence* is 有 (*yǒu*)'s English, in its locked pairing with 無 (*wú*). And the shaman's sense it would catch, the spirit come down, is the one this entry already admits it loses (see *Left open*).

**"Efficacious," "effective."** The right sense in the wrong register: an apothecary's label, and a mechanism's. *Potent* is the same claim in a word a person says.

**"Responsive."** An abstraction about a relation, and it tells you nothing you could see.

**"Potency," for 神.** A quality, not a party: it can be the ghosts' force but never Wang Bi's spirit standing beside the sage. And once 靈 is *potent*, *potency* for 神 would give two characters one root, in two chapters that never meet, where no check could see it.

**"Demons," "devils," for 鬼.** Christian demonology. A 鬼 is a dead person, and the Shuowen says it does harm, not that it is evil. Chapter 60 is about the harm stopping, which a devil's would not.

**"Spirit vessel," "divine vessel," "sacred system," at chapter 29.** The first is a genie's bottle, the second the watchlist's word, the third the mechanistic overlay `glossary/qi-器.md` set aside.

**The verb at chapter 60 needs scaffolding.** English cannot say *the ghosts do not spirit*, so the line will supply a verb, as Heshang Gong's 見 (*xiàn* — to show) does. Whatever verb the line takes, it is the construction's, not 神's, and it stays out of `render:` (`process/principles/supplied-verb-is-scaffolding.md`).

### What no rule can enforce

***Alive* is ruled out for 靈, and cannot be forbidden.** Chapter 39 holds 生 (*shēng*), whose English is *come to life* and *live*, two lines from 靈. A rule keyed to 靈's presence would fire on 生's lines.

***Present* is ruled out for 靈, and cannot be forbidden either.** Chapter 6 holds 存 (*cún* — to be there, to last), in 綿綿若存 (*mián mián ruò cún* — "unceasing, as if there"), and 存 may legitimately print *present*.

***Spirit* is held for 神 by three other entries**, which forbid it for 身 (*shēn* — body), 精 (*jīng* — vital essence) and 魄 (*pò* — aliveness). 神 shares no chapter with 身, so the build can never see those two meet; `concordance.py --english "spirit"`, run by hand, is the only check.

***Potency* cannot go on this list yet**, for the reason in *Left open*: chapter 60 prints it, and a forbidden word in a chapter where 神 stands is an error the build would report on the spot.

### Left open

**Chapter 60's English waits on chapter 60's grammar.** Its 非 (*fēi*) reads *not only* in our English, where Heshang Gong's 其□非无精神也 (*"it is not that the ghosts lack vital spirit"*) reads it *it is not that*, and the chapter contradicts itself as it stands. That is `WORKLIST.md` R6, the back-translation audit's deepest finding, and it is Shalom's call. The choice between *spirit* and *potency* for 神 belongs in the same rewrite, because the line has to be rebuilt either way.

**Chapter 39's *spirit* is singular and capitalized, by position.** Both 神 lines open their sentence, so *Spirit* takes a capital the edition otherwise refuses, and a capital-S *Spirit* is Hegel's or the creed's. The Shuowen's 天神 (*tiān shén* — the spirits of the sky) is plural beings, and *The spirits received the one* would end the capital and match the Shuowen. The cost: the list's other members are a sky, an earth and a valley, and the chapter's rhythm is singular nouns. Undecided.

***Potent* keeps the effect and loses the descent.** Wang Yi's 靈 is a spirit that has *come down* into a dancer; *potent* says it works, not that it has arrived. The rite's half of the word, the shaman, has no English in the verse and lives only here. The word also has a sexual edge in English, *potent* against *impotent*, which a spirit should not carry; it is faint next to *spirit*, and it was weighed before *potent* was chosen (Shalom's call, 2026-11-19).

**Heshang Gong's organ-spirits are lost at chapter 6.** His 谷神 is the spirit nourished in the body's five organs, and the verse follows Wang Bi's valley. That cost is recorded in `glossary/gu-谷.md`, where the valley was chosen.
