## 聖 (Shèng) — The Sage

*Say "shung," rhyming with "sung," falling. Thirty-one lines across twenty-six chapters. Thirty of them are 聖人 (shèng rén — the sage person), the book's figure for one who governs by not-doing. The thirty-first is 聖 alone, at chapter 19, and it is the first thing the people are told to cut off.*

**Locked: 聖人 → "the sage"; 聖 standing alone → "sagehood."** *(Not "the holy man," not "the saint," not "the wise man," not "the sage-king." Not "the Master" or "wisdom" either. Those two are ruled out but cannot go on the list; see* What no rule can enforce.*)*

In modern Chinese, 聖 is the Bible's word. 聖經 (*shèng jīng*, the holy classic) is the Bible, 聖靈 (*shèng líng*) is the Holy Spirit, and 聖誕 (*shèng dàn*, the holy birth) is Christmas. Read that back into a text from the fourth century BCE and 聖人 becomes a holy man. It was not one. The character began as an ear.

### The graph: an ear, a mouth, a person standing

**The oracle-bone graph is a person with an oversized ear, and a mouth beside it.** The Chinese University of Hong Kong's character database describes it as 從人從耳從口 (*from 人 a person, from 耳 an ear, from 口 a mouth*): a sound coming toward an open ear. In the oracle bones the graph is used to mean 聽 (*tīng* — to listen). 聖, 聽 and 聲 (*shēng* — sound) are one family and began as one graph. The Qing epigrapher 吳大澂 (*Wú Dàchéng*) put it plainly: 聖、聲古通, *"聖 and 聲 were anciently interchangeable."*

In the Western Zhou bronzes the standing person becomes 𡈼 (*tǐng* — a figure standing on the ground). 口 above 𡈼 later looked like 呈 (*chéng* — to present), and that is how the *Shuowen Jiezi* (c. 100 CE) came to analyse it:

> 聖，通也。从耳呈聲。
> *"聖 is 通 (tōng — getting through). From 耳 (the ear), with 呈 for sound."*

**The 呈 is a reanalysis, not the origin.** 鄭張尚芳 (*Zhèngzhāng Shàngfāng*) says so directly: 甲金文象人張耳對口會耳聰意，非呈聲, *"the oracle and bronze graphs show a person opening the ear toward a mouth, meaning keen hearing; it is not 呈 for sound."* The oracle-bone lexicographer 李孝定 (*Lǐ Xiàodìng*), as quoted by later scholars, gives the whole sequence in one sentence:

> 聖之初誼為聽覺官能之敏銳，故引申訓「通」；賢聖之意，又其引申也
> *"The first sense of 聖 is acuteness of the hearing faculty; extended, it is glossed 'getting through'; worthiness and sagehood are a further extension."*

So the ear comes first, getting through comes second, and the exalted figure comes last. 通 is a road with nothing blocking it, so 聖 is the hearing that gets all the way to the source.

**The Han and Qing scholars kept hold of the ear.** 段玉裁 (*Duàn Yùcái*, 1815) explains the radical: 聖从耳者，謂其耳順, *"聖 takes the ear because its ear is attuned."* 耳順 (*ěr shùn* — the ear going with the grain) is Confucius's own word for what came to him at sixty (*Analects* 2.4). Duàn then quotes the *Fengsu tongyi* (2nd c. CE): 聖者，聲也。言聞聲知情, *"聖 is sound: it means hearing the sound and knowing the situation."* The *Baihu tong* (79 CE) gives the same triple, 聖者，通也，道也，聲也, *"聖 is getting through, it is the way, it is sound."*

**The silk manuscripts still write the sage with the sound.** Mawangdui silk A (before 168 BCE) writes the sage as **聲人** (*shēng rén* — the sound person) throughout the half that carries chapters 1–37. That includes 是以聲人居無為之事 at chapter 2 and 絕聲棄知 at chapter 19. Its other half and silk B write 聖人. In this book's oldest complete copy, the graph family had not yet finished splitting.

### Before it was a summit

**For a long time 聖 was an everyday word.** It meant quick, perceptive, good at something.

- **The Odes.** 母氏聖善, *"our mother is 聖 and good"* (邶風·凱風). The Mao commentary glosses it 聖，叡也, *perceptive*.
- **The Odes again.** 或聖或否, *"some are 聖, some not"* (小雅·小旻), glossed 人有通聖者，有不能者, *"some people get through, and some cannot."*
- **段玉裁's summary:** 凡一事精通，亦得謂之聖, *"anyone thoroughly versed in a single thing may also be called 聖."*
- **The *Analects*.** When a minister asks 夫子聖者與？何其多能也, *"Is your master 聖? How many things he can do!"* (9.6), 聖 is all-round capability.

**Then the word climbed.** Confucius refuses it for himself: 若聖與仁，則吾豈敢, *"as for 聖 and humaneness, how would I dare?"* (7.34). Mencius sets it at the top: 聖人，人倫之至也, *"the sage is the summit of human relations."* The sages of Warring States lore are the founding kings who **made things**: writing, ritual, music, measures, law. That figure is the one chapter 19 tells the reader to cut off.

### Hearing against seeing: 聖 beside 智

**The tomb that held the oldest Laozi also names what 聖 is.** Guodian tomb 1 (~300 BCE) held the bamboo Laozi alongside a Confucian text, the 五行 (*Wǔ xíng* — the five conducts), which defines the two virtues that chapter 19 pairs:

> 見而知之，智也。聞而知之，聖也。
> *"To see it and know it is 智 (zhì — knowing). To hear it and know it is 聖."*

To that writer, 聖 and 智 are two ways of knowing: one by the ear, one by the eye. The pair 聖智 was a fixed phrase, and 絕聖棄智 cuts off both at once. A later text, the 文子 (*Wénzǐ*), puts the same definition in Laozi's own mouth: 聞而知之，聖也；見而知之，智也. That shows how close the reading sat to this book, though it is not evidence about what Laozi meant.

### The figure in this book

**The sage almost always arrives as a conclusion.** Twenty-one of the thirty 聖人 lines open 是以聖人 or 故聖人, *"therefore the sage."* The chapter first observes how sky and earth, water, the crowd or the state behave, and then the sage is what follows from it. The sage is drawn as **the one who has drawn the consequence**.

**The sage is described mostly by what they decline.** Many lines are refusals: not humane (5), does not travel (47), does not do the great (63), does not rely (77), does not cut (58), does not hoard (81), is not sick (71), has no fixed heart (49), does not exact payment (79). The positive verbs are few and plain: embraces one thing (22), acts for the belly (12), wears coarse cloth (70), puts them to use (28), grasps the left half of the tally (79).

**And the sage governs.** 聖人之治, *the sage's governing* (3). 處上, *stands above* the people (66). 為官長, becomes *chief of the officers* (28). Takes on the filth of the state and so is *the ruler of the world* (78). The book is addressed to people who hold office, and the sage is its model for holding it. **But 聖人 is not a rank.** The book has rank words of its own: 王 (*wáng* — ruler), 侯王 (*hóu wáng* — lords and rulers), 君 (*jūn* — ruler), 主 (*zhǔ* — lord). 聖人 is none of them.

### What the witnesses add

- **Guodian (~300 BCE)** has 聖人 in chapters 2, 57, 63, 64 and 66, all complete. Chapter 5 survives there only as its bellows passage, so *the sage is not concerned with humaneness* is not in the oldest witness. At **chapter 19 the slips carry no 聖**: the pair cut off is 智 and 辯 (*biàn* — disputation), and humaneness and duty are not cut either. The book's oldest copy keeps the sage and does not tell anyone to cut sagehood off. See `notes/manuscript.md` · Ch 19.
- **Chapter 26 shows the two figures drifting together in transmission.** Our base prints 君子 (*jūn zǐ* — the noble, literally a ruler's son, the Confucian honorific). **Both Mawangdui silks agree with it.** 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) and the Siku 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) print 聖人. Somewhere in the copying, the noble was absorbed into the sage. The English keeps them apart: 君子 reads *the noble* at chapters 26 and 31.
- **Chapter 73** carries 是以聖人猶難之, *"so the sage still finds it difficult,"* by both commentaries. It is word for word a line of chapter 63, and silk B does not have it at chapter 73.
- **Chapter 49**: silk B reads 聖人恒無心, *"the sage is ever without a heart [of their own],"* against our 無常心. **Chapter 60**: 韓非 (*Hán Fēi*) reads 聖人亦不傷民. Both are in `sources/variants.yaml`.

### Why "the sage"

**The English word began in a sense too.** *Sage* comes through French from Latin *sapere*, *to taste*, *to have taste*, and from there *to be wise*. So the Chinese word starts with the ear and the English with the tongue. Each names a person by a bodily sense that has become judgment. Neither word has a church in it. Nobody is canonised a sage.

**The ear belongs in the graph and the notes, not in the verse.** *The listener*, *the attuned one*, *the one who hears through* would each be a more accurate label for the oracle-bone graph. A label stops the reader to explain itself, and a Laozi whose sage is *the attuned one* has swapped a missionary register for a workshop one. See `process/principles/perform-dont-label.md`. *The sage* is old, plain, and already in the reader's ear. The research lives here and in the Atlas, where a reader can find it.

**One sage, not several.** Classical Chinese does not mark number, and Ames and Hall render 聖人 consistently as *the sages*. This edition keeps a single figure, because the book draws one. The pronoun is singular *they/their*, never *he* (standing rule 1).

**Sagehood, at chapter 19, so the reader sees the same word.** The shock of 絕聖棄智 is that a book which holds up the sage in twenty-five chapters tells you here to cut sagehood off. The English must let the reader hear the same root, or the contradiction the commentators argue over disappears.

Both classical commentators say which sagehood is meant. 河上公: 絕聖制作，反初守元。五帝垂象，蒼頡作書，不如三皇結繩無文, *"cut off the sages' making of things, return to the beginning and keep to the origin. The Five Emperors hanging out their images and Cāngjié creating writing are not as good as the Three August Ones' knotted cords, with no writing at all."* 王弼: 聖智，才之善也, *"sagehood and knowing are the best of talent."* The 莊子 (*Zhuāngzǐ*, 胠篋) uses the same four characters for the same target: 聖人不死，大盜不止…故絕聖棄知，大盜乃止, *"while the sages do not die, the great thieves do not stop… so cut off sagehood, discard knowing, and the great thieves stop."* In every reading, what is cut is sagehood as an institution: the inventor, the founder, the one whose tools a thief can take. *Sagehood* names that status and that role. *Sageliness*, a manner or a demeanour, does not.

### Set aside

**holy · saint · blessed.** 聖 became the word for *holy* when the nineteenth-century Bible translators chose it for that. The English Laozi then read that choice back into the original: Paul Carus's *"the holy man"* (1898), and Stephen Mitchell's chapter 19, *"throw away holiness and wisdom."* Chapter 19 shows the cost. A book that praises *the holy man* in twenty-five chapters and says *throw away holiness* in one becomes an attack on religion. The Chinese is an attack on founders and their inventions. This is the first entry on the overlay watchlist (`CLAUDE.md`, `process/overlay-audit.md`).

**the Master.** Mitchell's word, and the most common in popular English versions. It fails three ways:
- *Master* is spoken for. It belongs to 善 (*shàn* — masterful), which shares eight chapters with this character, and to 師 (*shī* — the teacher) in chapter 27, where *"the novice is the raw material of the master."*
- It makes the sage a teacher with disciples, which is the register of a guru.
- It puts back the rank that `glossary/shi-士.md` had to strip out of *the ancient masters*.

**the wise man · the wise one · the wise soul.** Blakney's *"the Wise Man,"* Le Guin's *"the wise soul."* *Man* is a male default the Chinese does not print; 人 is any person. *Soul* is on the watchlist in its own right. And *wise* is ground that 智 contests: chapter 19 sets the two characters side by side, and *"cut off wisdom, abandon cleverness"* would hand 聖 the very English 智's overlay is accused of.

**the perfect man.** Imported from the 莊子's 至人 (*zhì rén* — the utmost person), a different word for a different figure.

**sage-king · sage-ruler.** The sage governs, but the character is not a rank, and the book names its rulers with other words (above). The hyphen supplies a throne the Chinese does not print, and decides the question in *What stays open*.

**sageliness.** Wing-tsit Chan's and Robert Henricks's chapter 19, and this repository's former apparatus gloss. It names a manner where the commentators name a role.

### Working register

| Form | English | Where | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 聖人, subject | **the sage** | 25 chapters | 是以聖人抱一 → *"Therefore the sage embraces one thing"* (22) |
| 聖人之 + noun | **the sage's** / **of the sage** | 3, 81 | 聖人之道 → *"the way of the sage"* (81) |
| 聖人云 | **the sage says** | 57, 78 | 故聖人云 → *"Therefore the sage says:"* (57) |
| 聖, standing alone | **sagehood** | 19 | 絕聖棄智 → *"Cut off sagehood, abandon cleverness"* |

**The Chinese names the sage once and lets the subject run.** Chapter 2 prints 聖人 once and governs six clauses with it. English needs a subject in every sentence, so it repeats *the sage* or uses *they*. The repetition is the English grammar's, not the character's, and the count of English *sage* lines (thirty-six) will always run higher than the count of 聖 lines (thirty-one).

### What no rule can enforce

***"master"* and *"wise"* are ruled out for 聖 in the verse, and neither can go on the `forbidden:` list.** `check_locks.py` fires on a forbidden word wherever its character stands in that chapter's Chinese. **Master** is 善's English (*masterful*), and 善 shares eight chapters with 聖, among them 27, 49 and 79; it is also 師's in chapter 27. **Wise** and **wisdom** may yet be 智's: its entry is owed (`WORKLIST.md` T2-2), and it shares chapters 3, 19 and 27 with 聖. A bare *wise* would also fire inside *otherwise*. So the build holds *holy*, *saint* and the rest, and only this entry holds *master* and *wise*.

**The capital is held, by a different rule.** *Sage* and *Master* capitalised mid-sentence are caught by the `devotional-capitalization` rule, which maps both to 聖. That is why *"the Sage"* and *"the Master"* are not repeated on this list.

### What stays open

**The ear is in the graph and nowhere in the English, and the book itself is uneasy about ears.** The character for the sage is a picture of acute hearing. Yet in this book:
- *the five tones make the ears deaf* (12), in the same chapter where the sage *acts for the belly, not for the eye*;
- the Tao is *listened for and not heard* (14);
- *the great sound is barely heard* (41);
- the sage *knows without going and names without seeing* (47);
- at 49, everyone *pours out their attention, all eyes and ears*, and the sage treats them as infants;
- 河上公, on chapter 22, has the sage see *not with their own eyes, but by the eyes of the world* (乃因天下之目以視).

The figure whose name is an ear lives in a book that distrusts the senses. Two readings are possible. On one, 聖 is the hearing that gets through: it does not strain, which is why it is not deafened. On the other, the book has taken over a word for perceptive skill and is teaching it to stop straining. The graph cannot decide this, and a pattern across chapters is a reason to look, not a conclusion (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`). It is recorded here so a reader can look.

**Is the sage a ruler?** The book addresses rulers, the sage governs in four chapters (3, 28, 66, 78), and 河上公 titles chapter 32 聖德 (*the integrity of the sage*) though that chapter prints 侯王 (*lords and rulers*) and never 聖人. The commentator reads the ruler and the sage as one person. The text keeps two words. Whether 聖人 is the ideal *of* a ruler or an ideal *for* anyone is the same rank question that `WORKLIST.md` T4-7 holds open for 君子, 士 and 大丈夫. *The sage* leaves it open, and that is a choice.

**Chapter 19 against the other twenty-five.** On the commentators' reading, the sagehood cut off is the founders' and the sage praised is Laozi's: two figures sharing one word. That resolves the contradiction by splitting the word. The Guodian slips resolve it by not having the line. Neither resolution is in the received text, which prints both and leaves the reader holding them.
