## 谷 (Gǔ) — The Valley

*Say "goo," dipping and lifting. Ten lines in seven chapters. One of the text's plainest images, and the one it trusts with the dark female.*

**Recommended: 谷 (*gǔ*) → "valley," in every line.** *(Not "reservoir" or "basin," which hold the water a valley lets go. Not "hollow," "the void" or "emptiness," which name the gap instead of the land. Not "ravine," which belongs to 谿 (xī — a watercourse with no outlet); see* What no rule can enforce.*)* *The ruling between the two commentators on chapter 6 is still open; see the close.*

### The graph: water half seen, coming out of an opening

> 谷，泉出通川爲谷。从水半見，出於口。
> *"Valley: where a spring comes out and runs through to a river is a valley. From 水 (shuǐ — water) half seen, coming out of 口 (kǒu — an opening)."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

**The picture is water at the moment it comes out of the land.** In the oracle bones and the bronzes the graph is the same: broken strokes of water above, and an opening below. The broken strokes are water not yet a stream, a spring just out of the hill, and the opening is the valley's mouth (the *Ziyuan*, quoted by *Handian*). The Shuowen reads it the same way: 水半見 (*shuǐ bàn jiàn* — "water half seen"), 出於口 (*chū yú kǒu* — "coming out of an opening").

**The Shuowen defines the valley by where its water goes.** The definition has three verbs, 出 (*chū* — to come out), 通 (*tōng* — to run through) and 川 (*chuān* — a river) as the place it runs to. The character directly after it in the dictionary is its opposite:

> 谿，山瀆無所通者。从谷奚聲。
> *"Ravine: a mountain watercourse with no way through. From 谷 (gǔ — valley), with 奚 (xī, used here only for its sound)."*

A 谿 (*xī* — "ravine") is a valley closed at the bottom. Both are land that water runs down, and the Shuowen separates them only by whether the water gets out. **So a valley is not where water is held. It is where water passes.**

**Xu Shen's definitions run downhill in a chain.** 川 (*chuān* — a river) is 貫穿通流水也, *"water that cuts through and flows on."* 海 (*hǎi* — the sea) is 天池也，以納百川者, *"the pool of the sky, which takes in the hundred rivers."* Spring, valley, river, sea: the receiving happens at the bottom, and the dictionary gives it to the sea. The text gives it to the sea too (below).

**The valley stands in other characters, and in one of them it holds.** 容 (*róng* — to hold) is 盛也。从宀、谷, *"to hold, as a vessel holds; from 宀 (mián — a roof) and 谷 (gǔ — valley),"* a roof over a valley. In 欲 (*yù* — to want), 俗 (*sú* — custom) and 卻 (*què* — to step back), the valley character is only the sound. Chapter 15's valley-people have 不欲盈 (*bù yù yíng* — "no wish to be full"), so the valley's sound stands in the line about it, but that is sound, not evidence, and the entry rests nothing on it.

### Ten lines, and the valley is land in every one

> 谷神不死 (*gǔ shén bù sǐ* — "the valley's spirit does not die")
> *The spirit of the valley is deathless.* — ch 6

> 曠兮其若谷 (*kuàng xī qí ruò gǔ* — "wide, like a valley")
> *Open, like a valley.* — ch 15

> 為天下谷 (*wéi tiān xià gǔ* — "serve as the world's valley")
> *Serve as the world's valley.* — ch 28, twice

> 猶川谷之於江海 (*yóu chuān gǔ zhī yú jiāng hǎi* — "like streams and valleys to the river and the sea")
> *The Tao in the world is like streams and valleys to the river and the sea.* — ch 32

> 谷得一以盈 (*gǔ dé yī yǐ yíng* — "the valley received the one and became full")
> 谷無以盈，將恐竭 (*gǔ wú yǐ yíng, jiāng kǒng jié* — "the valley with no way to be full is likely to run dry")
> *The valley received the one, and so became full.* · *The valley with no way to be full is likely to run dry.* — ch 39

> 上德若谷 (*shàng dé ruò gǔ* — "the highest integrity seems a valley")
> *The highest integrity seems a valley.* — ch 41

> 江海所以能為百谷王者，以其善下之 (*jiāng hǎi suǒ yǐ néng wéi bǎi gǔ wàng zhě, yǐ qí shàn xià zhī* — "rivers and seas can rule the hundred valleys because they are masterful at being below them")
> *Rivers and seas can rule the hundred valleys / because they are masterful at staying below them.* — ch 66, and again in its next line

**In chapters 32 and 66 the valley is not the lowest place.** Valleys run down to the river and the sea, and it is the river and the sea that stay below. Heshang Gong (Han dynasty) on chapter 66: 江海以卑，故衆流歸之, *"rivers and seas are low, so the many streams return to them."* The oldest witness says it more plainly still. Guodian (~300 BCE) reads 以其能為百谷下 (*yǐ qí néng wéi bǎi gǔ xià* — "because they can be below the hundred valleys"). Wang Bi (d. 249) on chapter 32: 川谷之求江與海，非江海召之，不召不求而自歸者, *"streams and valleys seek the river and the sea, but the river and the sea do not summon them; unsummoned and unsought, they return of themselves."* Heshang Gong on the same line uses the Shuowen's own verb: 如川谷與江海相流通也, *"as streams and valleys and the river and the sea flow through to one another."*

So the valley's lowness is a slope's lowness. It is low because it is on the way down. That is why the valley gives water and the sea takes it in.

**In chapter 39 the valley fills, and the line after warns against wanting to stay full.** The verb is the vessel's, not the valley's: the Shuowen defines 盈 (*yíng* — full) as 滿器也, *"a full vessel."* Wang Bi: 清不能為清，盈不能為盈，皆有其母以存其形, *"clearness cannot make itself clear, fullness cannot make itself full; each has its mother, by which its form is kept."* Heshang Gong reads the warning line as a paradox: 谷當有盈縮虛實，不可但欲盈滿無已時，將恐枯竭不為谷, *"a valley must have filling and shrinking, emptiness and fullness; it cannot only want to be full without end, or it will dry out and stop being a valley."* A valley full the whole year is a lake. Chapter 15 says the same of the people it is likened to: 保此道者不欲盈, *"those who keep the Tao safe like this have no wish to be full."*

**In chapter 15 the valley is wide and open to the sky.** The Shuowen defines 曠 (*kuàng* — wide, bright) as 明也，从日廣聲, *"bright; from 日 (rì — the sun), with 廣 (guǎng — broad) for sound."* Heshang Gong: 曠者寬大，谷者空虛，不有德功名，無所不包也, *"'Wide' means broad and large; 'valley' means empty. It claims no integrity, achievement or name, and there is nothing it does not enfold."* *Open* carries the width and the light together.

**In chapter 41 the valley keeps nothing to itself.** Wang Bi on 上德若谷 (*shàng dé ruò gǔ* — "the highest integrity seems a valley"): 不德其德，無所懷也, *"it does not count its integrity as integrity; there is nothing it holds close."* 懷 (*huái* — to hold to the breast) is what the valley does not do. What comes into it goes on.

### The valley's company: the soil, and no shame in it

**The valley stands beside 辱 (*rǔ* — soiling, disgrace) twice.** Our base text reads chapter 28 as 知其榮，守其辱，為天下谷 (*zhī qí róng, shǒu qí rǔ, wéi tiān xià gǔ* — "know the glory, hold to the disgrace, serve as the world's valley"). In chapter 41 the line after the valley is 大白若辱 (*dà bái ruò rǔ*), rendered "the whitest white seems soiled." The verse now reads *soiled* in both, for the reason below.

**The graph of 辱 (*rǔ*) is a hand working a field.** The Shuowen explains it with farming: 恥也。从寸在辰下。失耕時，於封畺上戮之也。辰者，農之時也, *"shame. From 寸 (cùn — a hand) beneath 辰 (chén — a farming blade; the farming season). Whoever missed the ploughing season was punished on the field's boundary. Chén is the season of farming."* The oracle-bone graph is older than that story. It shows a hand holding the blade, which many paleographers read as a shell used to cut weeds, and its first sense was *to weed* or *to till*, later written 耨 (*nòu* — to hoe) (*Handian*; *Dong Chinese*). The sources differ on what tool the blade was. They agree that the hand is in the soil. The shame is a later meaning, laid over a farmer's work.

**Both times, Heshang Gong reads the valley's *rǔ* as dirt, and says the valley is not ashamed of it.** On chapter 28: 辱以喻汚濁 … 如是則天下歸之，如水流入深谷也, *"Rǔ figures the dirty and the murky … do this and the world returns to you, as water flows into a deep valley."* On chapter 41: 上德之人若深谷，不恥垢濁也, *"the person of highest integrity is like a deep valley, and is not ashamed of grime and murk."* The Shuowen defines 垢 (*gòu* — grime) as 濁也，从土, *"murky; from 土 (tǔ — earth)."* 恥 (*chǐ* — shame) is the word the Shuowen uses to define *rǔ* itself. The commentator is saying that the valley does not take the soil as the thing it is supposed to mean.

**The older witnesses change chapter 28's pair.** Both Mawangdui silks read 知其白，守其辱，為天下浴 (*zhī qí bái, shǒu qí rǔ, wéi tiān xià yù* — "know the white, hold to the soiled, serve as the world's valley"). The pair is 白 (*bái* — white) and *rǔ*, not 榮 (*róng* — glory) and *rǔ*. Beside white, *rǔ* is a mark on a surface, as it is in chapter 41. Beside glory, it is a judgment in other people's eyes. The *Zhuangzi* quotes the stanza the same way, ravine first and then the valley with white, and goes on in the same breath: 受天下之垢 (*shòu tiān xià zhī gòu* — "take on the world's grime"). Wang Bi reads chapter 41 straight into chapter 28: 知其白守其黑，大白然後乃得, *"know the white, hold to the black; only then is the great white attained."* The verse follows the silks: *"Know the white, hold to the soiled, / serve as the world's valley"* (Shalom's call, 2026-10-25). `sources/variants.yaml` and `notes/manuscript.md` have the facts, and `DISCOVERIES.md` §8 the question behind the call.

In both readings *rǔ* is the valley's company, and in neither is the valley ashamed of it. The difference is whether the line names a stain or a reputation. Either way, nothing in the valley's own character passes a verdict. The valley is made of earth, and earth is not a fault.

### Chapter 6: two commentators, two grammars

**Wang Bi reads the character as a noun, and makes it nothing.** 谷神，谷中央無谷也。無形無影，無逆無違，處卑不動，守靜不衰, *"The valley's spirit is the middle of the valley, where there is no valley. It has no form and no shadow, it opposes nothing and resists nothing; it stays low and does not move, keeps still and does not fail."* The valley's spirit is the empty center between the slopes.

**Heshang Gong reads it as a verb, and the valley leaves the line.** 谷，養也。人能養神則不死也, *"Gǔ means to nourish. If a person can nourish the spirit, they do not die."* 神 (*shén* — spirit) for him is the spirits of the five organs. The dark female is the nose and the mouth, and the gate is where breath comes and goes.

**Recommended: Wang Bi's grammar, and not his emptiness.** *The spirit of the valley is deathless* keeps 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) a noun. Five reasons:

- **The other nine lines.** Everywhere else in the book the character is a valley, and no one reads it otherwise.
- **The silks spell chapter 6 as they spell the land.** Both Mawangdui silks write 浴 (*yù* — to bathe) in chapter 6, the same graph they write in chapters 28, 32, 39 and 66. Those lines have rivers and seas in them, so the bathing graph there can only spell the valley (below). The scribes heard one word in all of them.
- **Heshang Gong gives no reason for 養 (*yǎng* — to nourish).** His gloss works only if 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) is heard as a different word that sounds the same, and the likeliest is 穀 (*gǔ* — grain), the book's own word in 不穀 (*bù gǔ* — "unworthy," literally "not good grain," ch 39). That is an inference, and the silks do not support it. The *Huainanzi* (139 BCE) does show the hearing was available: quoting chapter 28, it spells the valley 穀 (*gǔ* — grain). That proves the two graphs could stand for each other in the Han, which is not the same as proving chapter 6 means *nourish*.
- **A commentator's gloss is evidence, not a rendering.** Heshang Gong's breath is a reading of the Han dynasty, and a coherent one: 綿綿若存 (*mián mián ruò cún* — "unbroken, as if barely there") fits breath very well. It is still his reading, not the line.
- **The pointing test.** A valley can be pointed at. "The middle of the valley, where there is no valley" cannot, and neither can a nourished organ-spirit. Wang Bi's grammar can be kept without his metaphysics: what he does say in plain terms, 處卑不動 (*chǔ bēi bù dòng* — "it stays low and does not move"), is true of the land. The slopes stay. The water goes through.

**What Wang Bi's grammar costs.** Heshang Gong's reading gives chapter 6 a body that breathes, and the English loses that body. It also loses the one reading in which 用之不勤 (*yòng zhī bù qín* — "use it, and there is no strain") describes something a person does. A reader who wants the breath reading has it in Heshang Gong; the verse cannot carry both.

### 浴 (yù — to bathe) in the silks: a spelling, not a second word

**The Mawangdui silks write the valley as 浴 (*yù* — to bathe) almost everywhere.** It is 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) with 氵 (*shuǐ* — water) beside it. Both silks have it in every valley line that survives, with one exception: silk B writes plain 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) in chapter 39's second valley line.

**It cannot mean bathing.** In chapter 66, 百浴王 (*bǎi yù wàng* — "rule the hundred bathings") stands where every other witness has valleys, and *rivers and seas can rule the hundred bathings* is not a sentence. So the bathing graph is standing in for the valley by sound and shape. **The oldest witness writes the valley plainly.** Guodian has plain 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) in chapters 41 and 66, and 小谷 (*xiǎo gǔ* — "small valleys") in chapter 32. It does not carry chapters 6, 28 or 39. The added water may be a scribe's habit or a scribe's hearing, and no reading is built on it.

### 谿 (xī — ravine)

**The ravine stands in one chapter, twice.** Chapter 28's first stanza reads 知其雄，守其雌，為天下谿 (*zhī qí xióng, shǒu qí cí, wéi tiān xià xī* — "know the rooster, hold to the hen, serve as the world's ravine"). Its third stanza reads 為天下谷 (*wéi tiān xià gǔ* — "serve as the world's valley"). The chapter sets the closed watercourse and the open one in the same template, and the English keeps them apart with two words. It cannot keep the Shuowen's reason for the difference, which is the outlet, and so this section exists.

**The stanzas may answer each other.** The ravine's line ends 常德不離 (*cháng dé bù lí* — "the ever-present integrity never leaves you"): nothing leaves a watercourse with no outlet. The valley's ends 常德乃足 (*cháng dé nǎi zú* — "the ever-present integrity is enough at last"). Wang Bi on the ravine: 谿不求物而物自歸之, *"the ravine does not seek things, and things come to it of themselves."* That fit may be an accident of the template. It is a reason to look, and not yet a reason to conclude.

### What is set aside

**"reservoir."** It is a tank that keeps water in, and the Shuowen defines the valley by letting water out. If anything in the book is a reservoir, it is the ravine.

**"basin."** A basin is a vessel, and the vessel enters chapter 39 through 盈 (*yíng* — full, written with 皿 *mǐn*, a dish), not through the valley. The Shuowen's basin is the sea: 天池 (*tiān chí* — "the sky's pool"). A *basin* would put the sea's work on the valley, which is the confusion chapters 32 and 66 rule out.

**"hollow."** It is good English for a small valley, and its weight falls on the emptiness, the scooped-out part. Heshang Gong's 谷者空虛 (*gǔ zhě kōng xū* — "a valley is empty," ch 15) is a commentator's reading, and the land is the thing the line points at. *Hollow* also drifts toward 虛 (*xū* — empty), which has its own entry.

**"the void," "emptiness."** These are Wang Bi's 無谷 (*wú gǔ* — "no valley") made into a noun, and they label an idea where the line has a place. Both are already forbidden for 虛 (*xū* — empty).

**"the Valley."** A capital turns a place into a doctrine, and only the Tao keeps one.

**"to nourish," in chapter 6.** This is Heshang Gong's reading, argued above. It is not on the `forbidden:` list, because chapter 6 is not yet ruled.

### What no rule can enforce

***Ravine* is ruled out for the valley and cannot go on the `forbidden:` list.** `check_locks.py` fires on a forbidden word wherever its character stands in that chapter's Chinese, and chapter 28 holds both 谷 (*gǔ* — valley) and 谿 (*xī* — ravine). *Ravine* is the second one's English there, twice, so forbidding it for the valley would flag the correct line. Only this entry holds that distinction.

**The lock holds in this book only.** The I Ching vendors these locks as its own, and its 谷 (*gǔ*) is not always land. At its hexagram 48, 井谷 (*jǐng gǔ*) is the bottom of a well shaft, which it renders *the well's hollow*. At its hexagram 30 the character stands where its source text misprints 穀 (*gǔ* — grain). A lock that forbade *hollow* there would flag a deliberate rendering and a typo. So this entry carries `binds: [taoteching]`, and the I Ching rules its own valley.

### Left open

**Chapter 6's grammar.** The recommendation is to read the valley as a noun, as Wang Bi does, without his emptiness. It costs Heshang Gong's breathing body. The ruling belongs in the lock line, openly, when it is made.

**What 辱 (*rǔ* — soiling, disgrace) points at.** Chapter 28 now follows the silks and reads *soiled*. Whether the text means the earth, the low place in others' eyes, or both at once is `DISCOVERIES.md` §8, and the character is owed its own entry.

**Chapter 15's adjective.** Silk B reads 莊 (*zhuāng* — grave, dignified) where our base reads 曠 (*kuàng* — wide, bright), and both silks place the valley simile after the muddy water, not before it. Whether the silk's word is a loan for ours or a different word decides whether the valley there is *open* or *grave*.

**Chapter 32's streams.** Guodian reads 小谷 (*xiǎo gǔ* — "small valleys") where our base reads 川谷 (*chuān gǔ* — "streams and valleys"), and silk B agrees with Guodian. With *small*, the line is small valleys running down to the river and the sea, and the streams are gone.

**Full, or passing through.** Chapter 39's valley receives the one and becomes full. Chapter 15's valley-people have no wish to be full. The Shuowen's valley is a passage. 盈 (*yíng* — full) is what the line says, and the English keeps it. Whether the valley's fullness is a store or a flow, the water in the valley at the wet time of year and gone at the dry, is the question the image leaves for the reader.
