## 水 (Shuǐ) — Water

*Say "shway," dipping and lifting. Three lines in two chapters, and a fourth in one of the silks. The book's one image for the way it wants a person to be.*

**Recommended: 水 (*shuǐ*) → "water," in every line.** *(Not "the water element," which is the Han dynasty's five phases. Not "liquid" or "fluid," which name a state of matter where the line has a thing you can drink. Not "the Water," since a capital makes it a doctrine.)* *Three forks in its two chapters are still open; see the close.*

### The graph: streams side by side, and a level

> 水，準也。北方之行。象衆水並流，中有微陽之气也。
> *"Water: level. The agent of the north. It pictures many streams running side by side, with a faint breath of yang in the middle."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

**The picture is moving water.** The oracle-bone graph is a winding line with short strokes beside it: a current, and the water at its edges. The Shuowen reads the same picture as several streams running together.

**The Shuowen's first word for water is 準 (*zhǔn* — level, a standard).** Before it says what water looks like, it says what water does: it finds the level. Still water is how a builder knows what is flat, and 準 later names the builder's level and any standard measured against one. Heshang Gong hears the same thing in chapter 8's governing line: 无有不洗，清且平也, *"there is nothing it does not wash; it is clear and level."* 平 (*píng* — level) is the word.

**The rest of the definition is the Han's, not the book's.** 北方之行 (*běi fāng zhī xíng* — "the agent of the north") and 微陽之气 (*wēi yáng zhī qì* — "a faint breath of yang") place water in the five phases, the Han system of 五行 (*wǔ xíng* — five agents) that pairs it with the north, winter and the color black. The Tao Te Ching never uses that system. Its water is the water in a stream.

### Three lines, and what water does in them

> 上善若水 (*shàng shàn ruò shuǐ* — "the highest masterful is like water")
> *The most masterful are like water.* — ch 8

> 水善利萬物而不爭，處衆人之所惡，故幾於道 (*shuǐ shàn lì wàn wù ér bù zhēng, chǔ zhòng rén zhī suǒ wù, gù jī yú dào* — "water is masterful at benefiting the countless things and does not contend; it stays where the crowd loathes, and so is nearly the Tao")
> *Water is masterful at benefiting the countless things and does not contend. / It dwells in what the crowd loathes, / and so is nearly the Tao.* — ch 8

> 天下莫柔弱於水，而攻堅強者莫之能勝，以其無以易之 (*tiān xià mò róu ruò yú shuǐ, ér gōng jiān qiáng zhě mò zhī néng shèng, yǐ qí wú yǐ yì zhī* — "nothing in the world is softer and more yielding than water, yet at attacking the hard and strong nothing overcomes it, because nothing can take its place")
> *In all the world nothing is softer and more yielding than water, / yet at attacking the hard and the strong nothing overcomes it, / for nothing can take its place.* — ch 78

**Water does four things in these lines, and each is a thing you can watch it do.**

- **It benefits and does not contend.** 利 (*lì* — to benefit) is a blade in the Shuowen, 銛也 (*"sharp"*), and the benefit is concrete: Heshang Gong's water is 在天為霧露，在地為泉源, *"in the sky it is mist and dew, on the ground it is springs."* It gives, and it does not push to be first.
- **It goes where no one wants to be.** Heshang Gong names the place: 衆人惡卑濕垢濁，水獨靜流居之也, *"the crowd loathes the low, the damp, the grimy and the murky; water alone, flowing quietly, dwells there."* Wang Bi says it in three characters: 人惡卑也, *"people loathe the low."*
- **It is nearly the Tao, and not the Tao.** Wang Bi: 道無水有，故曰幾也, *"the Tao has no form and water has one, so the text says 'nearly.'"* Water is the closest thing to the Tao that can be held in the hand, and the closeness is the limit.
- **It is the softest thing, and nothing overcomes it.** Heshang Gong: 水能懷山襄陵，磨鐵消銅, *"water can embrace mountains and rise over hills, wear down iron and dissolve bronze."* Wang Bi on why nothing can replace it: 言用水之柔弱，無物可以易之也, *"it says that for the use of water's softness, there is nothing that can take its place."*

### Water takes the shape of what holds it

**Both commentators describe water by what it gives way to.** Heshang Gong on chapter 8's 事善能 (*shì shàn néng* — "in affairs, masterful at what can be done"): 能方能圓，曲直隨形, *"it can be square, it can be round; crooked or straight, it follows the form."* On chapter 78: 圓中則圓，方中則方，擁之則止，決之則行, *"in a round thing it is round, in a square thing square; dam it and it stops, open the dam and it goes."* On chapter 8's 動善時 (*dòng shàn shí* — "in moving, masterful at timing"): 夏散冬凝，應期而動, *"it disperses in summer and freezes in winter, moving with the season."*

This is the yielding that the 柔 (*róu* — soft) and 弱 (*ruò* — yielding) entries argue for, and water is where the book shows it. *Soft* is not a lack of strength. It is the strength that wears iron down.

### Water and the valley

**Water is what the valley is for.** The Shuowen defines the valley as 泉出通川 (*quán chū tōng chuān* — "a spring that comes out and runs through to a river"), and the river as 貫穿通流水也 (*guàn chuān tōng liú shuǐ yě* — "water that cuts through and flows on"). The valley is the land, and the water is what passes through it. Chapter 66's rivers and seas rule the hundred valleys 以其善下之 (*yǐ qí shàn xià zhī* — "because they are masterful at being below them"), and chapter 8's water does the same: it goes down, and it stays where it lands.

**Heshang Gong reads chapter 8's ground line through the female.** On 居善地 (*jū shàn dì* — "in settling, masterful at choosing ground"): 水性善喜於地，草木之上即流而下，有似於牝，動而下人也, *"water's nature delights in the ground; on grass and trees it runs straight down; it is like the female, moving and putting itself below others."* 牝 (*pìn* — the female) is chapter 6's dark female. The commentator joins the water, the low ground and the female in one sentence, which is the thread `notes/reading.md` calls the mother and her kin.

### Water and the grime

**Water's chapters hold the soil and the shame together.** Chapter 8's water dwells in what the crowd loathes, which Heshang Gong calls the low, the damp, the grimy and the murky. Chapter 78 ends with the sage taking on the state's 垢 (*gòu* — grime). Heshang Gong there: 君能愛國垢濁者，若江海不逆小流, *"a ruler who can love the state's grime and murk is like rivers and seas, which do not refuse the small streams."*

**The silks complicate the second.** Both Mawangdui silks write 訽 in chapter 78, a form of 詬 (*gòu* — reviling, shame), where the received text has 垢. The two words sound the same. The Shuowen builds 詬 on 言 (*yán* — speech) and defines it as 恥 (*chǐ* — shame), and builds 垢 on 土 (*tǔ* — earth) and defines it as 濁 (*zhuó* — murk). In the silks the sage takes on the state's spoken shame, and the grime is the received text's figure for it. `DISCOVERIES.md` §8 holds the question this belongs to.

### What the silks change

**Chapter 8: water is still, where our text says it does not contend.** Mawangdui A reads 水善，利萬物而有靜 (*shuǐ shàn, lì wàn wù ér yǒu jìng* — "water is masterful: it benefits the countless things and has stillness"). Silk B writes 有爭 (*yǒu zhēng*), which its editors read as 有靜 too, since 爭 (*zhēng* — to contend) is the sound element of 靜 (*jìng* — still). Both silks keep the chapter's close, 夫唯不爭 (*fū wéi bù zhēng* — "only because it does not contend"). So in the silks water begins the chapter still, and ends it not contending.

**Chapter 78: water is named twice.** Silk B reads 水之勝剛也，弱之勝強也 (*shuǐ zhī shèng gāng yě, ruò zhī shèng qiáng yě* — "water overcomes the unbending; the yielding overcomes the strong"). Our base reads 弱之勝強，柔之勝剛 (*ruò zhī shèng qiáng, róu zhī shèng gāng* — "the yielding overcomes the strong; the soft overcomes the unbending"). In silk B the soft thing that overcomes the unbending is water itself.

**Chapter 8's opening and its dwelling are the same in substance.** Silk A writes 上善治水 (*shàng shàn zhì shuǐ*), and its editors read 治 (*zhì*) as 似 (*sì* — like); silk B writes 如 (*rú* — like). Both silks write 居 (*jū* — to dwell) for our 處 (*chǔ* — to stay in), which our English already says: *dwells*. Guodian carries neither chapter.

### What is set aside

**"element," "the water element."** The five phases are Han cosmology, and the Shuowen's 北方之行 is where they enter. The book has no 五行 (*wǔ xíng* — five agents), and an English *element* reads that system back into it.

**"liquid," "fluid."** These name a state of matter, the vocabulary of physics. They label water where the line points at it, and the pointing test fails. Water can be drunk, and a liquid is a category.

**"the Water."** A capital turns the image into a principle. Only the Tao keeps one.

### Where *water* stands without 水

**Two lines print *water* for other characters, and both are licensed.** A lock is a claim in both directions, so these are named here.

- **Chapter 15, *"Mixed, like muddy water."*** The character is 濁 (*zhuó* — muddy), built on 水, and the Shuowen names it as a river. English needs a noun for the adjective to describe, and *water* is it. The 清 (*qīng* — clear) entry covers 濁.
- **Chapter 36, *"A fish cannot leave deep water."*** The character is 淵 (*yuān* — a deep pool), which the Shuowen calls 回水也 (*huí shuǐ yě* — "circling water") between two banks. *Deep water* is its English there, and its other chapters are `WORKLIST.md` T2-24.

Neither can be held by a rule: 水 stands in neither chapter, so the checker never looks. This entry and the reader hold it.

### Left open

**Chapter 8's first line: does water not contend, or is it still?** Both silks read 有靜 (*yǒu jìng* — "has stillness"); our base reads 不爭 (*bù zhēng* — "does not contend"). Shalom's call.

**Chapter 78's soft: the soft, or water?** Silk B names water a second time where our base has 柔 (*róu* — soft). Shalom's call.

**Chapter 78's grime: grime, or reviling?** Both silks read 詬 (*gòu* — reviling, shame) where the verse, on our base's 垢, reads *grime*. Shalom's call.

**Why water, and not the Tao.** Wang Bi's answer is that water has a form and the Tao does not. The book never says what else keeps water at *nearly*.
