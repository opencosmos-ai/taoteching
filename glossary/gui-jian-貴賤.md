## 貴 & 賤 (Guì, Jiàn) — Prized & Cheap

*Say "gway," falling, and "jyen," falling. 貴 stands twenty-two times across fifteen chapters; 賤 three times, always beside it. Two words off a price list, and the book uses them to price people, bodies, the Tao, and itself.*

**Recommended: 貴 → "prize" as a verb · "prized" as a state · 賤 → "cheap," and "cheapen" as a verb.** *(Not "noble" and "humble," which moralize a price. Not "precious," which belongs to 貨 (huò — goods)'s forbidden list and makes the book's own prizing sound like the jewels it refuses. Not "honor," which is 尊 (zūn — honor)'s, covered here.)* **One flexion:** *status* in 富貴 (fù guì — wealth and rank) at chapter 9. **Covered:** 尊 → *honor*.

### The graph: two cowries, and a price

> 貴，物不賤也。从貝，臾聲。
> *"貴: a thing that is not cheap. From 貝 (bèi — a cowry shell), with 臾 (yú) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

> 賤，賈少也。从貝，戔聲。
> *"賤: small in price. From 貝 (bèi — a cowry shell), with 戔 (jiān) for the sound."* — Shuowen

**Both characters stand on the cowry, the shell that was money.** The Shuowen defines each by the other and by the market: 貴 is *not cheap*, 賤 is *a low price*. Neither carries a moral. Before they were said of people they were said of goods on a stall, and that is the ledger this book keeps open. When it calls rulers 貴 and their root 賤, it is pricing them, not praising and blaming.

### Twenty-two lines, and what the book prices

**Goods, which the sage refuses to price.** 不貴難得之貨 (*bù guì nán dé zhī huò*) at chapters 3 and 64, *"does not prize rare goods"* (`glossary/huo-貨.md`).

**The body, which the sage prices highest.** Chapter 13's 貴大患若身 (*guì dà huàn ruò shēn* — "prize great trouble as your own body") and 貴以身為天下 (*"one who prizes their body as a way to serve the world"*); chapter 75's 貴生 (*guì shēng* — "to prize life").

**The Tao, and the mother.** 貴食母 (*guì shí mǔ* — "I prize feeding from the mother," 20); 尊道而貴德 (*"honor the Tao and prize integrity,"* 51); 貴此道 (*"prize this Tao,"* 62).

**People, high and low.** 侯王無以貴高 (*"rulers with no way to be prized and high,"* 39); 則我者貴 (*"those who pattern themselves on me are prized,"* 70); 不自貴 (*"does not prize themselves,"* 72).

> *So the prized takes the cheap as its root,*
>
> 故貴以賤為本，
>
> — chapter 39

> *neither prize nor cheapen them.*
> *And so, they become the most prized in the world.*
>
> 不可得而貴，不可得而賤。
> 故為天下貴。
>
> — chapter 56

**Chapter 56 is the hardest line, and it decides the English.** Those who have the dark sameness cannot be made near or far, benefited or harmed, 貴 or 賤, and so they become the world's 貴. The chapter takes the price scale off them and then prices them highest. Wang Bi says why the two halves of the line go together: 可得而貴，則可得而賤也, *"whatever can be prized can be made cheap."* To be beyond pricing is to be beyond cheapening, and the only English that shows the scale is a price word on both sides, and since 2026-11-19 the line has one (Shalom's call).

*Honor* and *debase* break the scale into two vocabularies, one of rank and one of coinage, and they hand the second line's 貴 an English, *honored*, that chapter 62 needs for 尊.

### What the commentators did with it

**Heshang Gong makes chapter 39's root a body of work.** 言必欲尊貴，當以薄賤為本，若禹稷躬稼，舜陶河濵，周公下白屋也, *"it says: if you must want to be honored and prized, take the thin and cheap as your root, as Yu and Ji farmed with their own hands, Shun made pots on the riverbank, and the Duke of Zhou bowed to men in thatched houses."* The cheap is not a humility to be felt. It is the work the founders did.

**At chapter 13 the commentators split, and the English takes Wang Bi.** Wang Bi: 無以易其身，故曰貴也, *"having nothing to trade one's body for: that is why it says 貴."* Prizing is refusing to sell. Heshang Gong reads the character as a different word: 貴，畏也, *"貴 means to fear,"* and the line becomes *fear great trouble as your own body*. The verse's *prize* follows Wang Bi and the price graph; Heshang Gong's fear is recorded here, not lost (`process/principles/divergence-stays-open.md`).

**On chapter 72 Wang Bi says what self-prizing does.** 自貴則物狎厭居生, *"prize yourself, and things crowd in and weary of dwelling and living."* The ruler who prices themselves high presses on the people's houses to be seen doing it (`notes/reading.md`, chapter 72).

### 尊 (zūn — honor)

> 尊，酒器也。从酋，廾以奉之。
> *"尊: a wine vessel. From 酋 (qiú — matured wine), with 廾 (gǒng — two hands) raising it."* — Shuowen, which goes on to name the six 尊 of the *Rites of Zhou* used *"in the rites of sacrifice and of receiving guests."*

**尊 is a wine vessel lifted in both hands to an ancestor or a guest.** The honor is a gesture: raising something up to someone. 貴 is a price; 尊 is a lifting. 尊 stands three times. Chapter 51 sets the two side by side, 萬物莫不尊道而貴德 (*"the countless things all honor the Tao and prize integrity"*) and 道之尊，德之貴 (*"this honoring of the Tao, this prizing of integrity"*). Chapter 62's 美言可以市尊 (*měi yán kě yǐ shì zūn* — "fine words can buy honor") makes it a thing traded at market, the book's own irony on the vessel.

**It lives in this entry because chapter 62 needs it kept apart from 貴.** The same chapter that says fine words *buy honor* (尊) says the Tao is 為天下貴, the world's most prized. Our English gave both *honor*, in one chapter, and the chapter's point, that what can be bought is not what the world truly prizes, went flat. *Revere* at chapter 51 also sat a step toward the hymnal; *honor* is plainer and fits the vessel raised in both hands.

### What is set aside

**"Noble" and "humble."** The moralized pair: a price turned into a rank of soul. Chapter 39 read *"the noble is rooted in the humble"* until 2026-09-05, and the line became a sermon on humility where the Chinese is a ledger. *Humble* is also the virtue-word this edition refuses for 後 (*hòu* — behind) (`glossary/xian-hou-先後.md`).

**"Lowly."** The same moral pricing as *humble*, in a softer coat.

**"Precious."** On 貨's forbidden list, because it makes chapter 3's line circular (*"do not treasure the precious"*). Spent on 貴 at chapters 70, 72 and 75, it also makes the sage's prized few and prized life sound like jewels. Those three chapters hold no 貨, so no check sees it; the ruling holds by argument (see below).

**"Honor," "honored," for 貴.** 尊's word. At chapters 56 and 62 it broke the price scale and collided with 尊 in the same chapter.

**"Value," "valuing."** An abstraction off the same root as *valuable*, and a third verb where *prize* serves. Chapter 51's *value integrity* beside *revere the Tao* made the pair sound like two kinds of regard; the Chinese makes it one regard and one gesture.

**"Debase."** Coinage, but of metal clipped and alloyed: a corruption. 賤 is only a low price, and *cheapen* says so.

**"Esteem."** Polite, abstract, and opinion rather than price.

### What no rule can enforce

***Precious* is ruled out for 貴, and cannot be forbidden yet.** Chapters 70, 72 and 75 print it now, and forbidding it would report all three at once. Once they change, it can join this list.

***Honor* is ruled out for 貴, and can never be forbidden here.** 尊 shares chapters 51 and 62 with 貴 and legitimately prints it. The distinction lives in this entry and in the reader.

*Value*, *revere* and *debase* are forbidden now that chapters 27, 51 and 56 have left them.

***Noble* is ruled out for 貴, and can never be forbidden here.** 君子 (*jūn zǐ* — the noble one) reads *the noble* at chapter 31, which also holds 貴 (貴左, 貴右).

### Left open

**Three lines would change, and each is Shalom's call.** (Chapters 27, 51, 56 and 62 were settled on 2026-11-19.) 70 (*precious* → *prized*); 72 (*hold themselves precious* → *prize themselves*); 75 (*hold life precious* → *prize life*).

**Heshang Gong's fear at chapter 13 is a real reading.** 貴，畏也 makes the line a warning, *fear great trouble as you fear for your body*, and the rest of the chapter, which is about shock at favor and disgrace, can carry it. The verse follows Wang Bi and the price. A reader of the chapter should know the line has two faces.

**The book prices the sage's few (70) by the inference it refuses for goods (3, 64).** Scarcity confers value on understanding and not on jade. `glossary/huo-貨.md` holds this tension, and the English should not resolve it: *prized* at 70 and *does not prize rare goods* at 64 now use one verb, so a reader can see the book doing both.
