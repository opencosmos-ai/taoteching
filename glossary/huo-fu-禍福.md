## 禍 & 福 (Huò, Fú) — Disaster & Fortune

*Say "hwaw," falling, and "foo," rising. 禍 stands four times in three chapters, 福 three times in two, and in chapter 58 they stand in each other's lines. Two altar words, and the book never puts anyone at the altar.*

**Recommended: 禍 → "disaster" · 福 → "fortune," and "good fortune" where it stands without its partner (65).** *(Not "blessing," which brings a blesser the book never names. Not "luck," which makes chance of what the book makes of conduct. Not "misfortune," which names 禍 by 福 and drops the harm. Not "calamity," which is 殃 (yāng — calamity)'s.)* **Covered:** 罪 (*zuì* — crime) → *crime*.

### The graph: two altars, one full and one refused

Both graphs stand on 示 (*shì* — the altar), the radical of everything done toward the spirits, and the Shuowen defines both through them:

> 福，祐也。从示，畐聲。
> *"福: aid. From 示 (shì — the altar), with 畐 (fú) for the sound."* — Shuowen (說文, *Shuōwén* — Xu Shen's dictionary, c. 100 CE)

> 禍，害也，神不福也。从示，咼聲。
> *"禍: harm. The spirits do not grant 福. From 示 (shì — the altar), with 咼 (guō) for the sound."* — Shuowen

祐 (*yòu* — aid) is help from above, and 禍 is defined as its withholding. In the dictionary the pair is a transaction at the altar: the spirits give, or they refuse.

**The sound part of 福 is a full vessel, and it lends itself to wealth.** 畐 (*fú* — to fill) also sounds in 富 (*fù* — wealthy), 備也。一曰厚也 (*"well provided; one says, thick"*). The book's 知足者富 (*zhī zú zhě fù* — "one who knows they have enough is wealthy," ch 33) and its 福 are cousins in the writing. Heshang Gong joins them on ch 33, where the one who knows enough 長保福祿 (*cháng bǎo fú lù* — "keeps their good fortune and stipend long"), `glossary/zhizu-知足.md`.

**The altar is also where the radical's own definition points to the next pair.** 示 is 天垂象，見吉凶，所以示人也 (*"the sky hangs down images and shows the auspicious and the ominous: that is how it shows people"*). 吉 (*jí* — auspicious) and 凶 (*xiōng* — ominous) are the diviner's reading of the sign. 福 and 禍 are what then befalls. The book keeps the two pairs apart, and so does this glossary: 凶 is owed its own entry (`WORKLIST.md` T2-53).

**Where the picture stops.** The altar is in the graph and never in the book. In none of its five lines does a spirit give 福 or withhold it. Each time, the cause is a way of acting, or the pair turning into each other. That is the whole case against *blessing*, and it is made by the lines below rather than by the razor.

### Five lines, and no one hands out either

> *There is no greater disaster than not knowing you have enough,*
> *no greater fault than wanting to get.*
>
> 禍莫大於不知足；
> 咎莫大於欲得。
>
> — chapter 46

> *Disaster is what fortune leans on;*
> *fortune is where disaster hides.*
> *Who knows where it ends? It has no fixed norm —*
>
> 禍兮福之所倚，
> 福兮禍之所伏。
> 孰知其極？其無正。
>
> — chapter 58

> *So to govern a state with cleverness robs the state.*
> *Not to govern it with cleverness is the state's good fortune.*
>
> 故以智治國，國之賊；
> 不以智治國，國之福。
>
> — chapter 65

> *There is no disaster greater than making light of the enemy.*
> *Making light of the enemy nearly costs me my treasures.*
>
> 禍莫大於輕敵，
> 輕敵幾喪吾寶。
>
> — chapter 69

**In every line, 禍 and 福 follow from what someone does.** Not knowing you have enough, making light of an enemy, governing with cleverness or without it. Where no one is acting, at ch 58, they come from each other. The book takes the dictionary's two altar words and empties the altar. The English has to be words that can stand in an empty room: *disaster* and *fortune* name what happens and imply nobody behind it.

**禍 takes a superlative frame twice, and the frame is identical.** 禍莫大於 (*huò mò dà yú* — "no disaster is greater than") opens ch 46 and ch 69 word for word, and `concordance.py --formulas` holds it as one formula. Where the Chinese repeats itself, the English should too (`WORKLIST.md` T3-17).

### Chapter 58 is the hardest line, and two old readers argue it from opposite ends

禍兮福之所倚 (*huò xī fú zhī suǒ yǐ* — "disaster: what fortune leans on") and 福兮禍之所伏 (*fú xī huò zhī suǒ fú* — "fortune: where disaster lies in wait") are one mirror. The two verbs are both bodies:

> 倚，依也。从人，奇聲。
> *"倚: to lean on. From 人 (rén — a person), with 奇 (qí) for the sound."* — Shuowen

> 伏，司也。从人从犬。
> *"伏: to watch (司 (sī), standing for 伺 (sì — to lie in wait)). From 人 (rén — a person) and 犬 (quǎn — a dog)."* — Shuowen

A person leaning, and a dog crouched beside a person, watching. One rests its weight; the other waits. *Leans on* keeps the first exactly, and 倚 holds *lean* in this book for that reason: `glossary/shi-恃.md` gave 恃 (*shì* — to rely on) *rely on* so that *lean* could stay here. *Hides* keeps the second's concealment, which is Heshang Gong's reading, and drops the dog's waiting, which is the graph's.

**Han Fei derives both halves from conduct, and never from luck.** On the first:

> 人有禍，則心畏恐；心畏恐，則行端直；……全壽富貴之謂福，而福本於有禍。
> *"When a person has disaster, the heart fears; when the heart fears, conduct turns straight… Being whole, long-lived, rich and honored is what is called 福, and 福 is rooted in having had disaster."* — Han Fei, *Jie Lao* (解老, *Jiě Lǎo* — "Explaining Laozi")

On the second, the same chain runs downhill: 人有福，則富貴至；……驕心生 (*"when a person has 福, wealth and rank arrive… and an arrogant heart is born"*), and it ends in 大禍 (*dà huò* — "great disaster"), 而禍本生於有福 (*"and disaster is born from having had 福"*). **His 福 is a list of conditions you could point at, whole, long-lived, rich, honored**, and the passage from one to the other runs through the heart's fear or its arrogance.

**Heshang Gong reads the first verb as cause, and the second as concealment:**

> 倚，因也。夫禍因福而生，人遭禍而能悔過責己，修善行道，則禍去而福來。
> *"倚 means 'arises from.' Disaster arises from fortune; [yet] a person who meets disaster and can regret their errors, take themselves to task, practise the good and walk the Tao, sees disaster go and fortune come."* — Heshang Gong, ch 58

> 禍伏匿於福中，人得福而為驕恣，則福去禍來。
> *"Disaster lies hidden within fortune: a person who gains fortune and turns arrogant and self-indulgent sees fortune go and disaster come."* — Heshang Gong, ch 58

Both commentators make the turn moral, a matter of fear or pride. **The chapter does not.** Its next line is 孰知其極 (*shú zhī qí jí* — "who knows where it ends?"), and Heshang Gong himself answers it with 禍福更相生 (*"disaster and fortune give birth to each other in turn"*), with no person in the sentence at all (`glossary/ji-極.md`). So the English keeps the pair turning without assigning the turn to virtue, and without assigning it to luck either.

### Chapter 65: the silks wrote integrity where the base writes fortune

Both Mawangdui silks read 國之德 (*guó zhī dé* — "the state's integrity") for 國之福 (*guó zhī fú* — "the state's fortune"), and write 知 (*zhī* — to know) for 治 (*zhì* — to govern) in the same line (`sources/variants.yaml`, ch 65). Our base keeps 福, and both commentators read it. Heshang Gong pays it out in things a state could have: 民守正直…上下相親，君臣同力，故為國之福也 (*"the people keep to the straight… high and low are close, ruler and minister pull together: so it is the state's fortune"*).

**The fork says something about the word.** A scribe could put 德 (*dé* — integrity) where 福 stood and lose no sense, because in this line the state's fortune *is* what its integrity yields. That is Han Fei's chain again, 福 as the outcome of conduct. *Good fortune* here faces 賊 (*zéi* — a robber) in the line before, *"robs the state,"* and the pair is the state's loss and the state's gain.

### 罪 (zuì — crime)

*Say "dzway," falling. Once in the received text, in chapter 62's last question.*

**The graph is a fish net, standing in for a graph the First Emperor would not see.** The Shuowen gives 罪 its own meaning first, and its legal one only by substitution:

> 罪，捕魚竹网。从网、非。秦以罪爲辠字。
> *"罪: a bamboo net for catching fish. From 网 (wǎng — a net) and 非 (fēi — wrong, set against). Qin used 罪 for the graph 辠."* — Shuowen

> 辠，犯法也。从辛从自，言辠人蹙鼻苦辛之憂。秦以辠似皇字，改爲罪。
> *"辠: to break the law. From 辛 (xīn — bitter, a blade) and 自 (zì — the nose): the offender wrinkling the nose in bitter grief. Because 辠 looked like 皇 (huáng — emperor), Qin changed it to 罪."* — Shuowen, read in Wikisource's transcription of the same Sibu Congkan facsimile

**The word is a legal one, from its first graph to its last.** 辠 is a breach of the law, and the net that replaced it catches what it is set against. Nothing in either graph is an offense against a god. That is why 罪 is on the overlay watchlist: *sin* puts a deity where the Shuowen puts a statute.

**The line, 有罪以免 (*yǒu zuì yǐ miǎn* — "having a crime, by it to be spared"), is about jeopardy, not guilt.** Heshang Gong gives the situation in full:

> 有罪謂遭亂世闇君妄行刑誅，修道則可以解死免於衆耶也。
> *"'Having a crime' means meeting a chaotic age and a benighted ruler who inflicts punishments and executions at random; cultivating the Tao, one can be released from death and escape from the crowd."* — Heshang Gong, ch 62

And Wang Bi makes 以 (*yǐ* — by it) the hinge, the Tao as the means: 以求則得求，以免則得免 (*yǐ qiú zé dé qiú, yǐ miǎn zé dé miǎn* — "use it to seek and you get what you seek; use it to escape and you escape"). **Han Fei uses the same two words in the same sense, on ch 46:** 胥靡有免，死罪時活 (*xū mí yǒu miǎn, sǐ zuì shí huó* — "convicts are sometimes released; those condemned to death sometimes live"). 罪 is the charge you stand under, and 免 (*miǎn*) is release from its penalty. Whether the crime was yours, the line does not say, and Heshang Gong's random punishments suggest it need not have been.

**The chapter's English depends on keeping that sense.** Ch 62 opens on 奧 (*ào* — the sanctuary), and `notes/translation.md` → *Ch 62 · 奧* argues *sanctuary* precisely because English keeps its older legal sense: the right of the pursued not to be seized. The chapter ends where its first word pointed. Read 罪 as wrongdoing confessed and forgiven, and 善 / 不善 (*shàn / bù shàn* — masterful / unmasterful) slides into *good* and *bad*, and the chapter becomes the doctrine of grace that `glossary/shan-善.md` refuses.

**The older witnesses hold 罪 a second time, in a clause our base lacks.** Mawangdui silk A, Fu Yi and Heshang Gong open ch 46's couplet with 罪莫大於可欲 (*zuì mò dà yú kě yù* — "no crime is greater than the wantable"); Heshang Gong glosses it 好淫色也 (*"loving lewd beauty"*). Our base keeps the two received clauses (`sources/variants.yaml`, ch 46; `notes/manuscript.md`). Where the clause stands, the three nouns climb 罪 · 禍 · 咎 (*crime · disaster · fault*), and *crime* there is again a word for an act, not a state of the soul.

**Why 罪 lives here.** It stands in one line, beside no partner in the received text, and in the older witnesses its only other line is the head of 禍's own sequence. It is the third noun of the calamity clauses, and the one the missionaries turned into *sin*.

### What is set aside

**"Blessing," "blessed," for 福.** It needs a blesser. The Shuowen's 祐 (*yòu* — aid) has one, a spirit at the altar, and the book never does: its 福 comes from not governing with cleverness, or from the turn of disaster. *Blessing* fails the hymn test outright, and at ch 65 it would make the state's good fortune a gift from above for a policy of restraint. The chapter's own Source table glosses the line *"a blessing to the state,"* which is the watchlist's word, pre-lock.

**"Luck," "lucky," for 福.** It makes 福 a matter of chance, and Han Fei and Heshang Gong derive it every time from conduct. The I Ching's entry for 吉 (*jí* — auspicious) refused *lucky* on the same ground, citing the *Xici*'s claim that the auspicious and the ominous arise from conduct (`iching/glossary/吉-ji.md`).

**"Happiness," for 福.** The modern dictionaries' gloss, and a feeling. Han Fei's 福 is a condition you could count: whole, long-lived, rich, honored. *Fortune* keeps it outside the heart, where the book keeps it.

**"Misfortune," for 禍.** It names 禍 by 福, as *un-fortune*, and so makes the pair a lack where the Shuowen makes 禍 a harm, 害也 (*hài yě* — "harm"). Ch 58's mirror needs two things, each fully present, each leaning on the other. A negative of the one cannot lean.

**"Calamity," for 禍.** 殃 (*yāng* — calamity)'s, at ch 52, on the death radical. `glossary/jiu-咎.md` already forbids it to 咎 (*jiù* — fault) for that reason.

**"Woe," for 禍.** King James English: *woe unto*. It fails the hymn test, and it is a feeling where 禍 is an event.

**"Sin," "transgression," "guilt," for 罪.** *Sin* is the watchlist's word, and it puts an offended god where the Shuowen puts a law. *Transgression* is the same overlay in Latin. *Guilt* settles what the line leaves open: Heshang Gong's 罪 falls at random under a benighted ruler, and it need not be earned.

### Working register

| Ch | Line | English | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 46 | 禍莫大於不知足 | *There is no greater disaster than not knowing you have enough* | the formula; 禍 against 咎's *fault* in the next line |
| 58 | 禍兮福之所倚 | *Disaster is what fortune leans on* | the mirror's first half; 倚 holds *lean* |
| 58 | 福兮禍之所伏 | *fortune is where disaster hides* | the mirror's second half |
| 65 | 國之福 | *the state's good fortune* | alone, *the state's fortune* reads as its treasury: *good* is supplied so the English keeps what befalls and loses the money |
| 69 | 禍莫大於輕敵 | *There is no disaster greater than making light of the enemy* | the formula again |
| 62 | 有罪以免 | 罪 → *crime* | a charge one stands under, and release from it |

*Fortune* at 58 needs no *good*: *disaster* beside it settles which sense of the English word is meant. At 65 nothing does, so the line takes *good fortune*. Both are the one rendering, and `concordance.py --english "fortune"` reads both as 福's.

### What no rule can enforce

***Disaster* is ruled 禍's alone, and this entry cannot hold that.** 凶 (*xiōng* — ominous) wears it at ch 16 and ch 30, and neither chapter holds 禍 or 福, so a `forbidden:` entry here never sees them. 凶's own entry can: none of its chapters (16, 30, 31) holds 禍, so *disaster* can go on 凶's `forbidden:` list without barring 禍 anywhere. Until then the reader holds it (`WORKLIST.md` T2-53).

***Sin* is ruled out for 罪, and nothing here can forbid it.** A `covers:` character carries a rendering and no `forbidden:` list, and ch 62, 罪's only chapter, holds neither 禍 nor 福, so the gate this entry opens never fires there. (*Sin* as a bare string would also catch *since* and *single*.)

### Left open

**Chapter 62's line still says *commit a crime*.** The English makes the reader the offender, where the line, its notes, and both commentators read a charge one stands under. `process/back-translation-audit.md` (finding 2) and `WORKLIST.md` R6 owe the repair; *seek, and you find* is settled and kept (`chapters/062.md` → Notes).

**Chapter 58's *hides* keeps Heshang Gong's concealment and drops the Shuowen's dog, watching.** *Lies in wait* would make the mirror two postures, a body leaning and a body crouched. It costs the line its brevity.

**Chapter 69 departs from chapter 46 in a formula the Chinese repeats word for word.** Ch 46 changed on 2026-11-19 to *"There is no greater disaster than…"*; ch 69 still reads *"There is no disaster greater than…"* (`WORKLIST.md` T3-17).

**The English *fortune* carries a goddess the Chinese never had.** *Fortuna* turned her wheel in Rome; the word kept her wheel and lost her name. It is the lightest personification English offers for 福, lighter than *blessing* by the whole width of a church, but it is not none. The graph has a full jar at an altar, and the book has neither.

**The oldest forms are unchecked.** The oracle-bone ancestors of both graphs have not been read against a palaeographic dictionary here. One published discussion reports that the oracle-bone graph of cracks in a shoulder blade, long read as 禍, is read by others as 繇 (*yáo* — a divination text) or 咎 (*jiù* — fault). If so, the calamity family begins in one graph. Confirming it belongs to the I Ching side as much as this one, and `WORKLIST.md` T2-53 carries it to 凶 (xiōng — ominous)'s entry.
