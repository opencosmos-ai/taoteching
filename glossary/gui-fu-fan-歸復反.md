## 歸 · 復 · 反 (Guī · Fù · Fǎn) — Return

*Say "gway," level; "foo," falling; "fahn," dipping and rising. 歸 (guī) stands in eleven lines across eight chapters, 復 (fù) in fourteen across eight, 反 (fǎn) in four across four. Three characters for one movement, each drawing a different part of it: the arrival, the going back, the turning over.*

**Locked: 歸 → "return"; 復 → "return," and "go back" where it takes a place; the doubled verb 復歸 → "return," one verb; 反 → "returning."** *(Not "returns again," which counts the doubled verb twice and says "a second time." Not "go home": the Chinese prints no house. Not "revert" or "reversion," a lawyer's and a biologist's words. Not "the Return," whose capital makes a doctrine.)* *(Shalom's calls, 2026-11-01: 歸 keeps "return," and 復歸 reads as one verb. 反 was fixed as "returning" at chapters 25 and 40 on 2026-09-06.)*

*根 (gēn — root), the place chapter 16 returns to, is covered here.*

### The graph: a bride's feet, a step retraced, a hand turning something over

> 歸，女嫁也。从止，从婦省，𠂤聲。
> *"歸 (guī): a woman marrying. From 止 (zhǐ — a foot), from an abbreviated 婦 (fù — wife), with 𠂤 (duī) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi* (the Han dictionary)

> 復，往來也。从彳复聲。
> *"復 (fù): going and coming back. From 彳 (chì — a step), with 复 (fù) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi*

> 反，覆也。从又，厂反形。
> *"反 (fǎn): to overturn. From 又 (yòu — a hand), with the shape of 厂 (hǎn — a cliff) turned around."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi*

**Three pictures, one movement.** 復 (fù) is a step going back along the way it came. 歸 (guī) is a woman's feet arriving at the house she will live in. 反 (fǎn) is a hand turning something over. Read together, they are the turn, the going back and the coming to rest.

**歸 also names an ending.** The Shuowen defines the dead through it: 鬼，人所歸為鬼 (*guǐ, rén suǒ guī wéi guǐ* — "鬼 (guǐ — the dead): what a person returns to is called 鬼"). So the word that arrives also carries a life's close, and chapter 16, where everything returns to the root and the root is stillness, means it.

**Where the picture stops.** The bride in 歸 (guī) comes from a marriage custom in which a woman left her family for her husband's. The book never uses the word for marriage, and takes it for what everything does: things return to the root, the countless things return to the Tao. In the graph she is the one who moves, but the English carries none of the custom, and no line here says where anyone belongs.

### 復歸: a doubled verb, and one English verb for it

> *Things come to their fullest,*
> *Each returns to its root.*
> *Returning to the root is called stillness.*
>
> 夫物芸芸，
> 各復歸其根。
> 歸根曰靜，
>
> — chapter 16

**復歸 (fù guī) is two verbs, side by side, for one motion with two parts.** 復 (fù) is the going back, the turn of the journey; 歸 (guī) is the arrival where the thing belongs. Classical Chinese often sets two verbs of motion together like this, where English uses one verb, sometimes with a particle: *go back*, *come home*. The compound stands in seven lines: 14, 16, three times in 28, and 52.

**It is not "return return," and it is not "return again."** 復 (fù) can be an adverb elsewhere, *again*, and an English that takes it so writes *"returns again."* But *again* says *a second time*, a repetition, and the commentators read the phrase as a going **back to the beginning**:

> 各返其所始也。
> *"Each goes back to where it began."* — Wang Bi, on chapter 16

> 言萬物無不枯落，各復反其根而更生也。
> *"It says the countless things all wither and fall; each goes back to its root, and lives anew."* — Heshang Gong, on chapter 16

Wang Bi glosses the two verbs with one, 返 (fǎn — go back). Heshang Gong keeps two, and adds the cycle: what goes back down to the root comes up again. Neither reads a second return.

**English "return" is built the way 復歸 is.** It is *re-* (back) and *turn*: a turning back that arrives. One English word holds both beats, so nothing the Chinese says is lost and nothing it doesn't say is added. **What the English drops is the sound of the doubling.** The Chinese strikes twice, and the English once. Spelling both beats out, as in *"each goes back, returns to its root,"* was weighed and declined (Shalom's call, 2026-11-01): faithful, but it breaks the line's music, and in chapter 28's triple it would break it three times.

### Chapter 16 uses every part of the movement in five lines

> *Go totally empty.*
> *Hold steadily to stillness.*
> *The countless things arise together,*
> *Relying upon this, I observe their return.*
>
> 致虛極，
> 守靜篤。
> 萬物並作，
> 吾以觀復。
>
> — chapter 16

The chapter names the whole cycle with 復 (fù) alone: *"I observe their return."* Wang Bi glosses it 以虛靜觀其反復 (*yǐ xū jìng guān qí fǎn fù* — "with emptiness and stillness I watch their going back and coming round"), and then, in his own words, 萬物雖並動作，卒復歸於虛靜 (*wàn wù suī bìng dòng zuò, zú fù guī yú xū jìng* — "though the countless things all stir and act, in the end they return to the empty and still"). Then the chapter's verse moves through the parts in order: 復歸其根, the doubled verb, the going back that arrives; 歸根, the arrival alone, *"returning to the root"*; and 復命, *"returning to what is given"* (`glossary/fuming-復命.md`).

So one English verb runs the length of a chapter in which the Chinese uses three forms. That is a cost, and the reason the distinctions live here rather than in the verse: the motion in chapter 16 is one motion, and the English keeps it whole.

### 歸 alone: arriving, and where a thing comes to rest

| Line | English | What 歸 does |
|---|---|---|
| 歸根曰靜 (16) | *Returning to the root is called stillness.* | arrives at the root |
| 若無所歸 (20) | *as if with nowhere to return* | has no place to arrive |
| 萬物歸焉 (34) | *The countless things return to it.* | the countless things come to the Tao |
| 德交歸焉 (60) | *their integrity converges and returns* | two integrities arrive together |
| 誠全而歸之 (22) | *Return to them.* | see *Left open* |

**The sense of arriving where one belongs is strongest in chapter 20.** The speaker is *"listless, as if with nowhere to return,"* and the commentators hear a homeless person. Wang Bi: 若無所宅 (*ruò wú suǒ zhái* — "as if having no dwelling"). Heshang Gong: 我乘乘如窮鄙，無所歸就 (*wǒ chéng chéng rú qióng bǐ, wú suǒ guī jiù* — "I am adrift, like the poor and lowly, with nowhere to go to and settle"). The verse keeps *return*, because the line prints no dwelling: the commentators' house is their reading of 歸 (guī), and a reading is not a rendering (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`). A reader of this entry can carry the dwelling back into the line.

**In chapter 34, 歸 is coming to someone.** 萬物歸焉而不為主 (*wàn wù guī yān ér bù wéi zhǔ* — "the countless things return to it, and it does not act as their master"). Heshang Gong: 萬物皆歸道受氣 (*wàn wù jiē guī dào shòu qì* — "the countless things all return to the Tao and receive breath"). It is the word a people uses for turning to a ruler they trust, and the chapter's point is that the Tao takes no rule from it.

### 復 alone: going back

| Line | English | What 復 does |
|---|---|---|
| 吾以觀復 (16) | *I observe their return* | names the whole cycle, as a noun |
| 復命 (16) | *returning to what is given* | `glossary/fuming-復命.md` |
| 民復孝慈 (19) | *the people return to devotion and tenderness* | goes back to a state |
| 復守其母 (52) | *return and hold fast to our mother* | goes back, then holds |
| 使民復結繩而用之 (80) | *Let the people go back to keeping their records in knotted cord* | goes back to a practice: *go back* |
| 復眾人之所過 (64) | *brings the crowd back from where they have gone too far* | made causative: *bring back* |
| 正復為奇 (58) | *The straight turns crooked* | the adverb, *in turn*, carried by *turns* |

**Chapter 58 is the one line where 復 (fù) is plainly the adverb.** 正復為奇，善復為妖 (*zhèng fù wéi qí, shàn fù wéi yāo* — "the straight in turn becomes crooked, the good in turn becomes monstrous"). The English carries it in the verb, *"turns crooked,"* which is where *in turn* belongs. Elsewhere, 復 (fù) is the going back, and the step in its graph is doing the work.

### 反: the turning over

| Line | English | What 反 does |
|---|---|---|
| 遠曰反 (25) | *going far means returning* | the far point turns back |
| 反者道之動 (40) | *Returning is the Tao's movement;* | the Tao's own motion |
| 與物反矣 (65) | *and turns back together with things* | turns back, alongside |
| 正言若反 (78) | *Words on the mark seem like their opposite.* | a word turned over: *opposite* |

**The hand in the graph is turning something over, and both senses follow from it.** Turned around, a thing goes back the way it came: *returning* (25, 40, 65). Turned over, a thing shows its other side: *opposite* (78). The English follows the grammar. Chapter 25 shows the first in a chain: 大曰逝，逝曰遠，遠曰反 (*dà yuē shì, shì yuē yuǎn, yuǎn yuē fǎn* — "great means departing, departing means going far, going far means returning"). The Tao goes out until it comes round.

### 根 (gēn — root)

> 根，木株也。从木艮聲。
> *"根 (gēn): the stump of a tree. From 木 (mù — tree), with 艮 (gèn) as its sound."* — the *Shuowen Jiezi*

**The root is a stump in the ground, something you could stand on.** It stands in five lines across four chapters: the dark female's gate is *"the root of sky and earth"* (6); things *"return to the root"* (16, twice); *"The heavy is the root of the light"* (26); *"the Tao of deep roots, firm base"* (59). It is covered here because chapter 16 makes it the place the whole movement arrives, and because its one temptation is the same as the family's: to turn something you could point at into an abstraction.

**"The source" is set aside for it.** A source is where water starts, and a principle in English. The root is a stump, the low dark part of a tree that the visible part comes back down to.

### What is set aside

**"Returns again."** It reads 復 (fù) as the adverb and says *a second time*, where both commentators read the doubled verb as going back to the beginning. One English verb, *return*, already carries the turn and the arrival.

**"Go home," "home."** The belonging sense of 歸 (guī) is real: the bride arriving, Wang Bi's *dwelling* at chapter 20. But the Chinese prints no house, and *home* would put one in a line that has only a stump in the ground (Shalom's call, 2026-11-01). The sense stays in this entry, where a reader can find it. *Going home* is also English shorthand for dying in a Christian register, and the line would carry that too.

**"Revert," "reversion."** A legal term for property coming back to an owner, and a biologist's term for a throwback. Both are the wrong kind of return: one a transaction, one a regression.

**"The Return," capitalized.** The capital makes a doctrine of a movement (`CLAUDE.md` standing rule 3).

**"Eternal return."** Nietzsche's phrase, with its whole argument attached. Chapter 16's return is not a recurrence of the same events. It is things going back down to where they began.

**"Cycle."** A label for what the chapter performs. The verse shows things rising, ripening and going back down; naming it a cycle replaces the watching with a concept (`process/principles/perform-dont-label.md`).

**"The source," for 根 (gēn — root).** Above.

### Working register

- **歸 (guī)** → *return*, in every line, as a verb (*"returns to it"*) or a gerund (*"Returning to the root"*).
- **復歸 (fù guī)** → *return*, one verb, in all seven lines: 14, 16, 28 ×3, 52.
- **復 (fù)** → *return* as a verb or noun; *go back* where it goes back to a practice (80); *bring back* where it is causative (64); carried by *turns* where it is the adverb *in turn* (58).
- **反 (fǎn)** → *returning* (25, 40); *turns back* (65); *opposite* (78), where the thing turned over shows its other side.
- **根 (gēn)** → *root*.

### What no rule can enforce

**The forbidden list holds only in 歸 (guī)'s chapters.** The build reads a pairing's chapters from its first character, so *home*, *revert*, *the Return* and the rest are held in chapters 14, 16, 20, 22, 28, 34, 52 and 60. In chapters where only 復 (fù) or 反 (fǎn) stands, 19, 25, 40, 58, 64, 65, 78 and 80, those rulings are held by this entry and by the reader. *Home* could not be forbidden there in any case: chapter 72 prints *homes* for another character.

**"Again" cannot be forbidden.** Other characters print it legitimately, as 又 (yòu — again) does in chapter 1's *"Dark, and dark again."* The ruling against *"returns again"* for 復歸 (fù guī) is held here.

### Left open

- **反 (fǎn) at chapter 40 may be reversal as well as return.** 反者道之動 (*fǎn zhě dào zhī dòng*) reads *"Returning is the Tao's movement,"* and the graph's turning over supports a second reading: things turn into their opposites, as chapter 58 says, *"The straight turns crooked."* The English chose *returning* on 2026-09-06, and chapter 25's *"going far means returning"* supports it. The other reading is real, and the line holds both.
- **Chapter 22's 誠全而歸之 (*chéng quán ér guī zhī*)** reads *"Return to them,"* an instruction to the reader. The Chinese may say that wholeness truly *returns to* the one who bends: *"truly whole, and it returns to them."* `glossary/quan-全.md` has the argument; the line has not been revisited with this entry.
- **Heshang Gong's cycle and Wang Bi's beginning are two readings of chapter 16.** In Heshang Gong, things go back to the root *and live anew*; in Wang Bi, they go back to where they began. The verse's *return* holds both, and the entry leaves them both standing.
- **The witnesses for the family's lines are unrecorded** in `sources/variants.yaml`, beyond chapter 16's 觀其復 (*guān qí fù* — "observe their return") and chapter 19's 民復孝慈 (*mín fù xiào cí* — "the people return to devotion and tenderness"). The Guodian bamboo carries chapter 16's first couplet only, so the silks are the oldest witnesses for its return.
