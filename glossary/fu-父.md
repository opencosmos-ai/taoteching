## 父 · 甫 (Fù · Fǔ) — The Father

*Say "foo," falling, for 父; "foo," dipping and then lifting, for 甫. One word in two spellings, three appearances across two chapters. The book's father: named far less often than its mother, and named all the same.*

**Locked: 父 (*fù*) and 甫 (*fǔ*) → "father," in every line.** *(Not "origin," which is 始's (shǐ — beginning). Not "root," which is 根's (gēn — root). Shalom's call, 2026-10-26: ch 42 first, then ch 21, the same day.)*

### The graph: a hand that holds a tool, and a seedling in a field

**父 is a hand holding something up.** The oracle-bone graph is 又 (*yòu* — a right hand) gripping a short stick or a stone axe. Many paleographers read the object as the axe, and 斧 (*fǔ* — axe) still carries 父 inside it. The Shuowen reads it as a staff, and defines the word by what the one holding it does:

> 父，矩也。家長率教者。从又舉杖。
> *"父: the carpenter's square. The head of the family, who leads and teaches. From 又 (yòu — a hand) raising 杖 (zhàng — a staff)."*

矩 (*jǔ* — the carpenter's square) is the measure a thing is made true by. The Shuowen's father is the one who sets the measure and **teaches**, which is worth holding beside ch 42, where the father is the father of learning. The picture stops at the household: the Shuowen is a Han dictionary, about 100 CE, and its family head with a staff is a Han reading. Neither chapter puts the father in a house.

**甫 began as a seedling in a field.** The oracle-bone graph is a sprout, 屮 (*chè* — a sprout), over 田 (*tián* — a field): the first form of 圃 (*pǔ* — a garden). In the bronzes the sprout was rewritten as 父 to give the sound, and the field wore down into 用 (*yòng* — to use). The Shuowen, which saw only that later shape, took it apart accordingly:

> 甫，男子美稱也。从用、父，父亦聲。
> *"甫: a fine name for a man. From 用 (yòng — use) and 父 (fù — father), and 父 also gives the sound."*

A few scholars doubt that the seedling graph and 甫 are one character, but most read it so. **Where this picture stops matters more than the picture.** The seedling is the history of a spelling. By the time the book was written, 甫 was the respectful word for a man, the one in names like Zhong Shanfu of the *Odes*. The seedling is lovely, and it is not what the word in ch 21 says.

### Three lines, and in each a father is where something starts

> *From ancient times until today, the Tao's name has never been erased.*
> *In this name, I observe the father of the many.*
> *How do I know the father of the many is so? By this.*
>
> 自古及今，其名不去，
> 以閱眾甫。
> 吾何以知眾甫之狀哉？以此。
>
> — chapter 21

> *What others teach, I also teach:*
> *The strong and rigid do not die a natural death.*
> *I take this as the father of learning.*
>
> 人之所教，我亦教之。
> 強梁者不得其死，
> 吾將以為教父。
>
> — chapter 42

**In both chapters the father is the one a thing starts from, and in neither is he a man in a house.** In ch 21 he is the father of the many, 眾 (*zhòng* — the many) used of things, not people, and you observe him through the Tao's name. In ch 42 he is the father of learning: the first lesson, the one the rest of learning comes from. The line's Chinese above reads 教父 (*jiào fù* — "the father of teaching"), and the English follows the Mawangdui silk's 學父 (*xué fù* — "the father of learning"), the older reading (`notes/manuscript.md` → Ch 42). Ch 21's 狀 (*zhuàng* — shape) is likewise rendered by the witnesses' 然 (*rán* — so).

### One word, written two ways

**The oldest witnesses write 父 in ch 21.** Where our base text, Wang Bi's, writes 眾甫 (*zhòng fǔ*), both Mawangdui silks write 眾父 (*zhòng fù* — "the father of the many"). Guodian, the oldest witness of all, holds neither chapter. So the word in every early copy that has these lines is 父, and 甫 is the received spelling (`sources/variants.yaml`).

**The two sounded almost alike, and the scribes traded them.** The Shuowen itself says 父 gives 甫 its sound. The honorific is one word in two spellings even for a single man. The *Zuo Commentary* mourns Confucius as 尼父 (*Ní fù* — "Father Ni"). Zheng Xuan, the Han commentator, writes the same title with the other character in his note on the capping rite of the *Yili*: 甫，是丈夫之美稱。孔子為尼甫 — *"甫 is a fine name for a grown man. Confucius was Ni-fu,"* written 尼甫. A reader of the time heard one word, whichever character the scribe chose.

**And the commentators read them as one.** Heshang Gong glosses the two chapters in the same three words, below. Nothing in any witness or any commentary treats 甫 and 父 as two words with two senses.

### What the commentators did with it

Both commentators turn the father into a *beginning*. That is evidence for what the word means, and it is where *origin* and *root* came from.

**Heshang Gong, ch 21:** 閱，禀也。甫，始也。言道禀與萬物始生，從道受氣 — *"閱 (yuè) means to bestow. 甫 (fǔ) means beginning. It says the Tao bestows on the countless things their first life, and they receive 氣 (qì — breath) from the Tao."*

**Heshang Gong, ch 42:** 父，始也。老子以強梁之人為教戒之始也 — *"父 (fù) means beginning. Laozi takes the strong and rigid person as the beginning of teaching and warning."*

**Wang Bi, ch 21:** 衆甫，物之始也。以無名說萬物始也 — *"眾甫 (zhòng fǔ) is the beginning of things. It is by the nameless that the beginning of the countless things is explained."*

**Wang Bi, ch 42:** 故得其違教之徒，適可以為教父也 — *"so a follower who goes against the teaching can serve exactly as the father of teaching."* The one who refuses the lesson becomes the lesson.

**Every one of these glosses 父 with 始 (*shǐ* — beginning), and a gloss says what a word means; it is not the word.** English *the father of*, as in *the father of medicine* or *the father of history*, already names the one a thing began from. Heshang Gong's sense stays inside the English, and the word stays the word Laozi wrote (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`).

### The first draft said *origin* and *root*, and the father was restored

The first version of this translation rendered 甫 as *"the origin of the many"* in ch 21 and 父 as *"the root of learning"* in ch 42; both were formerly the English, taken from the commentators' 始. On 2026-10-26 Shalom restored the father, for two reasons.

**The first is Laozi's own wording.** The word is *father* in every witness that holds these lines. *Origin* and *root* were a commentator's sense put in place of the author's word, and the English *the father of* keeps the sense without losing the word.

**The second is that a rule written to protect the feminine must not be used to erase the masculine.** This edition's first standing rule holds that no one is subordinated: the female is never put under, and the male is never the default. It refuses the male where the text merely *assumes* him, as when the sage becomes *he*, or a rank word presumes a man. It does not reach a father the text names on purpose, and since this restoration the rule says so in its own words: its aim is the end of all subordination, not the masculine put under the feminine. Cooling 父 into *origin* would have let that rule lie over the text like an overlay, removing a masculine presence that is authentically there. That is the same flinch the mother's entry refuses when it keeps 母 (*mǔ* — mother) from becoming *the Source*. An edition that kept the mother a mother and turned the father into a root would build its feminine center by subtraction. **The feminine center holds because the mother is named, not because the father is unnamed.**

The book itself keeps both. Ch 42, the chapter that names the father of learning, opens with the countless things that 負陰而抱陽 (*fù yīn ér bào yáng* — "carry yin on their backs and embrace yang"). Ch 28 says 知其雄，守其雌 (*zhī qí xióng, shǒu qí cí* — "know the rooster, hold to the hen"): the masculine is to be known, not erased. The male among animals, 牡 (*mǔ* — the male), whom the Shuowen defines as 畜父 (*chù fù* — "the father among livestock"), stands beside the female in chapters 55 and 61 (`glossary/pin-牝.md`).

### The mother and the father, both reached through a name

> *The nameless is the beginning of sky and earth.*
> *The named is the mother of the countless things.*
>
> 無名，天地之始；
> 有名，萬物之母。
>
> — chapter 1

**Ch 1 reaches the mother through naming, and ch 21 reaches the father through a name.** *The named is the mother of the countless things* in ch 1; in ch 21, *in this name, I observe the father of the many*. And ch 52 calls the beginning a mother outright: 天下有始，以為天下母 (*tiān xià yǒu shǐ, yǐ wéi tiān xià mǔ* — "the world has an origin, which serves as the mother of the world"). Heshang Gong calls the father 始 (*shǐ* — beginning) as well. In the book's own terms, the beginning of things is both: the mother, often and warmly, and the father, twice.

The book does not say whether these are one beginning seen from two sides or two. It names the mother far more often: `python3 tools/concordance.py 母` prints every line.

### What is set aside

- **"origin"**, the English of ch 21 until 2026-10-26. It is spoken for: ch 52's *"The world has an origin"* renders 始 (*shǐ* — beginning), so a reader comparing the chapters would take 甫 for 始, which is Heshang Gong's gloss and not the text. It is also a word with no body, the father's version of *the Source* (`process/principles/already-spoken-for.md`).
- **"root"**, the English of ch 42 until 2026-10-26. It is spoken for by 根 (*gēn* — root), the book's root of the plant, in chapters 6, 16, 26 and 59, and by 本 (*běn* — root, trunk) in ch 39. It turns a person into a plant, and it was a gloss.
- **"the Source," "the progenitor," "the first cause."** Each cools the father into an abstraction, the same flinch the mother's entry refuses. *First cause* is also Aristotle's and the schoolmen's, a theology the book never touches.
- **"patriarch."** It adds rule: a father who governs a household. The Shuowen's family head leads and teaches, but neither chapter gives the father any power over anyone (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`).
- **"forefather," "ancestor."** They put him in the past and at the ancestral altar. That is 祖 (*zǔ* — ancestor), a different word. The father of ch 21 is observed *today*, through a name that has never been erased.
- **"the Father."** A capital would make him the God of the creeds, and fail the hymn test outright. He is lowercase, like the mother (`CLAUDE.md` standing rule 3).
- **"teacher"** for ch 42's 學父. It drops the father, and it hands the line to Wang Bi's reading, in which the stubborn are the model, over the plain one, in which the lesson is the first one.

### Working register

| Line | Our English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ch 21 · 以閱眾甫 | *I observe the father of the many* | 眾 (*zhòng*) of things, so *the many* (`glossary/zhong-眾.md`) |
| ch 21 · 吾何以知眾甫之狀哉 | *How do I know the father of the many is so?* | the repetition kept: one father, said twice |
| ch 42 · 吾將以為教父 | *I take this as the father of learning* | the silk's 學父 (*xué fù*); no possessive, since 吾 (*wú* — I) only adopts it |

**The phrase is always *the father of*.** The book never sets 父 alone as a subject; it is always someone's or something's father. Keep the genitive in the English, as the mother keeps hers.

**Outside the verse, 父母 (*fù mǔ*) is "father and mother,"** never *parents*, wherever a commentary or the Shuowen pairs them (`CHANGELOG.md`, 2026-10-26).

### Left open

**Does *father* bring a God the Father into ch 21?** It is the hazard of the word. Ch 21 is the book's haziest chapter, only vague, only elusive, deep and dim, and an English reader may hear *the father of the many* as the creeds' Father of all. What resists that hearing is in the line: the father is never addressed, never praised, never a subject. He is *observed*, through a name, and the chapter answers how it knows him with two words, *by this*. Whether lowercase and the mother beside him are enough is a question for readers, and the reading notes owe it a thread (`notes/reading.md`).

**Why the received text wrote 甫.** The base text keeps 甫, so its Source table, and the Character Atlas built from it, show the honorific rather than the father. A reader of the Chinese meets the father only through this entry and the chapter note. Whether the received editors chose 甫 on purpose, as the more respectful and less domestic spelling, or simply out of habit, no witness says.
