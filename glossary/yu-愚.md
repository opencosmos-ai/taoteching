## 愚 (Yú) — Foolish

*Say "yü," rising. Three lines in three chapters. The ordinary word of its day for a fool, and the book takes it up as its own.*

**Locked: 愚 → "a fool" / "foolishness" / "foolish."** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-16. Not "guileless," which turns a word of contempt into a compliment and cannot stand at chapter 38. Not "simple," which sweetens it the same way, more quietly. Not "ignorant" or "stupid," which are a lack and an insult with no reversal left in them.)*

Chapters 20 and 38 have long said *fool* and *foolishness*. Until 2026-11-16 chapter 65 alone sweetened the word, as *guileless*, at the one line where it is done to the people, which is where the discomfort is. **愚 is an insult, and the book takes the insult for itself.** The English has to keep the insult, or the taking means nothing.

### The graph: a heart, and a monkey

> 愚，戇也。从心从禺。禺，猴屬，獸之愚者。
> *"愚 is dull-wittedness. From 心 (xīn — heart) and 禺 (yú). 禺 is a kind of monkey, the most foolish of beasts."*

Xu Shen's 禺, in its own entry: 母猴屬。頭似鬼。从甶从禸 — *"a kind of macaque; its head looks like a ghost's. From 甶 (fú — a ghost's head) and 禸 (róu — an animal's track)."* So to Xu Shen 愚 is a heart like a monkey's: the dullest animal Xu Shen could name, under the organ of thought.

**Where the picture stops.** 禺 is almost certainly in the graph for its sound. It reads \*ŋo in reconstructed Old Chinese, and it gives the same sound to 隅 (yú — corner), 遇 (yù — to meet) and 偶 (ǒu — an image), none of which has anything to do with monkeys. The monkey is Xu Shen's reading of the part, not the word's origin. The sources this entry consulted record no oracle-bone form. **What holds is the heart.** 愚 is a condition of the 心 (xīn — heart), and chapter 20 puts it exactly there: 愚人之心 (yú rén zhī xīn — "the heart of a fool").

The silks bear this out. At chapter 20 silk A writes only 禺 where silk B and our base write 愚, and at chapter 41 silk B writes 禺 for 隅 (yú — corner): 大方無禺 (dà fāng wú yú — "the great square has no corners"). The silks borrow 禺 for its sound, so silk A's 禺 is the same word spelled by ear, not a monkey.

### Three lines, and the word changes sides

> *The crowd all have more than enough.*
> *I alone seem to have missed out.*
> *I have the heart of a fool!*
> *How muddled!*
>
> 眾人皆有餘，
> 而我獨若遺。
> 我愚人之心也哉！
> 沌沌兮！
>
> — chapter 20

> *Knowing in advance is the blossom of the Tao, and the beginning of foolishness.*
>
> 前識者，道之華，而愚之始。
>
> — chapter 38

> *The ancients who were masterful at the Tao*
> *did not use it to make the people see clearly,*
> *but to make them fools.*
>
> 古之善為道者，
> 非以明民，
> 將以愚之。
>
> — chapter 65

**At chapter 38, 愚 is where a bad road ends. At chapter 20 it is the speaker's boast. At chapter 65 it is where the masters bring the people.** One word, carrying both valences, as 強 (qiáng — strong) does. Whatever English the word takes must stand in all three lines. *Guileless* cannot stand at chapter 38: knowing in advance is not the beginning of guilelessness. *Fool* stands in all three, and two of them already print it.

### In its own language, 愚 is an insult

The book did not choose a gentle word. In the writing of its time 愚 is the plain opposite of knowing, and a term of contempt:

- Xunzi: 是是非非謂之知，非是是非謂之愚 — *"calling right right and wrong wrong is knowing; calling right wrong and wrong right is foolishness."*
- The Analects (17.3): 唯上知與下愚不移 — *"only the highest knowers and the lowest fools do not change."*
- Zhuangzi uses it as an insult too: 愚者自以為覺 — *"the fool takes themselves to be awake."*

**So when the book says 愚, it picks up a word everyone uses to look down on someone.** It does the same with the low place, the soft and the empty. Others of its time did it too, and kept the insult in the word when they did:

- Confucius, of the minister Ning Wuzi (Analects 5.21): 邦有道則知，邦無道則愚。其知可及也，其愚不可及也 — *"when the state had the Tao, Ning Wuzi was knowing; when it lost the Tao, a fool. That knowing can be matched; that foolishness cannot."*
- Zhuangzi: 衆人役役，聖人愚芚 — *"the crowd toil and bustle; the sage is foolish and dull."*

The praise lands only because the word is still an insult. Turn 愚 into a virtue word in English and there is nothing left to reverse.

### Chapter 65 ends where Zhuangzi puts the fool

Chapter 65 opens with the masters making the people 愚, and closes on 玄德 (xuán dé — profound integrity) and 大順 (dà shùn — the great current). A chapter of the Zhuangzi joins the same three words in the same order:

> 其合緡緡，若愚若昏，是謂玄德，同乎大順。
> *"Its joining is seamless, as if foolish, as if dim: this is called profound integrity, at one with the great current."*

The Zhuangzi is not the Laozi, and it is not a commentary on it. A parallel like this locates a reading. It does not prove one (`process/principles/distribution-locates.md`). But it shows how a near-contemporary heard these words together: **the foolishness is the profound integrity.** Read that way, chapter 65 closes where it opened. The masters bring the people to foolishness, and the chapter ends in profound integrity running with the great current.

### What the commentators did with it

**At chapter 65 both turn the word into praise.** Wang Bi: 愚謂無知守真，順自然也 — *"愚 means unknowing, keeping to what is genuine, following what is so of itself."* Heshang Gong: 將以道德教民使朴質不詐偽 — *"they would teach the people by the Tao and integrity, making them plain and uncarved, without deceit or falsity."* *Guileless* is Heshang Gong's 不詐偽 (bù zhà wěi — "without deceit or falsity") put into the line. That is a gloss doing a rendering's job (`process/principles/commentary-is-not-a-rendering.md`). The commentators explain why the masters wanted it. The line says what they wanted.

**At chapter 20 they say what the fool's heart does not do.** Wang Bi: 絕愚之人，心無所別析，意無所好欲，猶然其情不可覩，我頹然若此也 — *"the utterly foolish person: the heart divides and parses nothing, the mind likes and wants nothing, and their state cannot be made out. I am slumped like that."* Heshang Gong: 不與俗人相隨，守一不移，如愚人之心也 — *"not following the worldly, keeping to the one without moving: like the heart of a fool."* Neither softens the word. Wang Bi's fool sorts nothing, and that is the point.

**At chapter 38 Han Fei tells a story that holds both sides of the word.** The diviner Zhan He sits with disciples, and an ox lows outside the gate. A disciple says it is a black ox with a white forehead. Zhan He says it is black, but the white is on its horns. They send someone to look: a black ox, with cloth wrapped round its horns. Then Han Fei: 嘗試釋詹子之察，而使五尺之愚童子視之，亦知其黑牛而以布裹其角也。故以詹子之察，苦心傷神，而後與五尺之愚童子同功，是以曰：「愚之首也。」 — *"Set Zhan He's discernment aside and have a foolish child of five feet look: the child too sees a black ox with cloth round its horns. So Zhan He's discernment, with a troubled heart and a wearied spirit, achieves only what a foolish child achieves. That is why it says 'the head of folly.'"* The forecaster's art is the folly. The foolish child just looks, and sees.

Heshang Gong on the same line: 言前識之人愚闇之倡始 — *"those who know in advance are the first to lead into folly and darkness."*

### The silks and Han Fei read "the head of folly"

At chapter 38 both silks and Han Fei's lemma read 愚之首 (yú zhī shǒu — "the head of folly") where our base reads 愚之始 (yú zhī shǐ — "the beginning of folly"). That makes the two lines a pair: 亂之首 (luàn zhī shǒu — "the head of disorder") and 愚之首, the head of disorder and the head of folly. Our base keeps 始, and the chapter keeps *head* and *beginning* apart, as its notes argue. The fork is recorded in `sources/variants.yaml`.

### What is set aside

**Guileless.** It turns contempt into a compliment, which is a verdict the Chinese does not carry (`process/principles/no-verdict-the-chinese-lacks.md`). It comes from a commentator's gloss, not the line. It cannot stand at chapter 38. And it hides the one link that answers the reader who calls chapter 65 authoritarian. With *fool* in both chapters, a reader who meets the masters making the people fools can find the speaker of chapter 20 saying *"I have the heart of a fool!"* The masters bring the people to where the sage stands. *Guileless* cut that thread.

**Simple.** The same sweetening, done more quietly. It slides between *pure* and *simple-minded* and lets the reader pick the kinder one, so it hides the insult rather than translating it. The uncarved, 樸 (pǔ), already holds the sense of plainness in this book.

**Ignorant, ignorance.** These name a lack, knowledge withheld. That is the authoritarian reading in one word, *"kept them ignorant."* The fool of chapter 20 lacks nothing they want. *Unknowing*, Wang Bi's 無知 (wú zhī), is already that phrase's English at chapters 3 and 10.

**Stupid.** A modern insult, and harder than the Chinese. It has none of the long tradition of the fool, and *"I have a stupid heart"* carries no boast.

**Dull.** Already spoken for. It renders 悶悶 (mèn mèn — dull) in chapter 20 itself, *"I alone am dull,"* and again in chapter 58.

**Make fools of them.** The English idiom means to dupe or humiliate someone, the opposite of what the masters do. Chapter 65 reads *make them fools*, never *make fools of them*.

**Foolish, at chapter 65.** Accurate, but it names only a lapse of judgment, and it settles toward blame what the Chinese leaves open. *Fools* keeps both sides of the word, the one looked down on and the long tradition of the court fool and the wise fool, and it repeats chapter 20's noun exactly. The bare 愚 can name people: the Analects' 下愚 (xià yú — "the lowest fools"). *(Shalom's intuition, 2026-11-16.)*

**The fool.** It would point at the archetype outright, and the Chinese marks no figure: there are no articles, and 愚 here is a plain verb about the people. *Make them the fool* also sets a plural against a singular, and it lands on the idiom *make someone the fool*, to make them the dupe. *Fools* lets the reader reach the archetype through chapter 20's echo, the way the book does.

**Great wisdom seems foolish.** The proverb 大智若愚 (dà zhì ruò yú) is not in this book. It comes from the Song dynasty, from Su Shi, whose words were 大智如愚 (dà zhì rú yú — "great wisdom is like foolishness"). It sweetens 愚 into wisdom in disguise. The book never says the fool is secretly wise. It says the sage is the fool.

### Working register

| Ch | Chinese | Our English | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 我**愚**人之心也哉 (*wǒ yú rén zhī xīn yě zāi*) | *I have the heart of a fool!* | 愚人 (yú rén — a foolish person), a noun |
| 38 | 而**愚**之始 (*ér yú zhī shǐ*) | *the beginning of foolishness* | a noun |
| 65 | 將以**愚**之 (*jiāng yǐ yú zhī*) | *but to make them fools* | a causative verb, set as *make them* + the noun |

**The chapter 65 block:**

> *The ancients who were masterful at the Tao*
> *did not use it to make the people see clearly,*
> *but to make them fools.*

The two halves are parallel causatives in the Chinese, 以明民 (yǐ míng mín — "using it to make the people see clearly") and 以愚之 (yǐ yú zhī — "using it to make them fools"), and the English keeps them parallel. 將 (jiāng) marks what was meant, and in the frame 非 X，將 Y (fēi X, jiāng Y — "not X, but rather Y") it works as *but*. The supplied *return* goes: nothing in the Chinese turns back (`WORKLIST.md` T1-27).

### What no rule can enforce

**Dull** belongs to 悶悶 (mèn mèn — dull), which shares chapter 20, so only this entry rules it out for 愚.

**Simple** is left off the list, because other characters may need it in these chapters. Only this entry holds it.

### Left open

**Chapter 65 reads sharply.** *"Did not use it to make the people see clearly, but to make them fools"* is the sentence quoted to call Laozi an authoritarian, now in plain English. The edition prints it. The answer is in the book itself: the speaker of chapter 20 claims the same word, and chapter 65 closes in profound integrity. `notes/reading.md` → *Ch 65* sets out the charge and the answer, and keeps its honest caveat: Laozi really is suspicious of sophistication as such.

**Chapter 38's fork stays open.** Our base reads *the beginning of foolishness*, and the silks and Han Fei read *the head of folly*, which pairs the line with the one before it (`sources/variants.yaml`).

**Whether the fool of chapter 65 is the fool of chapter 20 is the reader's to decide.** The English now lets a reader ask the question. It does not answer it.
