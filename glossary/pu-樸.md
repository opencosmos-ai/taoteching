## 樸 (Pǔ) — Uncarved Wood

*Say "poo," dipping and lifting. Eight appearances across six chapters — 15, 19, 28, 32, 37, 57. Perhaps the most concrete image in the book, and the one most often turned into an abstraction.*

**Locked: 樸 → "uncarved wood" / "the uncarved."** *(Lowercase. Not "simplicity," not "the Uncarved Block" as a capitalized proper noun.)*

樸 is a piece of timber that has not yet been made into anything. That is the whole word, and its power is that it stays a piece of wood. The temptation — and most translations succumb — is to lift it into a virtue: *simplicity, purity, primal innocence*. But an abstraction cannot be **split**, and splitting is precisely what Chapter 28 does to it. Keep the wood, and the argument survives.

### The graph

樸 is built on **木** (*mù*) — a tree, the oldest and plainest graph for wood — with a phonetic element beside it. The *Shuowen Jiezi* defines it 木素也: *wood in its 素 state*, where **素** (*sù*) is **undyed silk** — cloth still the color it was when it came off the worm.

So the character's own definition reaches for its twin. And Laozi puts them side by side, once, in **Chapter 19**:

> 見素抱樸
> *See the undyed; embrace the uncarved.*

Two materials, one condition: **before human working.** Silk before the dye-vat, timber before the chisel. Not innocent, not sacred — just not yet turned into something for us.

### Chapter 28 — the block and the vessels

The central passage, and the reason the wood must stay wood:

> 樸散則為器
> *When the uncarved is split up, it becomes vessels.*

**器** (*qì*) is a vessel, a tool, an implement — a thing with a **function**. And here is the whole idea in one motion: the block has no function. Carve it, and you get a bowl, a wheel, a handle — each useful, each *named*, each now only one thing. What the block loses in becoming useful is **everything it might otherwise have been.**

Note where this lands in the chapter's architecture. Chapter 28 is built of three parallel triads, each closing on 復歸於 — *return again to*: return to **the infant** (嬰兒), return to **the limitless** (無極), return to **the uncarved** (樸). All three name the same direction: back toward what has not yet been made particular.

And 器 in Chinese is not only pottery. It is also what a **person** amounts to — 成器 means *to become someone useful, to amount to something*. So 樸散則為器 reads, without strain, as a sentence about people: society takes the whole raw human and carves them into a function. A role. A job title. Useful, named, and no longer able to be anything else.

### Why the uncarved is nameless

Two chapters bind 樸 to 無名 (*wúmíng* — namelessness), and the link is exact rather than poetic:

> 道常無名，樸。(32) — *The Tao is ever nameless, uncarved.*
> 吾將鎮之以無名之樸。(37) — *I will settle them with the nameless uncarved.*

**Carving is naming.** A shaped thing can be named because it is now one determinate thing — *this is a bowl*. The block resists naming for the same reason it resists use: it is not yet any particular thing. Chapter 1's nameless (無名) and Chapter 28's uncarved (樸) are one condition described in two materials — one in language, one in wood.

Which explains the strangest line of the group:

> 樸雖小，天下莫能臣也。(32)
> *Though the uncarved is small, nothing in the world can make it a subject.*

**臣** (*chén*) is a vassal, a servant, one who serves under. You cannot subordinate the uncarved — because to subordinate is to assign a function, and to assign a function is to carve. What has no use cannot be put to use. Its smallness is not weakness; it is the reason nothing can get a grip on it.

### The resolution — 大制不割

Chapter 28 does not leave us with a prohibition on all shaping, which would be unlivable. It closes:

> 聖人用之，則為官長，故大制不割。
> *The sage uses them, and so becomes chief of officials — therefore the greatest shaping does no cutting.*

**制** (*zhì*) is to tailor, cut out, regulate, govern. **割** (*gē*) is to sever. So: *the great carving does not sever.* The sage does not refuse to govern, and does not pretend the world contains no vessels. What they refuse is the **cut** — the severing that turns a whole person into an instrument. Shape without severing; use without reducing. That is the practical form of 無為 (*wúwéi* — non-doing) applied to people.

### The far echo — Chapter 41

One more line belongs to this cluster, and its manuscript fork makes it sharper: 大器晚成 (41), which the older witnesses read as 大器**免**成 — *the great vessel is **never** completed.* This edition follows the manuscript reading.

Set it beside Chapter 28 and it snaps into place. If a vessel is made by cutting, then the **greatest** vessel is the one never finished being cut — which is to say, the block itself. The great vessel is never completed because completion is exactly what would ruin it.

### What is set aside

**"Simplicity."** The most common rendering and the most costly. It is abstract, it sounds like a lifestyle preference, and it cannot be split into vessels. Chapter 28's argument becomes unreadable.

**"Purity," "innocence," "primal wholeness."** These import moral and spiritual value the wood does not carry. An uncut log is not virtuous. It is simply uncut — which is a fact about its history, not its character.

**"The Uncarved Block," capitalized.** The initial capitals turn a log into a doctrine, and this is exactly the devotional typography the overlay audit warns against: in English, capitals confer deity. Laozi's word is small and homely — 樸雖小, *the uncarved is small*. Keep it lowercase.

**"Raw material," "unprocessed material."** Correct in denotation, industrial in feel, and they imply the thing is *awaiting* processing — which inverts the value.

**Leaving it untranslated as *pu*.** The image is fully available in English. Transliterating would mystify a piece of wood.

### Working register

- as noun → **uncarved wood**, or **the uncarved** where the sentence needs it to act as a subject (樸散則為器 → "when the uncarved is split up, it becomes vessels")
- in simile → **like uncarved wood** (敦兮其若樸 → "solid, like uncarved wood")
- bound to namelessness → **the nameless uncarved** (無名之樸)
- predicative, of people → **plain** — a licensed flexion, see below
- **器** as its counter-term → **vessel** where the thing is a container, **tool** or **instrument** where the point is function

**On "plain" at Chapter 57.** 我無欲，而民自樸 is rendered *"I am desireless, and the people become plain of themselves"* — and it should stay. The line sits in a four-part refrain (transform / right / enrich / ——), each member a short predicate ending in 自 (*of themselves*); "become uncarved of themselves" would stall it, and "plain" carries 樸's core sense exactly. This is a deliberate flexion, not a drift: the plain people of Chapter 57 and the uncut wood of Chapter 28 are the same image at two scales.


### Is there a paradox inside the word? — the "block" question

A tempting reading holds that 樸 is *self-contradictory by construction*: to have a **block**, someone must already have felled, squared, and sawn the timber — so the "uncarved block" is always already carved. It is an elegant thought. It does not survive the evidence, and it is worth showing why, because what replaces it is better.

**The lexicography.** The *Shuowen* defines 樸 as 木素 — *plain wood, unworked lumber.* Not a block. Not a blank. A **state**, not a shape.

**The word-family.** 樸 is 木 (wood) + the phonetic 菐. Change the material radical and you get **璞** (*pú*) — 玉 (jade) + the same 菐 — meaning **uncut jade still in the rock**, the stone as it comes out of the mountain. The shared phonetic carries the sense *raw, pre-worked*, and jade-in-the-matrix is unambiguously a natural object that no hand has touched. Whatever 菐 means, it is not "prepared stock."

**The company it keeps.** Chapter 28's architecture is decisive. Three parallel triads, each closing on 復歸於 — *return again to*:

> 復歸於**嬰兒** — return again to the **infant**
> 復歸於**無極** — return again to the **limitless**
> 復歸於**樸** — return again to the **uncarved**

Three slots, one grammar, one category. An infant is not a prepared material; neither is the limitless. 樸 sits among *original natural conditions*, not processed intermediates.

**The syntax.** Chapter 32 reads 道常無名。樸雖小 — *the Tao is ever nameless; though [it is] uncarved and small…* Here 樸 attaches to **the Tao itself**. The Tao is not a piece of squared timber awaiting a chisel.

So the "block" is **English**, not Chinese — a noun supplied by translators to give an adjective a body. Scholars have said so directly: Pas and Leung note that the addition of "block" is an *interpretation* rather than a translation of the original sense of plain wood. This is a further argument for our lock: **"uncarved wood," never "uncarved block."** The block imports a paradox Laozi did not write — and worse, by satisfying the appetite for paradox, it hides the three real ones.

### The paradoxes that are actually there

**1. You are told to return to a state that cannot be recovered.** 復歸於樸 (28). Wood does not un-carve. Once the block is vessels, no craft on earth makes it a block again. Yet the instruction stands, three times over, in parallel with returning to infancy and to the limitless — both equally irreversible. Either the instruction is impossible, or *returning* means something other than reversal: not restoring a former shape but ceasing to identify with the shape you were cut into. The text does not explain. It simply keeps pointing backward along a road that only runs forward.

**2. The great carving does not cut.** 大制不割 (28) — Laozi's own explicit paradox, placed in the very passage where the block becomes vessels. 制 is to tailor, cut out, govern; 割 is to sever. The greatest shaping does no severing. Not a refusal to shape — a shaping that leaves nothing cut.

**3. The un-usable is used.** Chapter 32 insists that nothing in the world can make the uncarved a subject — 天下莫能臣也 — because it has no function to be pressed into. Then Chapter 37: 吾將鎮之以無名之樸 — *I will settle them with the nameless uncarved.* The ruler **deploys** it. The thing defined by having no use is picked up and used, and the paradox is exactly the point: what settles a grasping world is the one thing that cannot be grasped.

**And one more, structural.** Chapter 32 says 始制有名 — *when the carving begins, there are names.* Naming **is** carving. Which means every word in this book is a cut, including the word 樸 — Laozi is using chisels to point at the uncut log. He seems to know it: 道可道，非常道 (1), and 知者不言 (56). The book is a carving that argues against carving.

That is the honest shape of the thing. The paradox is not buried in the etymology; it is in what Laozi *does* with a perfectly ordinary word for raw timber. Which is more interesting, and more his.

### Left open

The obvious objection: bowls are good. Wheels are good. A world of uncut logs is a world with nothing to eat from. Laozi knows this — Chapter 11 is a hymn to the usefulness of vessels, and Chapter 28 has the sage *using* the carved things and governing with them.

So this is not a counsel to remain unformed. It is closer to a warning about **which way the traffic runs.** Everything in the book moves toward the uncarved — return to the infant, return to the limitless, return to the block — while the world moves the other way, cutting and naming and assigning, until nothing whole is left. The instruction is directional, not absolute: know that you were a block before you were a tool, and that the tool is the smaller thing.

What the text will not say is how much carving a life requires. It offers only 大制不割 — *shape without severing* — which is less a rule than a discipline of the hand, and, like most of this book's central instructions, easier to recognize than to follow.
