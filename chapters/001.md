# Chapter 1

## Translation

The Tao that can be made a way is not the ever-present Tao.  
The name that can be named is not the ever-present name.  
The nameless is the beginning of sky and earth.  
The named is the mother of the countless things.

Ever desireless, you can observe its subtlety;  
Ever desiring, you can observe its direction.

These two emerge together, but differ in name.  
Together, they are called dark.  
Dark, and dark again, the gate of all subtlety.

## Notes

**道可道 → *The Tao that can be made a way*.** **The book opens on a paradox, and the paradox is in the grammar.** 道可道，非常道 (*dào kě dào, fēi cháng dào* — "the Tao that can be *dào*-ed is not the ever-present Tao") prints one graph three times in six characters: the name, the same word acting as a verb, and the name again. The line turns its own central word back on itself, so the ever-present Tao is denied in its own word. To keep the paradox, the English has to hold three things together:
- **the name**, *Tao*, which the line also teaches means *way*;
- **the turn**, the middle 道 being the same word, made to act;
- **the voice**, the Tao being what the verb is done to.

**How the second 道 is a verb.** Classical Chinese does not inflect; a word's job is its position. 可 (*kě* — can, may) always takes a verb and makes it passive — 可食 (*kě shí*) is *can be eaten*, edible — so whatever stands after 可 is an action. 道 (noun: the Tao) · 可 (can be) · 道 (verb: *dào*-ed). The next line is the same shape, and English can follow that one: 名可名, *the name that can be named*. The move was ordinary in Laozi's century — the 論語 (*Lúnyǔ* — the *Analects*) has 君君，臣臣 (*jūn jūn, chén chén* — "let the lord be a lord, the minister a minister") — so it is not a joke on two meanings but **one word turned on itself.** 說文 (*Shuōwén*) gives the noun as 所行道也 (*suǒ xíng dào yě* — "the road that is walked"), and the verb grows from it: to tread a way, to lead along it (later split off as 導, *dǎo* — to guide), to tell it in words.

**Why the voice is the point.** 可 + verb puts the subject on the receiving end (`glossary/ke-可.md`). The Tao is not doing the *dào*-ing; it is what would be *dào*-ed. So someone other than the Tao does it: a person who lays it out as a route, a course, a program to follow. The Tao's own manner is 自然 (*zì rán* — so of itself), and ch 25 ends on it: 道法自然, *"the Tao models itself on being what it is."* Being made a way is not something the Tao does of itself. It is done to the Tao, by human hands, and the line says that what comes of it is not the ever-present Tao. An English in another voice loses this. *The Tao that can become a way* makes the change the Tao's own, a claim about what the Tao does, where the Chinese speaks of what is done to it.

**How the English makes it a verb.** *Tao* is locked as a name (`glossary/dao-道.md`), and *"the Tao that can be Tao'd"* is not English. So the middle 道 is rendered by **what the name means**, and *made* turns it into a verb: *made a way*. *Made* is supplied scaffolding (`process/principles/supplied-verb-is-scaffolding.md`); *way* is 道's own word. The line reads *Tao · way · Tao*, carrying the word all three times the Chinese prints it, and a reader learns what the name means before learning anything else. *Made a way* is the ordinary English passive of *make it a way*, as in *she was made a judge*, and nothing in it is spare. **The precedent is already in the book**: where 道 is an ordinary way and not the name, this manuscript renders it *way* — 天之道 and 天道 (*tiān zhī dào*, *tiān dào* — the way of heaven) are *"the way of nature"* at ch 9, 47, 73, 77, 79 and 81; 人之道 (*rén zhī dào*) is *"the way of people"* at ch 77; 聖人之道 (*shèng rén zhī dào*) is *"the way of the sage"* at ch 81. The lock forbids *the Way* as a stand-in for the Tao; a lowercase *a way* is the common noun doing its own job.

**Where other translations let it go.** English versions keep one or two of the three and give up the rest. What goes most often is the turn.
- **The middle 道 becomes speech.** Waley (1934), *the Way that can be told of*; Lau (1963), *the way that can be spoken of*; Feng and English (1972), *the Tao that can be told*. The word appears twice, not three times, and the paradox becomes a claim about language: the Tao cannot be put into words. That is the next line's subject (名可名, *the name that can be named*), so the opening line is folded into its partner.
- **A different word takes the middle.** Legge (1891), *the Tao that can be trodden*, keeps the road and the passive, but the reader hears two words where the Chinese has one.
- **The turn is kept by translating the name.** Carus (1898), *the Reason that can be reasoned*, turns the word on itself exactly as the Chinese does, but only by making the Tao *Reason*: the Logos of John 1:1, which the Chinese Union Bible renders with 道 (`process/overlay-audit.md`).
- **The walker is kept, and the name is lost.** Le Guin (1997), *the way you can go*, names the person the line implies, but the Tao is gone from the sentence and *go* is a different word from *way*.

None of these keeps all three. *The Tao that can be made a way* does: the name, the same word in the middle, and the passive.

**What the line says, and who says it with us.** *Made a way* — laid out as a route, a course, a program to follow:
- 河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) glosses the second 道 with the noun itself: 謂經術政教之道也 — *"it means the **way** of classical learning and political instruction."* A Tao that can be laid out and taught.
- 韓非 (*Hán Fēi*, the oldest reader we carry): the ever-present has 無定理 (*wú dìng lǐ* — "no fixed pattern"), 是以不可道也…然而可論 — *"therefore it cannot be **dào**-ed… and yet it can be discussed."* On a reading of *spoken* that sentence contradicts itself; on *made a way* it is exact. A fixed course is the thing it cannot be.
- 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) is half with us: 可道之道，可名之名，指事造形，非其常也 — *"the Tao that can be dào-ed and the name that can be named point at affairs and **make forms**; they are not its constant."* 造形 (*zào xíng* — to make a form) is the making; 指事 (*zhǐ shì* — to point at affairs) is the designation, and that half we lose.

**And the two lines do two things, as the Chinese does.** Line 1 asks whether the Tao can be made a way; line 2, 名可名, whether it can be named. A speech reading folds the first into the second.

**What it costs, named rather than hidden.** **The speech sense goes.** It is real: the 詩經 (*Shījīng* — the Book of Songs) has 中冓之言，不可道也 (*"the words of the inner chamber cannot be told"*), and 河上公 closes his own gloss on this line with 不可稱道 (*bù kě chēng dào* — "it cannot be spoken of"). **This is a line where the commentators diverge and the English picks a side** — the route reading of 河上公's lemma gloss and 韓非, over 王弼's pointing — which `divergence-stays-open` would refuse. It is taken here on Shalom's call, because the route reading is the one that lets the English keep the paradox. *Made a way* is also longer than 道可道's three syllables, and a little longer than its partner line, *the name that can be named*. And it brushes the English idiom *make a way*, to clear a path; in this sentence the Tao is what is made, so the idiom does not take hold.

**What was refused, and why.**
- ***spoken*** — *speak* is 言 (*yán* — speech)'s English at ch 5, 17, 27, 56, 66 and 73, and it makes ch 1 rhyme in English with ch 56's 知者不言 (*"Those who know do not speak"*), where the Chinese uses a different character. It also makes 韓非 contradict himself.
- ***told*** — the strongest alternative: it carries speech *and* directions (*tell someone the way*). It loses the word *way*, which is the point of the line.
- ***followed*** — the book says the Tao *is* followed, and praises it: 惟道是從 (*wéi dào shì cóng* — ch 21, *"follows only the Tao"*), 從事於道 (*cóng shì yú dào* — ch 23). *Follow* is also 從 (*cóng*)'s, 隨 (*suí*)'s and 徒 (*tú*)'s word already.
- ***conceived as Tao*** — keeps three *Tao*s, but moves the act inside the head, where no commentator puts it; turns line 1 into line 2's naming; and is seminar register.
- ***mapped*** is 圖 (*tú* — to plan)'s word at ch 63; ***traced*** chimes with 跡 (*jì* — footprints) at ch 27, *"leaves no tracks."*
- ***become a way*** — lighter, and wrong in voice. It has the Tao turn into a way of itself, where the Chinese has the Tao made one. It breaks the parallel with 名可名, which English keeps passive. And *become* is the English of a verb the line does not print, 為 (*wéi* — to do, to serve as, to become).
- ***made into a way*** — the same passive with a spare word. *Made* already does the work *into* was added to do.

*The Literal column for this line was changed with it — it read "can be spoken", which was one reading set down as the gloss.*

**同謂之玄。玄之又玄 → *"Together, they are called dark. / Dark, and dark again"*: the chain, kept.** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-14.)* *(was: "Together, they are called profound darkness." and a stanza break before "Dark, and dark again".)* **The Chinese passes 玄 (*xuán* — dark) from one line into the next, and the English now does too.** 同謂之玄 (*tóng wèi zhī xuán* — "together they are called dark") ends on 玄, and 玄之又玄 (*xuán zhī yòu xuán* — "dark, and dark again") opens on it: a chain, 頂真 (*dǐng zhēn* — "capping"), as at ch 6. *Profound darkness* spent two abstract words on one character, so the link was nearly kept and not quite, and a blank line then cut it (`WORKLIST.md` T3-15; `process/form-audit.md` → *The page*, rule 3). **The glossary's own register already said *dark* for this line**: 玄 standing alone and named takes the colour word, because the image is doing the work and *profound* abstracts it (`glossary/xuan-miao-玄妙.md` → *Working register*).

**This is where the chapter's mystery lives, and why it needs no word *mystery*.** Wang Bi on this line: 玄者冥也，默然無有也 — *"玄 is the dim dark: silent, nothing there."* It is called 玄 only 取於不可得而謂之然也 — *"because it cannot be grasped, and so it is called that."* And if it were fixed as one 玄, 則是名則失之遠矣，故曰玄之又玄也 — *"that would make it a name, and miss by far; so it says 'dark, and dark again.'"* The doubling exists so the word cannot harden into a name. Said with one plain *dark*, then *dark* twice more, the English deepens the way a dyer re-dips silk, which is what English readers have heard as *mystery*. *Mystery* itself stays refused: it says the thing cannot be known, where line 5 has just said to observe it (`glossary/xuan-miao-玄妙.md`).

**The cost.** *Dark* alone is starker than *profound darkness*, and a reader may hear only absence of light. The next line's doubling, and 玄's uses elsewhere (玄牝 *the dark female*, 玄德 *profound integrity*), give it its depth. 同 (*tóng* — together, the same) keeps *Together*; see the note on line 7, below.

**眾妙之門 → *"the gate of all subtlety"*: one English for 妙 in both its slots.** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-14.)* *(was: "the gate of all subtleties.")* 妙 (*miào* — subtle) appears twice. In line 5, 以觀其妙 (*yǐ guān qí miào* — "to observe its subtle"), it is owned by 其 (*qí* — its): one quality, the subtlety of what the still gaze looks at. In line 9 it is counted by 眾 (*zhòng* — the many). **When a character takes two slots in one chapter, the English has to survive both** (`notes/translation.md` → *同 finished*, the ch 56 rule). *Subtlety* does: *its subtlety*, then *all subtlety*, with *all* carrying 眾. The echo is now word for word, as `glossary/xuan-miao-玄妙.md` asks: what you glimpse in stillness is what the gate opens onto.

**Why not the plural.** *Subtleties* leans in English toward *nuances*, the fine points of an argument, which no commentator reads here. Wang Bi has 妙者微之極也 — *"妙 is the faint at its limit."* Heshang Gong has 妙，要也 — *"妙 means the essential."* *The gate of subtlety* and *the gate of the subtle* were weighed the same day; both drop 眾. **The gate stays *of*, not *to*:** 之 (*zhī* — 's) is possessive and carries no direction, and the commentators split on which way the gate faces. Wang Bi has the subtle things coming out: 眾妙皆從同而出 — *"the many subtle things all come out from the same."* Heshang Gong has a person going in: 是謂知道要之門戶也 — *"this is called the door to knowing the Tao's essential."* *Of* holds both. **And *gate*, not *gateway*:** 門 (*mén* — gate) is *gate* at ch 6 and ch 10, a gate that opens and closes (天門開闔 — "the gate of the sky opens and closes").

**The cost.** Wang Bi's reading is distributive, 皆 (*jiē* — each, all): each subtle thing comes out on its own. The singular softens that plurality. *All* keeps the many in it, but not as countable things.

**常無欲，以觀其妙 → *"Ever desireless, you can observe its subtlety"*: a capacity, not a command.** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-14.)* *(was: "Ever desireless, observe its subtlety; / Ever desiring, observe its boundaries.")* 以 (*yǐ* — thereby, by this means) says what the state makes possible. **Both commentators read it as a capacity:** Wang Bi: 故常無欲空虛，可以觀其始物之妙 — *"so, ever desireless and empty, one **can** observe the subtlety where things begin"*. Heshang Gong: 人常能無欲，則可以觀道之要 — *"if a person can always be desireless, then they **can** observe the Tao's essential."* The back-translation audit's blind readers of the old line came away with an order (`process/back-translation-audit.md` → *Minor*, ch 1).

- ***You*** is scaffolding the commentators supply: Heshang Gong's 人 (*rén* — person), Wang Bi's 空虛 (*kōng xū* — empty), a state of the seer. *"In the absence of desire, its subtleties can be observed"* was weighed the same day and declined: the passive makes the subtlety a property of the thing and leaves no one to be desireless, and *in the absence of* turns 常 (*cháng* — ever) into a moment. The general *you* is the book's voice elsewhere: ch 47, *"Without leaving home, you know the whole world."*
- ***Ever* opens both lines**, as 常 opens both in the Chinese, so the word is heard where it repeats. 故 (*gù* — so) is left without English for that reason; the *Yongle Dadian* prints no 故 on this line (`sources/variants.yaml`). **Settled for this chapter**: whatever the book-wide 故 decision (`WORKLIST.md` T3-12) makes *So*, line 5 stays bare, as ch 23's 故飄風 (*gù piāo fēng* — "so a squall") does. *"So, ever desireless"* was proposed and withdrawn: it buried *ever* behind a connective.
- ***Present* is not carried into these lines.** *"Ever present and desireless"* would bring in the mindfulness register of *being present*, which the line does not hold, and would break the match with chs 32, 34 and 37 (below).

**The cost.** *You* is not printed. 徼 (*jiào*) is the next note.

**常有欲，以觀其徼 → *"Ever desiring, you can observe its direction."*** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-14.)* *(was: "observe its boundaries.")* **徼 is a heading, not an edge.** The graph has the road radical, 彳 (*chì* — a step), and our *Shuowen* defines it 循也 — *"to go along, to go round."* *Border* is a later sense, 邊徼 (*biān jiào* — the patrolled frontier), and no commentator we carry reads it here. Both read a destination:

- **Heshang Gong:** 徼，歸也。常有欲之人，可以觀世俗之所歸趣也 — *"徼 means returning. A person ever desiring can observe where the worldly are **heading**."* *Direction* is this reading exactly.
- **Wang Bi:** 徼，歸終也 — *"徼 is where things return to their end"* — and 故常有欲，可以觀其終物之徼也 — *"so, ever desiring, one can observe the 徼 where things end."* He pairs it with line 5's 始物之妙 (*"the subtlety where things begin"*), so that 妙 and 徼 are beginning and end: 萬物始於微而後成 — *"the countless things begin in the faint, and then are completed."*

**Why *direction*.** It keeps the Chinese parallel, 其 (*qí* — its) + a noun in both lines, so the frame 以觀其▢ (*yǐ guān qí ▢* — "thereby observe its ▢") is whole: *its subtlety / its direction*. And it is a quality the eye follows while it happens, as *subtlety* is, not a product read off afterwards. **It does not rank the two ways of seeing.** Desire reads which way things are going, which is real knowledge, and the next line has both gazes *emerge together*.

**What was weighed and why it lost.**
- ***boundaries*** and ***limits*** rest on the later frontier sense, and both make what desire sees the surface of things, a ranking the Chinese does not make; *limits* adds *limitation*.
- ***manifestations*** reads 徼 as 皦 (*jiǎo* — bright, *Shuowen*: 玉石之白也, *"the white of jade"*), the same sound element and ch 14's *bright*. Five Latinate syllables, and the two-realm metaphysics this edition refused at ch 21 (*the manifest realm*). The Mawangdui reading here is said to be 所噭 (*suǒ jiào*), variously taken as 皦, 竅 (*qiào* — an opening) or 徼; **unverified, and not in `sources/variants.yaml`**, so nothing rests on it.
- ***ends***, ***outcomes***, ***results***, ***consequences*** — Wang Bi's end, but each is a product, not something watched, and *observe its ends* is awkward English; *consequences* brings a moral warning; *its ends* can be heard as *its purposes*, a Tao with aims.
- ***where it leads***, ***where it ends***, ***where it goes*** — natural, but each breaks the noun parallel; *leads to* is 乃 (*nǎi* — then)'s supplied English in ch 16's chain, and *where it ends* is 極 (*jí* — the utmost)'s exact phrase at ch 58.

**The cost.** The line sides with Heshang Gong over Wang Bi's explicit end, 終 (*zhōng* — end): a direction is a heading, not an arrival, and Wang Bi's beginning-and-end pairing of 妙 and 徼 is implied rather than said. Both commentators gloss with 歸 (*guī* — returning), so this is a lean, not a split. *Direction* can also mean guidance (*under the direction of*); in a line about seeing, it reads as heading. **The Literal column for lines 5–9 was changed with these notes**; it had glossed 妙 as *mysteries* and 玄 as *profound*, the pre-lock English this chapter was revised to refuse.

**此兩者，同出而異名，同謂之玄 → *"These two emerge together, but differ in name. / Together, they are called dark."*** *(Shalom's call, 2026-11-14.)* *(was: "These two have the same origin, but differ in name.")* **同 (*tóng*) stands twice in two lines, and both times before a verb:** 同**出** (*tóng chū* — "together emerge") and 同**謂** (*tóng wèi* — "together are called"). In that slot it is an adverb, and **one English has to survive both** (`notes/translation.md` → *同 finished*, the ch 56 rule). The old lines had *the same* in one and *Together* in the other. *The same* cannot be an adverb here: *emerge the same* means *turn out identical*, which is the flattening the line refuses, so every attempt to keep it had to turn 同 into a noun. ***Together* survives both slots**, and it keeps the two as two: it needs two, and never says they are one. Ch 23's *the same* (`WORKLIST-CLOSED.md` T2-25) was settled for 同於X (*tóng yú* — "the same as X"), a different slot, and stands.

- **出 (*chū* — to come out) is *emerge*,** as at ch 50's 出生入死 (*chū shēng rù sǐ*), *"Emerge into life, enter into death,"* and among its senses in the atlas. ***Origin* is retired.** It turned a plain verb into a noun nobody printed, and it blurred 出 with 始 (*shǐ* — beginning): line 3's *beginning*, and ch 52's only other *origin*, which renders 始. On Wang Bi's reading that collapses the chapter, since his two are 始與母 (*shǐ yǔ mǔ* — "the beginning and the mother"), and *same origin* has the beginning come out of a beginning.
- **The commentators.** Heshang Gong reads 同 as an adverb, *alike*: 同出人心 — *"come out alike from the human heart"*; 同受氣於天 — *"alike receive breath from the sky."* Wang Bi reads it as a noun, the shared source: 同出者，同出於玄也 — *"'come out the same' means coming out the same from the dark"*, and on line 9, 眾妙皆從同而出 — *"the many subtle things all come out from the same."* **This line follows Heshang Gong's grammar.** Wang Bi's source is implied, not said: what both call dark is what the two come out of (Wang Bi: 始母之所出也 — *"that from which the beginning and the mother come out"*; Heshang Gong: 玄，天也 — *"the dark is the sky"*).
- **Weighed the same day and declined:** *Their sameness is called dark* — it says the two are the same, where the line says they share a source and differ, as twins share a womb. *What is the same is called dark* and *These two emerge from the same* — Wang Bi's noun, exact, but elliptical, and a supplied *from*. *They both arise together* — *arise together* is ch 16's 並作 (*bìng zuò* — arise side by side), and *They both* breaks 此兩者 (*cǐ liǎng zhě* — these two), *these two* at chs 65 and 73. *Their togetherness* — the register of greeting cards, and it calls their bond dark where both commentators call the source dark.

**The cost.** *Together* against *differ* is a weaker opposition than *same* against *different*, the antonym pair the Chinese prints; *but* carries the contrast. *Together* can suggest the two emerge at one moment; both commentators' co-emergence allows it. Closes `WORKLIST.md` T2-27: its worry, that *together* reads as *jointly they form one thing*, is answered by line 7, where the two emerge together *and differ in name*.

**For the reader: 常, the one word four times.** 常 (*cháng* — ever) stands four times in this chapter: *the ever-present Tao*, *the ever-present name*, *ever desireless*, *ever desiring*. The oldest copy, the Mawangdui silk, writes all four with a still older word, 恒 (*héng* — ever), drawn in the earliest script as **a crescent moon between two lines**: the constancy of the moon, which changes shape every night and never once fails to come back. Not a thing outside time. Change that keeps its appointment.

The oldest reader, Han Fei (d. 233 BCE), says what the word is doing in line 1. What rises and falls, what flourishes first and fades after, cannot be called 常. Then the turn: 常者無攸易，無定理 — *"the ever-present has nothing it changes into, and **no fixed pattern**,"* 非在於常所 — *"it is in no fixed place,"* 是以不可道也 — *"and that is why it cannot be made a way."* **What is constant here is what is not fixed.** A way that has been made — a teaching, a method, a program to follow — has a fixed shape, and so it rises and falls like everything else. The Tao that is there every time has no fixed shape, and so it does not fade. That is the paradox of the first line.

And the chapter does not leave the word with the Tao. Lines 5 and 6 give it to the one who looks: *ever* desireless, *ever* desiring. Elsewhere the book uses these same words for the Tao's own condition: *"The Tao is ever nameless"* (ch 32), *"The Tao is ever non-doing"* (ch 37), and in ch 34, word for word, 常無欲 — *"Ever desireless, it can be named small."* So the instruction underneath the chapter is this: **to see what is ever-present, you become ever something yourself**, and what you become is what the Tao already is.
