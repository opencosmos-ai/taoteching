# Chapter 32

## Translation

The Tao is ever nameless, uncarved.  
Though small, nothing in the world can make it a subject.  
If rulers are able to hold to this, the countless things will submit of themselves.  
Sky and earth join together to send down sweet dew.  
No one commands the people, and they even out of themselves.

When the carving begins, there are names.  
Since names already exist, one must know when to stop.  
Knowing when to stop, you come to no danger.

The Tao in the world is like streams and valleys to the river and the sea.

## Notes

**知止可以不殆 — we follow 所以, and the English stands.** *(2026-10-14.)* Our base prints 可以 (*kě yǐ* — can), which would make the line *"knowing when to stop, you **can** come to no danger"*: an opening, not a guarantee. **The base is alone.** The Siku editors' note on this line in our vendored 王弼 (*Wáng Bì*) reads 〔案河上公注本及各本俱作所〕, *"Heshang Gong's edition and all editions read 所"*; 王弼's own comment closes 故知止**所以**不殆也; and Mawangdui A, where the characters survive, reads 所以不. 所以 (*suǒ yǐ*) is *that by which*: knowing when to stop **is how** you come to no danger. *"Knowing when to stop, you come to no danger"* already says that, and it now matches ch 44's 知止不殆, *"know when to stop, and you come to no danger,"* as the witnesses do. Same shape as ch 21's 然 / 狀. Logged in `sources/variants.yaml` and `notes/manuscript.md`.

**知止可以不殆 → "Knowing when to stop, you come to no danger."** *(2026-08-31, Pass C, `WORKLIST.md` T2-11.)* *(was: "one avoids depletion.")* 殆 (*dài*) is **peril**: 說文解字 危也, the graph 歺 (*è* — the bare-bone radical, as in 死 *sǐ* "to die") with 台 for sound. **Depletion is an energy metaphor**, this edition's named besetting temptation, and it renders no character at all. The same line stands at ch 44 and now reads alike; 殆's five occurrences (16, 25, 32, 44, 52) all read *danger*, and *danger* renders nothing else.

**河上公 (*Héshàng Gōng*) on the parallel line at ch 44** gives the sense plainly: 知可止則財利不累身…則身**不危殆**也 — *"knowing when one can stop, wealth and profit do not burden the body… then the body is not in peril."*

### 制 (*zhì*) → "carving", at ch 32 and ch 28

*(2026-09-07, Shalom's call. Was *cutting* in both.)*

**The change exists to make one thread visible in English.** This chapter opens 道常無名**樸** — *"the Tao is ever nameless, **uncarved**"* — and six lines later says 始**制**有名. Those two lines are the same argument arriving and departing: the uncut thing has no name; **the moment the blade goes in, names exist.** With 樸 as *uncarved* and 制 as *cutting*, an English reader had two different roots and no way to see that the second line undoes the first. **Uncarved / carving shows it.**

**説文 makes 制 a knife on timber, not a metaphor.** 裁也。从**刀**从**未**。未，物成有滋味，可裁斷 — *"to cut out. From 刀 (*dāo* — a knife) and 未 (*wèi*). 未 means things have matured and have flavour, so they can be cut through."* **未 is a tree with grown branches.** So the chain the chapter runs on is entirely woodwork: 樸 (uncarved timber) → 制 (the blade) → 器 (the vessel it becomes, ch 28's 樸散則為器) → 名 (the name that vessel now has).

**Why a cut produces a name.** A name needs a separate thing to attach to. While the timber is whole there is nothing to point at; cut it into a bowl and a spoon and there are two things, and two things need two names. **Naming is the receipt for a cut already made** — which is ch 1's 無名天地之始，有名萬物之母. `glossary/pu-樸.md` already reached this independently: *"Naming **is** carving… The book is a carving that argues against carving."*

**⚠ The loss, and it is a real one: 制 is a tailor's word as much as a carpenter's.** 說文's defining gloss is **裁** (*cái*) — to cut cloth to a pattern — and 制 also means to regulate and to govern, which is the sense 河上公 takes here (道無名，能**制**於有名, *"the nameless can govern the named"*). **"Carving" narrows a word that cuts cloth, timber and states down to timber alone.** That narrowing was accepted deliberately, because without it the line about names does not make sense in English: the reader cannot see what is being cut, and the chapter's central move goes silent. The wider sense is recorded here and in `glossary/pu-樸.md`.

*(A second English carries "carv-": 斫 (*zhuó* — to hew) at ch 74, 代大匠斫. The two characters never share a chapter, so no check will fire — but they now share a root, and 斫 is axe-work where 制 is a fitted cut. `WORKLIST.md` T2-50.)*

### 說文 hides the chapter's answer inside the character of its problem

制's entry closes **一曰止也** — *"one definition: **to stop**."* The very next line of this chapter is 知**止**, **know when to stop**. The chapter is not saying *do not carve* — there are no officials without titles and no bowl without a blade. It is saying that the word for the cut already contains the word for the halt.

**王弼 gives the social reading and names the danger:** 始制謂樸散始為官長之時也。始制官長，不可不立**名分**以定尊卑，故始制有名也。過此以往，將**爭錐刀之末** — *"始制 means the moment the uncarved scatters and first becomes officials and chiefs. Once you institute officials you cannot avoid setting up **name-and-role** to fix high from low… **Go on past this and they will fight over the tip of an awl.**"* That is 樸散則為器 at ch 28, in the same words.

**河上公 parses the line differently and it is worth keeping.** 始，道也。有名，萬物也。道無名，能制於有名；無形，能制於有形也 — *"始 is the Tao; 有名 is the countless things. The nameless Tao can govern the named, the formless can govern the formed."* He reads 始 as the **Tao** and 制 as **govern**, where 王弼 reads 始制 as *when cutting begins*. **We follow 王弼**, because his reading is what ties the line to 樸 three lines above and to ch 28; 河上公's is recorded, not adopted.

### The rest of the chapter

**譬道之在天下，猶川谷之於江海 → "The Tao in the world is like streams and valleys to the river and the sea."** *(Was: "In this world, with its high streams and valleys, the Tao is like the river and the deep sea.")* **高 and 深 are not in the line** — *high* and *deep* were both invented — and the 譬…猶 simile frame had been dissolved into a description. `WORKLIST.md` T1-14.

**王弼's gloss is the finest thing in the chapter, and it ties the ending to the middle:** 川谷之求江與海，**非江海召之**，不召不求而自歸者世。行道於天下者，**不令而自均**，不求而自得 — *"the streams and valleys seek the rivers and the sea; **it is not that the rivers and sea summon them**. Unsummoned and unseeking they return of themselves. One who practises the Tao in the world **does not command, and things even themselves**; does not seek, and gets of themselves."* **不令 and 自均 are quoted verbatim from 民莫之令而自均**, four lines earlier. *(His 召 — to summon — is also ch 73's word at 不召而自來, *"It does not summon, yet things come of themselves."*)*

**The English keeps the Chinese's ambiguity deliberately.** Grammatically 道之在天下 : 川谷之於江海 maps the Tao onto the **streams**; both commentators read it the other way, the Tao as the **sea** that things come to unbidden. *"Like streams and valleys to the river and the sea"* preserves the order and lets both stand.

**自均 → "even out of themselves," not "naturally harmonize."** **Harmonize is 和's word** (ch 2), where 均 (*jūn*) is to **level** — 河上公: 皆**自均調若一**也, *"all even themselves out, as if one."* And **自 occurs twice in three lines** (自賓, 自均) and had *of themselves* against *naturally*; 自然's lock is *of itself / of themselves*, so both now read alike. *"Without any external command"* had also invented the **external**.

**侯王若能守之 is verbatim at ch 32 and ch 37, and 能 had been dropped here.** Ch 37 reads *"If rulers are able to hold to this"*; this chapter read *"If rulers hold it."* Now matched.

**賓 (*bīn*) → "submit of themselves," and it is the chapter's weakest joint.** A **hapax** — one occurrence in the book — so nothing constrains it. 賓 is a **guest**, and 河上公 glosses it 賓服, *submit and follow*. *Submit* carries a compulsion this chapter spends its length denying, and *guest* is taken by 客 (ch 15, 35). **"Defer of themselves" is the live alternative** and is free.
