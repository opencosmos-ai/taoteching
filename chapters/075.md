# Chapter 75

## Translation

The people go hungry  
because those over them eat up so much in taxes.

The people are hard to govern  
because those over them will not stop doing.

The people make light of death  
because those over them want their own lives so rich.

Those who don't use living for doing  
are worthier than those who hold life precious.

## Notes

**有為 (*yǒu wéi* — "having doing") occurs exactly once in the whole book, and it is this line.** 無為 (*wúwéi* — non-doing) is named a dozen times as the thing to do; this is the only place Laozi names its opposite outright, and he names it as the cause of a state that cannot be governed. Chapter 75 is where the 無為 lock earns its keep: 為 (*wéi*) is a hand on an elephant — *handling* — so 上之有為 (*shàng zhī yǒu wéi*) is those above having their **doing**, a hand that will not come off.

**The word we cannot use here is "action."** The lock forbids "non-action" for 無為 precisely because it abstracts 為 into a category; importing "action" back in at the one line where the bracket 有為 … 無以生為 (*wú yǐ shēng wéi* — "make no doing of living") is visible would break it exactly where it shows. Nor will the smoother English do: *meddling*, *interference*, *officiousness* each smuggle in a judgment about the **quality** of the doing, and the chapter makes no such distinction. It is not that those over them do the wrong things. It is that they do.

**So 有 (*yǒu* — having) is read as persistence, not as a noun: "will not stop doing."** An earlier draft had the literal "have their doing" and defended the awkwardness as load-bearing. It was not — it read as a typo, and the strangeness was doing no work the plain line does not do better. Caught by Shalom, 2026-08-25. The live alternative, still on the table, is *"cannot keep their hands off"* — which is the character itself, since non-doing in this edition is defined as taking your hand off. It is the better line and it costs the word *doing*, so the echo two stanzas later goes silent.

**The 是以 refrain is dropped, and there is precedent for it four chapters back.** Our base closes each charge by repeating the word it opened on — 民之飢，以其上食稅之多，**是以飢** (*mín zhī jī … shì yǐ jī* — "the people's hunger … therefore hungry").

**The figure and its distribution.** The general shape X…是以X occurs in ch 38, 70, 71, 72 and 75. But the **full three-part form** — statement, then a causal clause in 以其 (*yǐ qí* — because their), then a verbatim restatement — occurs in only **two** chapters: ch 71's 聖人不病，以其病病，是以不病 (*shèng rén bù bìng, yǐ qí bìng bìng, shì yǐ bù bìng*), and ch 75, three times. *(A first pass here claimed the figure was unique to ch 75; the search that produced that required a 之 in the clause and missed ch 71 and the others. Corrected 2026-08-26.)*

**Shalom cut ch 71's on 2026-08-24 as editorial redundancy.** Ch 75 follows it, so this is consistency rather than a new call. The reason holds in both: 以 (*yǐ* — because) and 是以 (therefore) in one breath is incantation, and classical Chinese carries it as liturgy — but **English hears deliberate redundancy as stammering unless the register is already sermonic**, and sermonic is the register this edition exists to refuse. Rendered straight, *"The people go hungry / because those over them eat up so much in taxes. / That is why they go hungry"* reads as a translator who has lost their place.

**What carries it instead.** The figure is relocated, not lost. *"The people … / because those over them …"*, three times unvaried, is anaphora doing the same hammering one level up — at the stanza where it died at the line. *(Ch 72 shows a third strategy: 夫唯不厭，是以不厭 is **folded**, both halves surviving in one sentence, because 厭 carries two senses there.)*

**One culprit charged three times, and the charges climb.** Tax grain (appetite for goods), doing that will not stop (appetite for control), a life kept rich (appetite for life itself). The third is what the first two are made of, which is why the closing couplet answers with no policy at all.

**求生之厚 → "want their own lives so rich" — and it must stay about *life*, not about goods.** 厚 (*hòu*) is thickness: piling, heaping, richness. 河上公 glosses the line 求生活之道太厚，貪利以自危 — *"their way of seeking to live is too rich; they covet gain and so endanger themselves"* — luxury and appetite together, and for a Warring States ruler the longevity cult sits in it too. "Rich" holds both and closes neither.

**"The luxuries of life" was tried and rejected, and the reason is structural.** It moves the charge onto *stuff*, which makes the third charge a restatement of the first (the tax grain), collapses the three-step escalation to two, and — the real cost — **strands the closing couplet.** 貴生 (*guì shēng* — prizing life) is 求生之厚 restated one register up; the couplet only lands as an answer if the charge it answers was about **life**. "Their own" does the indictment's work: the people go hungry, and those over them want their own lives rich.

**輕死 → "make light of death", following 輕 (*qīng*) across the book.** 輕 is a light cart (車 *chē* — chariot). Ch 26 sets it against 重 (*zhòng* — heavy): 重為輕根, *"the heavy is the root of the ungrounded."* Ch 69 has 輕敵, already rendered *"making light of the enemy."* Keeping "make light of" here keeps 26, 69 and 75 audible as one word. And the line is a hinge: ch 74 opens 民不畏死, *"when the people do not fear death"* — 75 explains how they got that way, and the answer is the ruler's own appetite. See the 72 → 74 → 75 thread in `notes/reading.md`.

**是賢於貴生 → "are worthier than those who hold life precious" — the closing line judges a person, not a value.** 無以生為**者** (*zhě* — the one who) makes it a person, and 河上公's gloss is entirely about what kind of person results: 爵禄不干於意，財利不入於身 — *"rank and salary do not intrude on their intent, wealth and gain do not enter their person."* A draft read *"value life more than those who prize it"*, which converts a judgment of persons into a theory of value. Caught by Shalom, 2026-08-26.

**An earlier note in this file over-argued ch 3 and is corrected here.** It claimed that 不尚賢 (*bù shàng xián* — "do not exalt the worthy," ch 3) barred *worthy* as an English for 賢. Too strong: **the book uses 賢 approvingly in this very line.** Ch 3 forbids *exalting* worth, not worth itself, and refusing the word smooths a tension the text genuinely has. 賢 reads *worthier* here.

**But "nobler" fails, and not merely on register.** 貴 (*guì*) *is* noble — 貴族 (*guì zú*) is the aristocracy — and 貴 is the **losing** side of this sentence. Rendering 賢 as *nobler* puts 貴's own English on both sides of the comparison and the opposition vanishes. The two characters pull opposite ways: 貴 is worth by **standing**, while 賢, though it also carries 貝 (*bèi* — cowrie, shell money), is defined by *Shuowen* as 多才也 (*duō cái yě* — "having much ability") — worth by **capacity**. The chapter's last move prefers merit to rank, and "noble" is the one English word meaning both, so it cannot carry the preference. Second cost: this is a chapter indicting rulers for eating the people, and ending it on *nobler* returns the argument in the aristocracy's own vocabulary.

**貴 stays *precious*, as in ch 3, 64, 70 and 72** — and "hold life precious" lets ch 72's 自愛，不自貴 (*zì ài, bù zì guì* — "loves themselves and does not hold themselves precious") sound through, two chapters back inside the 72 → 74 → 75 thread.

**無以生為 → "don't use living for doing", which is the grammar and not a paraphrase.** 以 (*yǐ*) is genuinely *use / by means of*, so the line reads *"have nothing by which living is made a doing."* Shalom's phrasing, 2026-08-25, and it does what earlier drafts could not: **"doing" now sounds in the second stanza and again in the couplet**, so the bracket 有為 … 無以生為 is finally audible in English. 河上公 arrived at the same reading by inserting a character the text lacks — 無以生為**務**者 (*wù* — business, task), *"one who does not make living their business."*

**And 河上公 says what it buys.** 爵禄不干於意，財利不入於身，天子不得臣，諸侯不得使 — *"rank and salary do not intrude on their intent, wealth and gain do not enter their person, the Son of Heaven cannot make them a subject, the feudal lords cannot employ them."* The payoff of not making a project of your living is **that you cannot be used**. In a chapter about rulers eating people, that is the exit, and it is left implicit in our English exactly as it is in the base text.

**上 → "those over them", not "their superiors" and not "those above them".** 上 (*shàng*) is a stroke above a line, and the vertical is load-bearing here for one specific reason: **ch 66 pairs the same two characters** — 是以聖人處上而民不重 (*shì yǐ shèng rén chǔ shàng ér mín bù zhòng* — "so the sage dwells above and the people are not weighed down"). Ch 75 is that line's negative: the same 上 over the same 民 (*mín* — the people), and the people are starving. So a vertical word stays. But note the limit: 上/下 is **not** a fixed valuation in this book. Ch 39 has 高以下為基 (*gāo yǐ xià wéi jī* — "the high takes the low as its foundation"), and ch 76, the very next chapter, reads 柔弱處上 (*róu ruò chǔ shàng* — "the supple and weak occupy above"). In ch 8, 38, 41 and 71, 上 is simply *highest/best*, with no position in it at all.

**"Over" rather than "above" because ch 66 is measuring weight.** 不重 (*bù zhòng*) is *not weighed down*; "above" is neutral altitude, "over" is pressure on the back, and this chapter is about people being eaten. *"Their rulers"* was rejected on evidence: 王 (*wáng* — ruler) is locked and is **not in this chapter**, so it would be a rendering with no character behind it. *"Their leaders"* has a ch 17 precedent for 太上 (*tài shàng* — the highest) but drops the vertical and breaks the 66 link.

**民 throughout, no 人 anywhere.** Four occurrences of 民 (*mín* — the governed) and no 人 (*rén* — a person), and 民 is locked to *the people* (`glossary/min-民.md`), which is what all four already read.

**Textual note.** Our base reads 以其**上**求生之厚 in the third charge; both vendored commentaries' lemmas lack the 上, which moves the appetite from the rulers to the people. Logged in `notes/manuscript.md` and `sources/variants.yaml`. We keep the base — 王弼's own comment demands it.
